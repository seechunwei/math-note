
> [!definition]
> A first-order differential equation of the form 
> $$
> \frac{dy}{dx}=g(x)h(y)
> $$
> is said to be separable or to have separable variables.

When we get a first-order DE like this, it is very natural to separate it in to both side according to the variable. Thus, we divide both side by $h(y)$. 
$$
\begin{align}
p(y) \frac{dy}{dx}=g(x) && ,p(y)= \frac{1}{h(y)}
\end{align}
$$

Now let $y=\phi(x)$ represent a solution of $DE$. Thus, we must have $p(\phi(x)) \phi'(x)=g(x)$. Therefore, we integrate both side and get
$$
\int  p(\phi(x)) \phi'(x)\, dx= \int g(x) \, dx
$$

But $dy=\phi'(x)dx$ [[Linear Approximation and Differentials]] , thus by substitution

$$
\int p(y) \, dy= \int g(x) \, dx\text{ or }H(y)=G(x)+c
$$
where $H(y)$ and $G(x)$ are antiderivatives of $p(y)= \frac{1}{h(y)}$ and $g(x)$. respectively.

> [!remark]
> We need $g(x)$ and $p(y)$ to be continuous so that they have antiderivative. Why?
> [[12 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^1cbb3f]]. The first part of the FTC state that if a function $f(x)$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then there exist a antiderivative $g(x)=\int f(x) \, dx$ which is also continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$.
> 

No need to use 2 constant in the integration because eventually it will become single constant ($c_{3}=c_{1}-c_{2}$)

## Example 1
Solve $(1+x)dy-ydx=0$

Standard way:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dy}{y}&=\frac{dx}{(1+x)}   \\
\int \frac{1}{y} \, dx&=\int \frac{1}{1+x} \, d x&&(1)
\end{align}
$$
Notice that $(1)$ is not rigorous because $\int$ is not a algebraic operator an integral means accumulate a function over an interval with respect to a specific variable. It should be clarify like 

Rigorous way:
$$
\begin{align}
(1+x)dy&=ydx \\
\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{1}{1+x} \\
\int \frac{1}{y}\cdot y'(x) \, dx &= \int \frac{1}{1+x} \, dx&& (2)\\ 
\int  \frac{1}{y} \, dy&= \int \frac{1}{1+x}  \, dx  \\
\ln |y|&=\ln\lvert 1+x \rvert+c_{1}  \\
\lvert y \rvert &= e^{\ln \lvert 1+x \rvert+c_{1}}  \\
\lvert y \rvert&=c_{2}|1+x| &&c_{2}=ec_{1} \\
y&=\pm c_{2}(1+x) \\
&=c_{3}(1+x)
\end{align}
$$

$(2)$ is justified by the definition of differential $dy=y'(x)dx$

## Example 2
Solve the IVP $\frac{dy}{dx}= -\frac{x}{y}$, $y(4)=-3$.

$$
\begin{align}
y \frac{dy}{dx}&=-x \\
\int y\cdot y' \, dx&=  \int -x \, dx \\
\int y \, dy&=-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+c \\
\frac{y^{2}}{2}&=- \frac{x^{2}}{2}+c \\
y^{2}+x^{2}&=c_{1} &&c_{1}=2c
\end{align}
$$

Substitute $y(4)=-3$
$$
\begin{align}
9+16&=c_{1} \\
c_{1}&=25
\end{align}
$$
Thus, $G(x,y)=y^{2}+x^{2}-25$ is an implicit solution for the IVP. Thus, we take the function that satisfy the condition $(y<0)$: $y=\phi(x)= -\sqrt{ 25-x^{2} }, -5<x<5$

> [!remark]
> Notice that some time we will lose a solution. It is because in our step we divide $h(y)$ with include a implicit assumption that $h(y)\neq 0$. For example if we use separate method to solve an autonomous DE, then $h(y)=0$ which is the constant solution will not obtained at the end of step.
>
>Thus the initial value problem might have many solution.


## **Integral-Defined Function**
What is an integral-defined function is a function where the independent variable ($x$) appears as a limit of integration (usually the upper limit):

$$
y(x)=y_{0}+\int_{x_{0}}^{x} g(t) \, dt
$$
$x$(Upper limit): The actual variable of the function $y(x)$
$x_{0}$(Lower limit): The starting point from the initial condition
$t$(Dummy variable)

### Why Does this Formula solve the IVP?

Condition 1: Satisfy the DE
Let $\frac{dy}{dx}=g(x)$ and $y(x)$ be the solution.
Differentiate $y(x)$ using FTC1
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dy}{dx}&= \frac{d}{dx}\left( y_{0}+\int_{x_{0}}^{x} g(t) \, dx \right) \\
\end{align}
$$
Notice that since $y_{0}$ is a constant, $\frac{d}{dx}y_{0}=0$. By FTC2, $\int_{x_{0}}^{x} g(t) \, dt=G(x)-G(x_{0})$ where $G(t)$ is antiderivative of $g(t)$. 

Notice that $\frac{d}{dx}[G(x)]=g(x)$ and $\frac{d}{dx}[G(x_{0})]=0$, thus 
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[ \int_{x_{0}}^{x} g(t) \, dt \right]=g(x)
$$

Condition 2: It must satisfy $y(x_{0})=y_{0}$
Notice that
$$
\begin{align}
y(x_{0})&=y_{0}+\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}} g(t) \, dt \\
&=y_{0}+0 \\
&=y_{0}
\end{align}
$$

### Why we need this?
When a solution method include integration, it may lead to an integral-defined function. Suppose we have $\frac{dy}{dx}=g(x)$ and $y(x_{0})=y_{0}$. What if the antiderivative cannot be express in elementary functions? 

For example, $\frac{dy}{dx}=e^{-x^{2}}$ with $y(0)=y_{0}$. The integral $\int e^{-x^{2}}\,dx$ cannot be expressed in terms of elementary functions, yet the exact solution exists and is written directly as an integral-defined function:
$$
y(x) = y_{0} + \int_{0}^{x} e^{-t^{2}} \, dt
$$

### General Separable IVP Using Definite Integrals
For any separable initial-value problem:
$$
p(y)\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x), \quad y(x_{0}) = y_{0}
$$
we can integrate directly with matched limits:
$$
\int_{y_{0}}^{y} p(s) \, ds = \int_{x_{0}}^{x} g(t) \, dt
$$

#### Derivation of the Steps:

1. **Write the equation with a dummy variable $t$:**
   $$
   p(y(t)) \cdot y'(t) = g(t)
   $$

2. **Integrate both sides from $x_{0}$ to $x$:**
   $$
   \int_{x_{0}}^{x} p(y(t)) \cdot y'(t) \, dt = \int_{x_{0}}^{x} g(t) \, dt
   $$

3. **Apply substitution (change of variables) to the left side:**
   Let $s = y(t)$, so $ds = y'(t)\,dt$.
   - When $t = x_{0}$, the lower limit is $s = y(x_{0}) = y_{0}$ (from the initial condition).
   - When $t = x$, the upper limit is $s = y(x) = y$.

   Substituting $s$, $ds$, and the limits into the left side yields:
   $$
   \int_{x_{0}}^{x} p(y(t)) \cdot y'(t) \, dt = \int_{y_{0}}^{y} p(s) \, ds
   $$

4. **Combine both sides:**
   $$
   \int_{y_{0}}^{y} p(s) \, ds = \int_{x_{0}}^{x} g(t) \, dt
   $$

> [!tip] Advantage
> The initial condition $(x_{0}, y_{0})$ is automatically built into the lower limits of integration, completely eliminating the need to solve for an arbitrary constant $+c$.
