We usually solve a differential equation by recognizing it as a certain kind of equation (say, separable, linear, exact, and so on)

What if it does not fall into one of the classes we learn before? The substitution procedures that are discussed in this section may be helpful in this situation.

---

### The Three Classic Substitutions in Chapter 2.5

Think of **Substitution** not as a destination, but as a **bridge (or shape-shifter)**.  
A substitution takes a stubborn equation that does not fall into one of the standard solvable forms, and transforms it into one of them:

| Non-Solvable Form | The Obstacle | The Substitution | What it transforms INTO |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Homogeneous**<br>$(x^2 + y^2)dx + (x^2 - xy)dy = 0$ | Not separable, not linear, not exact. | **$y = ux$** (or $x = vy$) | **Separable** in $x$ and $u$! |
| **Bernoulli**<br>$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = f(x)\mathbf{y^n}$ | Not linear because of the nonlinear $y^n$. | **$u = y^{1-n}$** | **Linear** in $u$ and $x$! |
| **Linear Combination**<br>$\frac{dy}{dx} = (x + y)^2$ | $x$ and $y$ are tangled inside a linear expression $(Ax+By+C)$. | **$u = Ax + By + C$** | **Separable** in $u$ and $x$! |

---

### Summary: First-Order ODE Methods & Decision Strategy

| Method           | When it applies                                                                                   | Limitation                                                                             |
| :--------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Separable**    | When $x$ and $y$ can be factored apart into $f(x)g(y)$.                                           | Fails on cross-terms like $x^2 + y^2$ or $2y^2 + 3x$.                                  |
| **Linear**       | When $y$ and $\frac{dy}{dx}$ appear strictly to the first power ($y^1$).                          | Fails completely if you have $y^2, \frac{1}{y}, \sin y, e^y$.                          |
| **Exact**        | When $M\,dx + N\,dy$ represents the total differential $df$ of a surface $f(x, y)$ ($M_y = N_x$). | Can solve **highly nonlinear, non-separable** equations directly without substitution. |
| **Substitution** | When the equation is neither separable, linear, nor exact, but fits a structural pattern.         | Acts as a **bridge** to transform the equation into a Separable or Linear ODE.         |

#### The Grand Strategy: How to Approach ANY First-Order ODE

```text
1. Can I separate x and y directly? (Separable)
      │ No
2. Is it linear in y? (dy/dx + P(x)y = Q(x)) (Linear)
      │ No
3. Does My = Nx? (Exact)
      │ No
4. Can an integrating factor μ(x) or μ(y) make it exact?
      │ No
5. Does it fit a Substitution pattern?
      ├── All terms same degree? ───> Homogeneous: y = ux (becomes Separable)
      ├── Has a y^n term?        ───> Bernoulli:   u = y^(1-n) (becomes Linear)
      └── Has (Ax + By + C)?     ───> Linear Arg:  u = Ax + By + C (becomes Separable)
```

Let say we have a DE $\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y)$, and we let $y=g(x,u)$. So now it become $f(x,g(x,u))$.

It means that now the variable $y$ is being replace by another relation depend on variable $x$ and $u$. (The whole DE only in term of $x$ and $u$)

If $g$ possesses first-partial derivatives, then the Chain Rule

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}
$$

### Why is $u$ a function of $x$?
In the original differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$, $y$ is an unknown function of $x$ (that is, $y = y(x)$—which is why $\frac{dy}{dx}$ exists).

When we introduce a helper variable $u$ related to $x$ and $y$ (such as $y = g(x, u)$ or $u = x + y$), as $x$ varies, $y(x)$ varies, which forces $u$ to vary with $x$ as well.
Therefore, $u$ is regarded as a function of $x$: $u = u(x)$, and has its own derivative $\frac{du}{dx}$.

---

### The Total Rate of Change (Multivariable Chain Rule)

Let $y=g(x,u)$.

From multivariable calculus, the total rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$ is:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}}_{\text{direct rate}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}}_{\text{indirect rate through } u}
$$

#### 1. The "Two Paths" Mental Picture
Because $u$ depends on $x$, when $x$ changes, it affects $y$ along **two simultaneous pathways**:

```text
       Path 1 (Direct)
    x ------------------> y
     \                   /
      \                 /
       -> u ----------->
       Path 2 (Indirect)
```

1. **Path 1 (Direct):** $x$ changes $y$ directly (treating $u$ as constant).
   * The rate of change along this path is the partial derivative:
     $$
     \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}
     $$
2. **Path 2 (Indirect):** $x$ changes $u$, which in turn changes $y$.
   * First, $x$ changes $u$ at the rate $\frac{du}{dx}$.
   * Then, that change in $u$ changes $y$ at the rate $\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}$.
   * Along this path, the rate is:
     $$
     \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}
     $$

Adding both pathways gives the total derivative:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}
$$

#### 2. The "Tiny Nudge" Explanation ($\Delta x$)
If you nudge $x$ by a tiny amount $\Delta x$:
1. $x$ changes by $\Delta x$.
2. This forces $u$ to change by a tiny amount $\Delta u \approx \left(\frac{du}{dx}\right)\Delta x$.

By linear approximation, the total change $\Delta y$ is:
$$
\Delta y \approx \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\right)\Delta x + \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\right)\Delta u
$$

Dividing both sides by $\Delta x$:
$$
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \approx \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}
$$

Taking the limit as $\Delta x \to 0$ (since $\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta x} = 1$):
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\frac{du}{dx}
$$

#### 3. Familiar Check: The Product Rule is just this formula!
Suppose $y = x \cdot u$. Here, $g(x, u) = x \cdot u$:
* $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = u$
* $\frac{\partial g}{\partial u} = x$

Plugging into the formula:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = u + x \frac{du}{dx}
$$
This is literally the ordinary **Product Rule**!

### Transforming the Differential Equation

Where does the equation $g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx} = f(x, g(x, u))$ come from?

Notice that we have **two separate facts** that both describe $\frac{dy}{dx}$:

1. **Fact 1 (The original Differential Equation given to us):**
   $$
   \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)
   $$
2. **Fact 2 (From differentiating our substitution $y = g(x, u)$):**
   $$
   \frac{dy}{dx} = g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx}
   $$

#### Setting Them Equal
Because **both** expressions are equal to $\frac{dy}{dx}$, we set them equal to each other:
$$
\underbrace{g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx}}_{\text{Fact 2}} = \underbrace{f(x, y)}_{\text{Fact 1}}
$$

Now, to eliminate the remaining $y$ on the right side, replace $y$ with $g(x, u)$:
$$
g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx} = f(x, g(x, u))
$$


---

### Solving for $\frac{du}{dx}$

Now we isolate $\frac{du}{dx}$ using basic algebra:
$$
g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx} = f(x, g(x, u)) - g_x(x, u)
$$
$$
\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{f(x, g(x, u)) - g_x(x, u)}{g_u(x, u)}
$$

Because the entire right-hand side now depends only on $x$ and $u$ (every $y$ has been eliminated), we define it as $F(x, u)$:
$$
\frac{du}{dx} = F(x, u)
$$

If we solve this new DE for $u = \phi(x)$, then the solution to the original equation is:
$$
y = g(x, \phi(x))
$$

---

> [!remark] Daily Practice vs. Theoretical Proof
> * **In Daily Practice (Differentiate with respect to $x$ directly):**  
>   When solving actual problems (such as $u = x + y$, $u = \frac{y}{x}$, or $u = y^{1-n}$), you **do not** need to write out the abstract partial derivatives $g_x$ and $g_u$. Simply differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ directly:
>   $$
>   u = x + y \implies \frac{du}{dx} = 1 + \frac{dy}{dx} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{du}{dx} - 1
>   $$
>   This is 100% mathematically rigorous (justified by the Sum Rule of single-variable calculus) and avoids algebraic mistakes.
> 
> * **Why the Formula in the Proof Looks Complicated:**  
>   A textbook proof must work for **any arbitrary substitution** $y = g(x, u)$ (whether linear, fractional, trigonometric, or nonlinear). Because the author cannot know in advance which function you will pick, they must use the general multivariable chain rule $\frac{dy}{dx} = g_x + g_u \frac{du}{dx}$ to prove that a transformed first-order ODE $\frac{du}{dx} = F(x, u)$ always exists. For any specific problem, that general formula simply reduces to ordinary single-variable differentiation!

### Concrete Example

Consider the differential equation:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = (x + y)^2
$$

1. **Choose substitution:** Let $u = x + y \implies y = u - x$, so $g(x, u) = u - x$.
2. **Differentiate:** 
   $$
   \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{du}{dx} - 1
   $$
3. **Substitute into the DE:**
   $$
   \frac{du}{dx} - 1 = u^2 \implies \frac{du}{dx} = u^2 + 1
   $$
4. **Solve for $u$:** This is separable!
   $$
   \frac{du}{u^2 + 1} = dx \implies \arctan(u) = x + c \implies u = \tan(x + c)
   $$
5. **Back-substitute to find $y$:**
   $$
   y = u - x = \tan(x + c) - x
   $$

## Homogeneous Equations

A function $f(x, y)$ is called **homogeneous of degree $\alpha$** if:
$$
f(tx, ty) = t^\alpha f(x, y)
$$
for some real number $\alpha$.

It means that when we scale the variables by $t$, it will scale the function value by $t^\alpha$ with degree $\alpha$.

### Examples

1. **Homogeneous function:**
   $$
   f(x, y) = x^3 + y^3
   $$
   Testing with $tx$ and $ty$:
   $$
   \begin{align}
   f(tx, ty) &= (tx)^3 + (ty)^3 \\
   &= t^3(x^3 + y^3) \\
   &= t^3 f(x, y)
   \end{align}
   $$
   Thus, $f(x, y)$ is homogeneous of degree $\alpha = 3$.

2. **Non-homogeneous function:**
   $$
   f(x, y) = x^2 y^3 + 1
   $$
   Testing with $tx$ and $ty$:
   $$
   f(tx, ty) = (tx)^2(ty)^3 + 1 = t^5 x^2 y^3 + 1 \neq t^\alpha f(x, y)
   $$
   Whereas $f(x, y) = x^2 y^3 + 1$ is **not homogeneous**.

---

### Homogeneous Differential Equation

A first-order differential equation in differential form:
$$
M(x, y) \, dx + N(x, y) \, dy = 0
$$
is said to be **homogeneous** if both coefficients $M(x, y)$ and $N(x, y)$ are homogeneous of the **same degree** $\alpha$:
$$
M(tx, ty) = t^\alpha M(x, y) \quad \text{and} \quad N(tx, ty) = t^\alpha N(x, y)
$$

or equivalently:
$$
M(x, y) = x^\alpha M(1, u) \quad \text{and} \quad N(x, y) = x^\alpha N(1, u)
$$
where $u = \frac{y}{x}$.

#### Derivation (Why setting $t = \frac{1}{x}$ works):
Since $M(tx, ty) = t^\alpha M(x, y)$ holds for any scalar $t > 0$, choose $t = \frac{1}{x}$:
$$
M\left(\frac{1}{x} \cdot x, \; \frac{1}{x} \cdot y\right) = \left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^\alpha M(x, y)
$$
$$
M\left(1, \; \frac{y}{x}\right) = \frac{1}{x^\alpha} M(x, y)
$$
Multiplying both sides by $x^\alpha$ and letting $u = \frac{y}{x}$:
$$
M(x, y) = x^\alpha M(1, u)
$$
Similarly, $N(x, y) = x^\alpha N(1, u)$. (Likewise, choosing $t = \frac{1}{y}$ and $v = \frac{x}{y}$ gives $M(x, y) = y^\alpha M(v, 1)$ and $N(x, y) = y^\alpha N(v, 1)$).

---

### Method of Solution: Why Substitution Makes it Separable

Substitute $M(x, y) = x^\alpha M(1, u)$ and $N(x, y) = x^\alpha N(1, u)$ into the differential equation:
$$
x^\alpha M(1, u) \, dx + x^\alpha N(1, u) \, dy = 0
$$

1. **Divide by $x^\alpha$:**
   $$
   M(1, u) \, dx + N(1, u) \, dy = 0
   $$

2. **Substitute $y = ux \implies dy = u \, dx + x \, du$:**
   $$
   M(1, u) \, dx + N(1, u)(u \, dx + x \, du) = 0
   $$

3. **Group the $dx$ and $du$ terms:**
   $$
   \big[M(1, u) + u N(1, u)\big] \, dx + x N(1, u) \, du = 0
   $$

4. **Separate the variables:**
   $$
   \frac{dx}{x} + \frac{N(1, u)}{M(1, u) + u N(1, u)} \, du = 0
   $$

> [!tip] Choice of Substitution: Why pick $y = ux$ vs $x = vy$?
> Both substitutions work, but picking the right one saves algebraic pain:
> * **If $N(x, y)$ is simpler than $M(x, y)$:** Pick **$y = ux$** (with $dy = u \, dx + x \, du$).  
>   Here, $dy$ expands into two terms and gets multiplied by $N(x, y)$. 
> * **If $M(x, y)$ is simpler than $N(x, y)$:** Pick **$x = vy$** (with $dx = v \, dy + y \, dv$).  
>   Here, $dx$ expands into two terms and gets multiplied by $M(x, y)$. 

> [!note] Deep Intuition: How Did Anyone Think of $u = \frac{y}{x}$? (Scale-Invariance & Degree 0)
> It often feels like black magic when textbooks introduce the substitution $u = \frac{y}{x}$. Here is the geometric and physical intuition behind why it was discovered and why it always forces separation:
> 
> 1. **Degree of $M, N$ vs. Degree of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ (The Cancellation):**
>    - While $M(x, y)$ and $N(x, y)$ each scale by $t^\alpha$ (homogeneous of degree $\alpha$), the derivative is their ratio:
>      $$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{M(x, y)}{N(x, y)}$$
>    - Scaling both variables by $t$ gives:
>      $$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{M(tx, ty)}{N(tx, ty)} = -\frac{t^\alpha M(x, y)}{t^\alpha N(x, y)} = \frac{t^\alpha}{t^\alpha}\left(-\frac{M(x, y)}{N(x, y)}\right) = t^0 \cdot \left(-\frac{M(x, y)}{N(x, y)}\right)$$
>    - The scaling factors $t^\alpha$ in numerator and denominator **cancel out completely** ($n - n = 0$). Thus, the slope $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is homogeneous of **degree 0**—it is strictly **scale-invariant**.
> 
> 2. **Geometric Meaning (Rays from the Origin):**
>    - Scale-invariance means that along any straight ray $y = mx$ emanating from the origin, the ratio $\frac{y}{x} = m$ is constant.
>    - Therefore, the slope of the solution curve $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is **identical** at every point along that ray (whether at $(1, 2)$, $(10, 20)$, or $(1000, 2000)$).
>    - The differential equation does not care about distance from the origin ($r$); it **only cares about direction/angle** (the ratio $\frac{y}{x}$).
> 
> 3. **The Deepest Reason to Divide by $x^\alpha$ (Euler's Trick: $t = \frac{1}{x}$):**
>    - Remember the scaling identity for degree $0$: $f(tx, ty) = f(x, y)$ must hold for **any** scaling factor $t > 0$.
>    - What if we make the clever choice **$t = \frac{1}{x}$**?
>      $$f(x, y) = f\left(\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)x, \; \left(\frac{1}{x}\right)y\right) = f\left(1, \; \frac{y}{x}\right)$$
>    - Choosing $t = \frac{1}{x}$ automatically scales the $x$-coordinate to $1$, collapsing the entire expression into a function of the single bundle $\frac{y}{x}$!
>    - Dividing numerator and denominator by $x^2$ (or $x^\alpha$) is simply carrying out this exact choice:
>      $$\frac{x^2 + y^2}{2xy} \xrightarrow{\div x^2} \frac{\frac{x^2}{x^2} + \frac{y^2}{x^2}}{\frac{2xy}{x^2}} = \frac{1 + \left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^2}{2\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)}$$
>    - No loose $x$'s or $y$'s remain—every variable is now trapped inside the bundle $u = \frac{y}{x}$.
> 
> 4. **Why Separation is Guaranteed (The Mechanics):**
>    - Once written as $\frac{dy}{dx} = F(u)$, substituting $y = ux$ gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = u + x\frac{du}{dx}$ via the product rule.
>    - Equating them:
>      $$u + x\frac{du}{dx} = F(u) \implies x\frac{du}{dx} = F(u) - u$$
>    - Separating variables gives:
>      $$\frac{1}{F(u) - u}\,du = \frac{1}{x}\,dx$$
>    - Because the left side depends purely on $u$ and the right side purely on $x$, the equation is **guaranteed to separate every single time**.

### Derivation: How to get $dy = u \, dx + x \, du$

Given the relation $y = u \cdot x$, where $u$ is a function of $x$:

#### Method 1: Product Rule (Single-Variable Differentials)
Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ using the standard **Product Rule**:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}[u \cdot x] = u \frac{dx}{dx} + x \frac{du}{dx} = u + x \frac{du}{dx}
$$

Multiplying both sides by the differential $dx$:
$$
dy = \left(u + x \frac{du}{dx}\right)dx = u \, dx + x \, du
$$
*(Or equivalently, using the product rule for differentials: $d(ux) = u \, dx + x \, du$.)*

---

#### Method 2: Partial Derivative Method (Total Differential)
View $y$ as a multivariable function $g(x, u) = u \cdot x$.  
From multivariable calculus, the **total differential** $dy$ is defined as:
$$
dy = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} \, dx + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \, du
$$

Compute each first partial derivative:
1. **With respect to $x$** (holding $u$ constant):
   $$
   \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}[u \cdot x] = u
   $$
2. **With respect to $u$** (holding $x$ constant):
   $$
   \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial}{\partial u}[u \cdot x] = x
   $$

Substituting these back into the total differential formula:
$$
dy = u \, dx + x \, du
$$

*(Similarly, for $x = v \cdot y$, the total differential gives $dx = \frac{\partial (vy)}{\partial y} \, dy + \frac{\partial (vy)}{\partial v} \, dv = v \, dy + y \, dv$.)*

## Example: Solving a Homogeneous Equation

Solve the differential equation:
$$
(x^2 + y^2) \, dx + (x^2 - xy) \, dy = 0
$$

### Step 1: Check homogeneity
Observe that:
$$
M(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 \quad \text{and} \quad N(x, y) = x^2 - xy
$$
Both are homogeneous functions of degree $2$.

---

### Step 2: Choose the substitution
Let:
$$
y = ux \implies dy = u \, dx + x \, du
$$
*(We start directly from here because from the derivation earlier, we know that since both coefficients are homogeneous of degree 2, the factor $x^2$ will cancel out completely).*

---

### Step 3: Substitute into the DE
$$
(x^2 + u^2 x^2) \, dx + (x^2 - u x^2)[u \, dx + x \, du] = 0
$$

Factor out $x^2$:
$$
x^2(1 + u^2) \, dx + x^2(1 - u)[u \, dx + x \, du] = 0
$$

Distribute and expand:
$$
x^2(1 + u^2) \, dx + x^3(1 - u) \, du + x^2(u - u^2) \, dx = 0
$$

Combine the $dx$ terms:
$$
x^2\big[(1 + u^2) + (u - u^2)\big] \, dx + x^3(1 - u) \, du = 0
$$
$$
x^2(1 + u) \, dx + x^3(1 - u) \, du = 0
$$

---

### Step 4: Separate variables
Divide both sides by $x^3(1 + u)$:
$$
\frac{dx}{x} + \frac{1 - u}{1 + u} \, du = 0
$$
$$
\frac{1}{x} \, dx = \frac{u - 1}{u + 1} \, du
$$

Rewrite the rational expression using algebraic division:
$$
\frac{u - 1}{u + 1} = \frac{(u + 1) - 2}{u + 1} = 1 - \frac{2}{u + 1}
$$

Thus:
$$
\int \frac{1}{x} \, dx = \int \left(1 - \frac{2}{u + 1}\right) \, du
$$

---

### Step 5: Integrate and back-substitute
Integrating both sides:
$$
\ln |x| = u - 2\ln |u + 1| + c
$$

Back-substitute $u = \frac{y}{x}$:
$$
\ln |x| = \frac{y}{x} - 2\ln \left|\frac{y}{x} + 1\right| + c
$$

Using logarithm properties to simplify:
$$
\ln |x| + 2\ln \left|\frac{x + y}{x}\right| = \frac{y}{x} + c
$$
$$
\ln |x| + \ln \frac{(x + y)^2}{x^2} = \frac{y}{x} + c
$$
$$
\ln \left|\frac{(x + y)^2}{x}\right| = \frac{y}{x} + c \implies (x + y)^2 = c_1 x e^{y/x}
$$

## Solving a Bernoulli DE

The differential equation:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = f(x)y^n \quad \text{--- (4)}
$$
where $n$ is any real number, is called **Bernoulli's equation**. Note that for $n = 0$ and $n = 1$, the equation is already linear.

For $n \neq 0$ and $n \neq 1$, the substitution $u = y^{1-n}$ reduces any equation of form (4) to a **linear equation**.

---

### Example

Solve:
$$
x \frac{dy}{dx} + y = x^2 y^2
$$

#### Step 1: Write in Standard Form
Before substituting, divide by $x$ so the coefficient of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is 1 (standard Bernoulli form):
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{1}{x}y = x y^2
$$
Here, $P(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, $f(x) = x$, and $n = 2$.

> [!warning] Common Pitfall
> If you substitute directly into $x\frac{dy}{dx} + y = x^2y^2$ without dividing by $x$ first, make sure not to drop the leading factor of $x$:
> $$
> x\left(-u^{-2}\frac{du}{dx}\right) + u^{-1} = x^2 u^{-2} \implies x\frac{du}{dx} - u = -x^2 \implies \frac{du}{dx} - \frac{1}{x}u = -x
> $$
> Putting the equation into standard form first prevents dropping that $x$!

---

#### Step 2: Apply the Substitution
Since $n = 2$, let:
$$
u = y^{1-2} = y^{-1} \implies y = u^{-1}
$$

By the Chain Rule:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} = -u^{-2} \frac{du}{dx}
$$

Substitute $\frac{dy}{dx} = -u^{-2}\frac{du}{dx}$ and $y = u^{-1}$ into the standard form:
$$
-u^{-2} \frac{du}{dx} + \frac{1}{x} u^{-1} = x (u^{-2})
$$

Multiply the entire equation by $-u^2$:
$$
\frac{du}{dx} - \frac{1}{x}u = -x
$$
This is now a standard **first-order linear differential equation** in $u$!

---

#### Step 3: Solve the Linear ODE with an Integrating Factor
The integrating factor is:
$$
\mu(x) = e^{\int -\frac{1}{x} \, dx} = e^{-\ln |x|} = \frac{1}{|x|} \implies \mu(x) = \frac{1}{x}
$$

Multiply the linear DE by $\mu(x) = \frac{1}{x}$:
$$
\frac{1}{x}\frac{du}{dx} - \frac{1}{x^2}u = -1
$$
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[ u \cdot \frac{1}{x} \right] = -1
$$

Integrate both sides with respect to $x$:
$$
u \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \int -1 \, dx = -x + C
$$

Multiply by $x$:
$$
u = Cx - x^2
$$

---

#### Step 4: Back-substitute to find $y$
Since $u = y^{-1} = \frac{1}{y}$:
$$
\frac{1}{y} = Cx - x^2 \implies y = \frac{1}{Cx - x^2}
$$

> [!remark]
> Remark
> Notice that $y=0$ is the singular solution that missed

## Reduction To Separation of Variables
