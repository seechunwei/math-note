
> [!definition]
> An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if $A$ is similar to a diagonal matrix.

In other word an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if there exist diagonal matrix $D$ and invertible matrix $P$ such that $A=PDP^{-1}$.

Since $P^{-1}AP=D$, we say that $P$ **diagonalizes** $A$, and $P$ is also called the **diagonalizing matrix**.

> [!NOTE] The Diagonalization Theorem
> Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix. Then
> 
> a) the matrix $A$ is diagonalizable iff $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors
> b) $A=PDP^{-1}$, where $D$ is a diagonal matrix, iff the columns of $P$ are $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors of $A$.
> 
> In this case, the diagonal entries of $D$ are eigenvalues of $A$ that correspond, respectively, to the eigenvectors in $P$.

In other words, $A$ is diagonalizable iff there are enough eigenvectors to form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. We call such a basis an **eigenvector basis** of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

Proof a) b) :
Notice that the supposition of a) and b) are actually talking the same thing and the implication of b) is the hint to implication of a).

First, observe that if $P$ is any $n\times n$ matrix with columns $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$ and if $D$ is any diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}$ , then

$$
\begin{align}
AP=A\begin{bmatrix}
v_{1} & v_{2} & \dots & v_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
Av_{1} & Av_{2} & \dots & Av_{n}
\end{bmatrix} &&(1)
\end{align}
$$

while

$$
\begin{align}
PD=P\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_{1} & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_{2} & \dots & 0 \\
\vdots  & \vdots  &  & \vdots  \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \lambda_{n}
\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_{1}v_{1} & \lambda_{2}v_{2} & \dots & \lambda_{n}v_{n}
\end{bmatrix} &&(2)
\end{align}
$$

> [!remark]
> (2) (by definition of multiplication)
> The first columns of $PD$:
> 
> $$
> \lambda_{1}v_{1}+0v_{2}+\dots+0v_{n}
> $$
> It is the same for the rest
> 
> Notice that the step above here is the set up for this proof, the next step we will start to use our supposition.

Suppose $A$ is diagonalizable and $A=PDP^{-1}$. Then $AP=PD$. $(1)$ and $(2)$ implies that

$$
\begin{align}
\begin{bmatrix}
Av_{1} & Av_{2} & \dots & Av_{n}
\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_{1}v_{1} & \lambda_{2}v_{2} & \dots & \lambda_{n}v_{n}
\end{bmatrix} &&(3)
\end{align}
$$

Equating the columns, we find that

$$
\begin{align}
Av_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}&&(4)
\end{align}
$$
for $1\leq i\leq n$.

Since $P$ is invertible, its columns $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$ must be linearly independent Also, since these columns are nonzero, the equation (4) show that $\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}$ are eigenvalues and $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$ are corresponding eigenvector 

This argument prove the forward direction of a) and b).

Conversely, suppose $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvector $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$, and let $P=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1} & \dots & v_{n}\end{bmatrix}$ and use the corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_{1},\dots\lambda_{n}$ to construct $D$. By equation (1)-(3) $AP=PD$. Since the columns of $P$ is linearly independent it follow that $P$ is invertible, thus $AP=PD\implies A=PDP^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

## Diagonalizing Matrices

Example: Diagonalize the following matrix, if possible

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 3 & 3 \\
-3 & -5 & -3 \\
3 & 3 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$
That is, find an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that $A=PDP^{-1}$.

Step 1: Find the eigenvalues of $A$.
By definition $\lambda$ is an eigenvalues of $A$ iff
$$
(A-\lambda I)x=0
$$
has nontrivial solution. Or by characteristic equation

$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=0
$$

$$
\begin{align}
 \begin{bmatrix}
1-\lambda & 3 & 3 \\
-3 & -5-\lambda & -3 \\
3 & 3 & 1-\lambda
\end{bmatrix}&\xrightarrow[]{R_{2}^{1}} \begin{bmatrix}
-3 & -5-\lambda & -3 \\
1-\lambda & 3 & 3 \\
3 & 3 & 1-\lambda
\end{bmatrix} \\
&\xrightarrow[]{R_{3}^{1}(1)} \begin{bmatrix}
-3 & -5-\lambda & -3 \\
1-\lambda & 3 & 3 \\
0 & -2-\lambda & -2-\lambda
\end{bmatrix} \\
&\xrightarrow[]{R_{1}\left( -\frac{1}{3} \right)}\begin{bmatrix}
1 & \frac{5+\lambda}{3} & 1 \\
1-\lambda & 3 & 3 \\
0 & -2-\lambda & -2-\lambda
\end{bmatrix} \\
&\xrightarrow[]{R_{2}^{1}(\lambda-1)} \begin{bmatrix}
1 & \frac{5+\lambda}{3} & 1 \\
0 & \frac{(5+\lambda)(\lambda-1)+9}{3} & \lambda+2 \\
0 & -2-\lambda & -2-\lambda
\end{bmatrix}&&(1)
\end{align}
$$

Since we perform $R_{3}^{1}(1)$ and $R_{1}\left( -\frac{1}{3} \right)$, it follow that $\det(A-\lambda I)=(-1)(3)\det B$ where $B$ is the matrix in (1). Thus,

Cofactor expansion across first column;

$$
\begin{align}
\det(A-\lambda I)&=3\cdot(-1)^{2}(1)\det \begin{bmatrix}
 \frac{(5+\lambda)(\lambda-1)+9}{3} & \lambda+2 \\
-2-\lambda & -2-\lambda
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=3\det \begin{bmatrix}
 \frac{(\lambda+2)^{2}}{3} & \lambda+2 \\
-(\lambda+2) & -(\lambda+2) \\
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=3(\lambda+2)^{2}\det \begin{bmatrix} 
\frac{\lambda+2}{3} & 1 \\
-1 & -1
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=3(\lambda+2)^{2}\left( -\frac{\lambda+2}{3}+1 \right) \\
&=(\lambda+2)^{2}(-\lambda-2+3) \\
&=(\lambda+2)^{2}(-\lambda+1) \\
&=-(\lambda-1)(\lambda+2)^{2}
\end{align}
$$

The eigenvalue is $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=-2$

Step 2:
Find 3 linearly independent eigenvectors of $A$. Of we couldn't find , then $A$ is not diagonalizable.

Basis for $\lambda=1:$ $v_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

Basis for $\lambda=-2:$ $v_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ and $v_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

We can obtain these vector by solving $(A-\lambda I)x=0$.

Step 3: Construct $P$ from the vectors in step 2.

$$
P=\begin{bmatrix}
v_{1} & v_{2} & v_{3}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & -1 \\
-1 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Step 4: Construct $D$ from the corresponding eigenvalues.

Notice it is essential that the order of the eigenvalues match the order of eigenvector in $P$.

$$
D=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -2 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & -2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

> [!theorem] Theorem 6
> An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues.

Proof:
Let $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$ be eigenvector corresponding to the $n$ distinct eigenvalues of a matrix $A$. Then $\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{n} \}$ is linearly independent, by Theorem 2. Hence, $A$ is diagonalizable by theorem 5. $\blacksquare$

## Matrices Whose Eigenvalues Are Not Distinct

> [!theorem] Theorem 7
> Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix whose distinct eigenvalues are $\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p}$
> 
> a) For $1\leq k\leq p$, the dimension of the eigenspace for $\lambda_{k}$ is less than or equal to the multiplicity of the eigenvalue $\lambda_{k}$.
> 
> b) The matrix $A$ is diagonalizable iff the sum of the dimensions of the eigenspaces (geometric multiplicity) equals $n$, and this happens iff 
> i) the characteristic polynomial factors completely into linear factors or
> ii) the dimension of the eigenspace for each $\lambda_{k}$ equals the multiplicity of $\lambda_{k}$.
> 
> This 2 cases implies that $A$ is diagonalizable iff geometric multiplicity equal to algebraic multiplicity for each eigenvalues
> 
> c)If $A$ is diagonalizable and $B_{k}$ is a basis for the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda_{k}$ for each $k$, then the collection of vectors in the sets $B_{1},\dots,B_{p}$ form an eigenvector basis for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.
