Any vector space with the inner product is called an inner product space.

Ay linear combination of vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a vector space. In that vector space we can define an function that assign each pair of vector a real number $<u,v>$ . This means that $F:u,v\mapsto<u,v>$ is a inner product function.

1) Assign every pair of vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ a unique real number in $\mathbb{R}$.


How to know the function defined is a inner product? We need to check the axiom which is actually the property of inner product
1) $\langle u, u \rangle=\langle v,u \rangle$
2) $\langle u+v,w \rangle=\langle u,w \rangle+\langle v,w \rangle$
3) $\langle cu,v \rangle=c\langle u,v \rangle=\langle u,cv \rangle$
4) $\langle u,u \rangle\geq 0$ and $\langle u,u \rangle=0 \text{ iff }u=0$

Notice that axiom 2 and 3 is like linearity of determinant. It is called multilinearity.

Both inner product and the determinant are functions that take multiple inputs at the same time.

An inner product has two slots: $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$. If you lock the second slot by putting a fixed vector $w$ in it, the first slot becomes a linear machine:

- **Addition:** $\langle u + v, w \rangle = \langle u, w \rangle + \langle v, w \rangle$

- **Scaling:** $\langle cu, w \rangle = c\langle u, w \rangle$


Because it is linear in both the first slot and (by symmetry) the second slot, we call it **bilinear** (two-linear). bilinear is multilinearity plus symmetry. (Notice that det is not symmetry because)

$$
\det(u,v)=-\det(v,u)
$$

### The Standard Inner Product Space

Let $A,B\in M_{m\times n}$ , their standard inner product is defined as

$$
\langle A,B \rangle=Tr(A^{T}B)
$$
where $Tr$ is the trace( the sum of diagonal element of a matrix)

It is **exactly the standard dot product**, but instead of flattening the numbers into a long vertical column first, you leave them in a grid:

$$\langle A, B \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}b_{ij}$$

### Example in $\mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$

If $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, then:

$$\langle A, B \rangle = (1)(5) + (2)(0) + (3)(1) + (4)(2) = 5 + 0 + 3 + 8 = 16$$

The standard **Frobenius Norm (Length)** of matrix $A$ is simply the square root of the sum of all its squared entries: $\Vert{}A\Vert{} = \sqrt{1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2} = \sqrt{30}$.

## 1. The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality states that the absolute value of the inner product of two vectors is always less than or equal to the product of their individual lengths.

For any vectors $u$ and $v$ in an inner product space:

$$\vert{}\langle u, v \rangle\vert{} \le \Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{}$$

Equality holds ($\vert{}\langle u, v \rangle\vert{} = \Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{}$) if and only if $u$ and $v$ are linearly dependent (meaning one is a scalar multiple of the other).

### The Intuition: The Shadow Cannot Exceed the Object

Think back to basic trigonometry where the dot product is defined as $\langle u, v \rangle = \Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{} \cos(\theta)$. Because the value of $\vert{}\cos(\theta)\vert{}$ is trapped strictly between $0$ and $1$, the value of $\vert{}\langle u, v \rangle\vert{}$ can never exceed $\Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{}$.

Geometrically, $\langle u, v \rangle$ measures the "shadow" or projection of one vector onto another. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality simply states that **the shadow cast by an object can never be longer than the object itself**. The only way the shadow matches the full length is if the vector points in the exact same (or exact opposite) direction ($\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$).

### Why It Matters

Without Cauchy-Schwarz, we couldn't define angles in abstract spaces. Because it guarantees that $\frac{\vert{}\langle u, v \rangle\vert{}}{\Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{}} \le 1$, it ensures that when we define an angle using:

$$\cos(\theta) = \frac{\langle u, v \rangle}{\Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{}}$$

the resulting value is always a valid input for the $\arccos$ function.

## 2. The Triangle Inequality

^a09264

The Triangle Inequality states that for any two vectors $u$ and $v$, the length of their sum is less than or equal to the sum of their individual lengths.

$$\Vert{}u + v\Vert{} \le \Vert{}u\Vert{} + \Vert{}v\Vert{}$$

### The Intuition: The Shortest Distance is a Straight Line

If you start at a point, walk along vector $u$, and then walk along vector $v$, your total displacement vector from the start is $u + v$.

The triangle inequality is the mathematical way of saying: **taking a direct straight line to your destination ($\Vert{}u+v\Vert{}$) is always shorter than, or equal to, taking a detour through a third point ($\Vert{}u\Vert{} + \Vert{}v\Vert{}$)**.

## 3. How Cauchy-Schwarz Proves the Triangle Inequality

These two inequalities are not independent; the Triangle Inequality is actually a direct mathematical consequence of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Here is the elegant algebraic connection:

1. Start by squaring the length of $\Vert{}u + v\Vert{}$, which is the inner product of the vector with itself:
    
    $$\Vert{}u + v\Vert{}^2 = \langle u + v, u + v \rangle$$
    
2. Use the linearity and symmetry of the inner product to expand the slots:
    
    $$\Vert{}u + v\Vert{}^2 = \langle u, u \rangle + \langle u, v \rangle + \langle v, u \rangle + \langle v, v \rangle$$
    
    $$\Vert{}u + v\Vert{}^2 = \Vert{}u\Vert{}^2 + 2\langle u, v \rangle + \Vert{}v\Vert{}^2$$
    
3. A number is always less than or equal to its absolute value ($\langle u, v \rangle \le \vert{}\langle u, v \rangle\vert{}$). Now, apply **Cauchy-Schwarz** ($\vert{}\langle u, v \rangle\vert{} \le \Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{}$) to the middle term:
    
    $$\Vert{}u + v\Vert{}^2 \le \Vert{}u\Vert{}^2 + 2\Vert{}u\Vert{} \Vert{}v\Vert{} + \Vert{}v\Vert{}^2$$
    
4. Recognize that the right side is a perfect square trinomial ($x^2 + 2xy + y^2 = (x+y)^2$):
    
    $$\Vert{}u + v\Vert{}^2 \le (\Vert{}u\Vert{} + \Vert{}v\Vert{})^2$$
    
5. Taking the square root of both sides gives you the **Triangle Inequality**:
    
    $$\Vert{}u + v\Vert{} \le \Vert{}u\Vert{} + \Vert{}v\Vert{}$$
