
The Gram-Schmidt process is a simple algorithm for producing an orthogonal or orthonormal basis for any nonzero subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem 11 The Gram-Schmidt Process
> 
> Given a basis $\{ x_{1},\dots,x_{p} \}$ for a nonzero subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, define
> 
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> v_{1}&=x_{1} \\
> v_{2}&= x_{2}- \frac{x_{2}\cdot v_{1}}{v_{1}\cdot v_{1}} \\
> \vdots  \\
> v_{p}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Then $\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$. In addition
> 
> $$
> Span \{ v_{1},\dots,v_{k} \}=Span\{ x_{1},\dots,x_{k} \} \text{ for }1\leq k\leq p
> $$
> 

## The Theorem Statement

Let $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p\}$ be a basis for a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Define a new set of vectors $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_p\}$ inductively by:

$$v_1 = x_1$$

$$v_{k+1} = x_{k+1} - \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{x_{k+1} \cdot v_j}{\|v_j\|^2} v_j \quad \text{for } 1 \le k < p$$

Then, for each $k \in \{1, \dots, p\}$, the set $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W_k = \text{Span}\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$. Consequently, when the process terminates at $k = p$, the set $\{v_1, \dots, v_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$.

Proof:
We prove by mathematical induction. Let $P(k)$ be the statement

The constructed set $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is an orthogonal basis for the subspace $W_k = \text{Span}\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$, and every vector in this set is non-zero.

### 1. Base Case ($k = 1$)

Let $k = 1$. By definition, $v_1 = x_1$.

- Since $\{x_1, \dots, x_p\}$ is a basis, its vectors are linearly independent, which guarantees $x_1 \neq 0$. Thus, $v_1 \neq 0$.
    
- The space $W_1 = \text{Span}\{x_1\} = \text{Span}\{v_1\}$.
    
- A set containing a single non-zero vector is trivially orthogonal and linearly independent. (Why? because the orthogonal projection $x$ onto $v_{1}$ is exactly the linear combination of $v_{1}$ to form $x$)
    
Therefore, $\{v_1\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W_1$, so **$P(1)$ is true**.

### 2. Inductive Hypothesis

Assume that $P(k)$ is true for some arbitrary integer $k$ such that $1 \le k < p$.

That is, we assume $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W_k = \text{Span}\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$, and $v_i \neq 0$ for all $1 \le i \le k$.

### 3. Inductive Step

We must now show that $P(k+1)$ is true. That is, we must prove that the expanded set $\{v_1, \dots, v_k, v_{k+1}\}$ forms an orthogonal basis for $W_{k+1} = \text{Span}\{x_1, \dots, x_{k+1}\}$.

We break this down into three verifications: **Orthogonality**, **Non-zero status**, and **Span**.

#### Verification A: Orthogonality of the set

We know from our Inductive Hypothesis that the vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ are already mutually orthogonal ($v_j \cdot v_i = 0$ for $j \neq i$). We only need to show that our newly constructed vector $v_{k+1}$ is orthogonal to any arbitrary vector $v_i$ where $1 \le i \le k$.

Take the dot product of $v_{k+1}$ with $v_i$:

$$v_{k+1} \cdot v_i = \left( x_{k+1} - \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{x_{k+1} \cdot v_j}{\|v_j\|^2} v_j \right) \cdot v_i$$

Distributing the dot product across the terms:

$$v_{k+1} \cdot v_i = x_{k+1} \cdot v_i - \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{x_{k+1} \cdot v_j}{\|v_j\|^2} (v_j \cdot v_i)$$

By the inductive hypothesis, $v_j \cdot v_i = 0$ for every single term in the summation except when $j = i$. Therefore, the entire summation collapses to just the $j = i$ term:

$$v_{k+1} \cdot v_i = x_{k+1} \cdot v_i - \frac{x_{k+1} \cdot v_i}{\|v_i\|^2} (v_i \cdot v_i)$$

Since $\|v_i\|^2 = v_i \cdot v_i$, these terms cancel out perfectly:

$$v_{k+1} \cdot v_i = x_{k+1} \cdot v_i - x_{k+1} \cdot v_i = 0$$

Thus, $v_{k+1}$ is orthogonal to every vector in $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$. The set $\{v_1, \dots, v_{k+1}\}$ is entirely orthogonal.

#### Verification B: $v_{k+1}$ is a non-zero vector

Suppose for contradiction that $v_{k+1} = 0$. By the construction formula, this implies:

$$0 = x_{k+1} - \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{x_{k+1} \cdot v_j}{\|v_j\|^2} v_j \implies x_{k+1} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{x_{k+1} \cdot v_j}{\|v_j\|^2} v_j$$

This means $x_{k+1} \in \text{Span}\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$.

However, by our inductive hypothesis, $\text{Span}\{v_1, \dots, v_k\} = \text{Span}\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$.

Substituting this back yields $x_{k+1} \in \text{Span}\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$.

This directly contradicts the fact that the original set $\{x_1, \dots, x_p\}$ is a basis (and therefore must be linearly independent). No vector can be a linear combination of its predecessors. Thus, **$v_{k+1} \neq 0$**.

#### Verification C: The Basis Theorem argument

We have established that $\{v_1, \dots, v_{k+1}\}$ is an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors. Notice that $v_{k+1}\in\text{Span}\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{k},x_{k+1} \}$. Since $W_{k+1}=\text{Span}\{ x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1} \}$, it follow that $v_{k+1}\in W_{k+1}$. (Why?)

It is because by inductive hypothesis $\text{Span}\{ x_{1},\dots,x_{k} \}=\text{Span}\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{k} \}$
which implies that 

$$\text{Span}\{ x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1} \}=:Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{k},x_{k+1} \}$$


Thus, $\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{k+1} \}\subset W_{k+1}$ 

By theorem 4, $\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{k+1} \}$ is a linearly independent set containing $k+1$ element. Since $\text{dim}W_{k+1}=k+1$.  By the **Basis Theorem**, this set must automatically span the space and form a full basis for $W_{k+1}$.


Therefore, **$P(k+1)$ is true**. $\blacksquare$ 

> [!remark]
> Theorem $11$ show that any nonzero subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ has an orthogonal basis.

> [!question]
> What if the given set is not a basis?
> Then it might be a linearly dependent set. Let say $u_{2}\in\text{Span}\{ u_{1} \}$, then $\text{proj}_{L}u_{2}=u_{2}$ which implies
> 
> $$
> u_{2}'= u_{2}-\text{proj}_{L}u_{2}=0
> $$
> Thus, the new vector that we obtain is a zero vector.

## Orthonormal Bases

An orthonormal basis is constructed easily from an orthogonal basis $\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}$; simply normalize all the $v_{k}$.

## QR Factorization of Matrices

> [!theorem] Theorem 12 The QR Factorization
> 
> If $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix with linearly independent columns, then $A$ can be factored as $A=QR$, where $Q$ is an $m\times n$ matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for Col $A$ and $R$ is an $n\times n$ upper triangular invertible matrix with positive entries on its diagonal.
> 

Proof:
The columns of $A$ form a basis $\{ x_{1},\dots,x_{n} \}$ for Col $A$ ($\mathbb{R}^{n}$). Construct an orthonormal basis $\{ u_{1},\dots u_{n} \}$ by Theorem 11 and normalizing the vector.

Let $Q=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1} & u_{2} & \dots & u_{n}\end{bmatrix}$. For $1\leq k\leq n$, $x_{k}\in Span\{ x_{1},\dots,x_{k} \}=Span\{ u_{1},\dots,u_{k} \}$. So there exist $r_{1k},\dots r_{kk}$ such that

$$
x_{k}=r_{1k}u_{1}+\dots+r_{kk}u_{k}+0 u_{k+1}
+\dots+0 u_{n}$$

We may assume that $r_{kk}\geq 0$. (If $r_{kk}< 0$, multiply both $r_{kk}$ and $u_{k}$ by $-1$)

Let
 $$rk=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1k} \\ \vdots \\ r_{kk} \\ \vdots \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$$

Then, $x_{k}=Qr_{k}$ for $k=1,\dots,n$. Let $R=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1} & \dots & r_{n}\end{bmatrix}$. Then

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} & \dots & x_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
Qr_{1} & \dots & Qr_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=QR
$$

The fact that $R$ is invertible follows easily from the fact that the columns of $A$ are linearly independent (Exercise 23). Since $R$ is clearly upper triangular, its nonnegative diagonal entries must be positive.

Exercise 23:
Proof:
Suppose $A=QR,$ where $Q$ is $m\times n$ and $R$ is $n\times n$ and the columns of $A$ are linearly independent. Thus, $\text{Nul}A=\{ 0 \}$. Since $A=QR$, it follow that
$$
\begin{align}
\text{Nul}A&=\text{Nul}(QR)
\end{align}
$$
Thus, the system 
$$
(QR)x=0
$$
only has trivial solution. 

It didn't work, another approach:
Suppose there exists a vector $\mathbf{x}$ such that:

$$R\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$$
We are given that $A = QR$. Let's multiply both sides of our equation by the matrix $Q$ from the left:

$$Q(R\mathbf{x}) = Q(\mathbf{0})$$

By associative property of matrix multiplication, this becomes:

$$(QR)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$$

Since $A = QR$, we can substitute $A$ into the equation:

$$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$$
Since the columns of $A$ are linearly independent, it follow that $Ax=0$ only have trivial solution which is $x=0$. Thus, it follow that $Rx=0$ only have trivial solution. Since $R$ is an $n\times n$ square matrix, by Invertible Matrix Theorem, $R$ is invertible. $\blacksquare$.


> [!question]
> 
> Why we can assume $r_{kk}\geq 0$? 
> 
> In the Gram-Schmidt process (or any construction of the orthonormal basis), the choice of direction for each basis vector $\mathbf{u}_k$ is flexible. If you multiply an orthonormal vector by $-1$, it remains normalized and orthogonal to all other vectors.
