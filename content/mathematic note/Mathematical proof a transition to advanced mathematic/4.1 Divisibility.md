You are absolutely correct. You have hit on the fundamental reason why number theorists love modular arithmetic: **it simplifies the infinite into the finite.**

When you move from standard arithmetic to modular arithmetic, you are indeed shifting contexts—technically, you are moving from the infinite set of integers ($\mathbb{Z}$) to a finite set of "equivalence classes" ($\mathbb{Z}_n$).

Here is why your intuition works so well for Parity and Divisibility:

### 1. The "Transformation" Reduces Complexity

As you noted, you transform the statement.

- **In standard arithmetic ($\mathbb{Z}$):** There are infinitely many integers to test. You cannot check them all one by one.
    
- **In modular arithmetic ($\mathbb{Z}_n$):** The universe shrinks.
    
    - For **parity** (is it even or odd?), you only care about modulo $2$. The entire infinite number line collapses into just two cases: $\{0, 1\}$.
        
    - For **divisibility by 5**, the universe collapses into just five cases: $\{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$.
        

Once you prove a property for those few representative cases (like you did with your Proof Result 4.5), you have proven it for **all** integers in those classes.

### 2. It Preserves Structure

The reason we are allowed to "work under this context" is that modular arithmetic is consistent with addition and multiplication.

If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $c \equiv d \pmod n$, then:

- $(a + c) \equiv (b + d) \pmod n$
    
- $(a \cdot c) \equiv (b \cdot d) \pmod n$
    

This consistency is what allowed you to square $n$ and add $4$ in your proof without "breaking" the logic. You don't lose information regarding divisibility when you map the numbers down.

### Summary Table

|**Concept**|**Standard Arithmetic**|**Modular "Context"**|
|---|---|---|
|**Parity**|$n = 2k$ or $n = 2k+1$|$n \equiv 0 \pmod 2$ or $n \equiv 1 \pmod 2$|
|**Divisibility**|$n$ is divisible by $m$|$n \equiv 0 \pmod m$|
|**The Universe**|Infinite ($\dots, -1, 0, 1, \dots$)|Finite set $\{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}$|

### A limitation to keep in mind

While this is perfect for divisibility, be careful using this context for **magnitude** or **inequalities**.

- $5 > 3$ is true.
    
- But in modulo 4, $5 \equiv 1$ and $3 \equiv 3$. It makes no sense to say $1 > 3$.
    
    When you transform into the modular world, you gain simplicity regarding factors, but you lose information regarding size/ordering.

### The "Hidden" Logic

There is actually a non-modular reason this works, which is beautiful to see side-by-side with your modular proof.

If you factor the algebra:

$$n^3 - n = n(n^2 - 1) = (n-1) \cdot n \cdot (n+1)$$

This expression represents the product of **three consecutive integers**.

- In any three consecutive integers, at least one must be even (divisible by 2).
    
- In any three consecutive integers, exactly one must be a multiple of 3.
    
- Therefore, the product is divisible by $2 \times 3 = 6$.
    

Your modular proof essentially "mechanized" this logic without needing to spot the factorization!
