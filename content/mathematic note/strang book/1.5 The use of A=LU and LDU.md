A can be split into 2 product of matrix which is $LU$. $L$ is lower triangle matrix that represent the forward elimination part while $U$ is upper triangle matrix that represent the backward substitution. 


What is the use of $A=LU$, it helps to increase the efficiency of calculating the solution instead of using row operation.

- **Factoring $A$:** Finding $L$ and $U$ is the expensive part. It takes roughly $\frac{n^3}{3}$ operations.
    
- **Solving the Systems:** Once you have $L$ and $U$, solving $Lc = b$ and $Ux = c$ is incredibly cheap—only about $n^2$ operations each.
    

**The "Killer App" for LU:** If you have a situation where the matrix $A$ stays the same but you have 100 different "right-hand sides" ($b_1, b_2, \dots, b_{100}$), you only factor $A$ **once**. For every new $b$, you just do the $n^2$ substitution steps. If you used standard Gaussian elimination every time, you’d be doing the $n^3$ work over and over.


### How it work?

Since $A=LU$, it follow that 

$$
\begin{align}
Ax&=b \\
(LU)x&=b \\
L(Ux)&=b
\end{align}
$$

Thus, we let $Ux=c$, and we want to find such $c$

$$
Lc=b
$$

After we find the c, then we can solve for $Ux=c$.


### LDU
If $A = LU$ is the "record" of elimination, then **$A = LDU$** is the "refined" version. It’s what happens when you decide that $L$ and $U$ should be treated with equal respect.

In the standard $A = LU$, the matrix $L$ usually has $1$s on the diagonal, but $U$ has the **pivots** ($d_1, d_2, \dots, d_n$) on its diagonal. $LDU$ decomposition pulls those pivots out into a separate diagonal matrix $D$.

---

## 1. The Structure: Pulling Out the Pivots

To get from $LU$ to $LDU$, you take the upper triangular matrix $U$ and divide each row by its pivot.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \ell_{21} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & u_{12} \\ 0 & d_2 \end{bmatrix} \implies \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \ell_{21} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & 0 \\ 0 & d_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & u_{12}/d_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Now you have:

- **$L$:** Unit Lower Triangular (1s on the diagonal).
    
- **$D$:** Diagonal (the pivots).
    
- **$U$:** Unit Upper Triangular (1s on the diagonal).
    
---

## 2. Why Mathematicians Love $LDU$

### **Reason A: Perfect Symmetry**

This is the "aha!" moment. If your original matrix $A$ is **symmetric** ($A = A^T$), then something magical happens: **$U$ is exactly $L^T$**.


$$A = LDL^T$$

Instead of storing two different triangular matrices, you only need to store one ($L$) and the diagonal ($D$). This is the foundation for the **Cholesky Decomposition** ($A = LL^T$ for positive definite matrices). It reveals that the "forward" elimination and "backward" elimination are mirror images of each other.

### **Reason B: Uniqueness**

The $LU$ factorization isn't technically unique unless you specify that $L$ must have $1$s on the diagonal (making it "unit" lower triangular). However, the $LDU$ factorization is **uniquely determined** by $A$ (assuming no row exchanges are needed). In the world of abstract logic, uniqueness is gold—it means you've found the "true" internal structure of the operator.

### **Reason C: The "Energy" of the Matrix**

The diagonal $D$ contains the pivots. In physics and engineering, these pivots often relate to the "stiffness" or "energy" of a system. By isolating them in $D$, you can see exactly how "strong" or "stable" each dimension of the transformation is without them being tangled up inside the entries of $U$.

### The existence of permutation matrix P

All the things we are talking above is under the assumption that no row exchange. What is a system need row exchange?

When?
When a system of matrix have zero in their pivot position,

In the nonsingular case, there is a permutation matrix P that reorders the rows of A to avoid zeros in the pivot positions. Then Ax=b has a unique solution: 

**With the rows reordered in advance, PA can be factored into LU.**

## 2. Enter the Permutation Matrix ($P$)

A mathematician doesn't like to just "swap rows on the fly" because it messes up the record-keeping in $L$. Instead, we use a **Permutation Matrix $P$**.

- **What is $P$?** It is the Identity matrix with its rows reordered.
    
- **The Logic:** Multiplying $P$ by $A$ performs the row swaps **before** you even start elimination.
    

By finding the right $P$ first, you ensure that $PA$ (the reordered $A$) is a matrix that "behaves" and allows for a perfect $LU$ decomposition. Thus:

$$PA = LU$$

## 3. The Multiplier Shift (The Tricky Part)

The text points out a subtle danger: **"You have to be careful with $L$."**

If you perform an elimination step (creating a multiplier $\ell_{21}$) and _then_ swap row 2 and row 3, that multiplier $\ell_{21}$ is now physically in the wrong place. It was meant for the "old" row 2, which is now row 3.

In the example provided:

- To get $0$ in the $(2,1)$ position of $A$, the multiplier was $1$ ($row 2 - 1 \times row 1$).
    
- To get $0$ in the $(3,1)$ position of $A$, the multiplier was $2$ ($row 3 - 2 \times row 1$).
    
- **But**, because rows 2 and 3 were swapped, those multipliers also "swapped" their importance.
    
- In the final $L$, you see $\ell_{21} = 2$ and $\ell_{31} = 1$. They effectively moved along with the rows.
