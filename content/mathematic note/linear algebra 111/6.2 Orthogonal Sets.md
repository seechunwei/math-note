### How to understand orthogonality intuitively?

If $\{ u_{1},u_{2} \}$ is orthogonal set, then we say that information along the direction of $\mathbf{u}_1$ is "completely invisible" to $\mathbf{u}_2$. It means that moving along the $u_{1}$ axis has absolutely zero effect on the position along the $u_{2}$ axis.

#### Think of moving east vs north
Walking a distance toward east doesn't help to move you a single millimeter closer or further from the North Pole.

#### Express using mathematic

Let's test what happens when a vector contains information _only_ along the direction of $\mathbf{u}_1$. Let this vector be $\mathbf{x} = c_1 \mathbf{u}_1$ (where $c_1 \neq 0$).

Now, let's see what the $\mathbf{u}_2$ sensor detects when it scans $\mathbf{x}$:

$$\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = (c_1 \mathbf{u}_1) \cdot \mathbf{u}_2$$

Using the properties of the dot product, we can pull the scalar coefficient $c_1$ to the front:

$$\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = c_1 (\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2)$$

Because $\mathbf{u}_1$ and $\mathbf{u}_2$ are strictly orthogonal, their dot product is by definition exactly $0$:

$$\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = c_1 (0) = 0$$

No matter how massive $c_1$ is—even if $c_1 = 1,000,000$—the sensor tracking $\mathbf{u}_2$ will read exactly **0**. The vector $\mathbf{u}_2$ looks at $\mathbf{x}$ and detects absolutely nothing. This is what makes it "invisible."

When break down $y$ into a sum of projections, each basis vector hold a part of the $y$ and the intersection of each part is empty set.

## 3. The Geometric Intuition: Dimension Shifting

Geometrically, a matrix acts as a transformation that maps vectors from one space to another.

- A $3 \times 2$ matrix $U$ takes a vector from a **2D space** ($\mathbb{R}^2$) and shoots it up into a **3D space** ($\mathbb{R}^3$).
    
- If the columns are orthonormal, $U$ does this without stretching space; it just perfectly embeds a flat 2D plane into a 3D room.
    
- When you apply $U^T U$, you are taking a 2D vector, mapping it to 3D, and then pulling it right back to 2D. Because you never left the "plane" of information, you land exactly back where you started ($U^T U = I_2$).
    
- But when you apply $U U^T$, you are starting in **3D space**. $U^T$ squashes that 3D space down onto a 2D plane, throwing away an entire dimension of data in the process. When $U$ lifts it back up to 3D, **that lost dimension is gone forever.** Because information was destroyed, $U U^T$ cannot be the identity mapping. Instead, it acts as an _orthogonal projection matrix_ that projects 3D space down onto the 2D subspace spanned by the columns of $U$.
