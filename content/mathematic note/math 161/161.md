To derive the formula for the standard deviation of the difference between two means (often called the **Standard Error of the Difference**), we rely on a fundamental property of variance known as the **Variance Sum Law**.

The formula you are looking for is:

$$\sigma_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}$$

Here is the step-by-step derivation.

### 1. The Variance Property

The most critical rule in this derivation is how variance behaves when you combine two **independent** random variables, $X$ and $Y$.

$$Var(X - Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)$$

Why is it a plus sign?

Mathematically, the coefficient of $Y$ is $-1$. When you pull a constant out of a variance calculation, you must square it.

$$Var(X - Y) = Var(X + (-1)Y)$$

$$= Var(X) + (-1)^2 Var(Y)$$

$$= Var(X) + 1 \cdot Var(Y)$$

$$= Var(X) + Var(Y)$$

### 2. Apply to Sample Means

We are interested in the difference between two sample means, $\bar{x}_1$ and $\bar{x}_2$. Let's apply the rule above to these variables:

$$Var(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) = Var(\bar{x}_1) + Var(\bar{x}_2)$$

### 3. Substitute the Variance of a Mean

We know from the Central Limit Theorem that the variance of a single sample mean ($\bar{x}$) is the population variance divided by the sample size:

$$Var(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

Substitute this into our equation:

$$Var(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}$$

### 4. Convert to Standard Deviation

We now have the _variance_ of the difference. To get the **standard deviation** (standard error), we simply take the square root of the variance:

$$\sigma_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{Var(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)}$$

$$\sigma_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}$$

### Summary

1. **Start with Variance:** You cannot add standard deviations directly; you must add variances.
    
2. **Square the Negative:** The subtraction in the numerator ($X - Y$) becomes addition in the variance formula because squaring the negative 1 makes it positive.
    
3. **Root it:** Take the square root at the very end to return to standard deviation units.

The derivation for the difference between two **proportions** follows the exact same logic as the difference between two means, but the starting ingredient (the variance of a single proportion) is different.

Here is the step-by-step derivation for the standard deviation of the difference between sample proportions ($\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$).

### 1. The Variance Sum Law (Again)

Just like with means, we rely on the rule that the variance of a difference is the **sum** of the variances (for independent samples):

$$Var(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = Var(\hat{p}_1) + Var(\hat{p}_2)$$

### 2. The Variance of a Single Proportion

This is the key difference. For means, we used $\sigma^2/n$. For proportions, we must look at the **Bernoulli distribution** (success/failure).

- If you count the number of successes ($X$) in $n$ trials, the variance is $np(1-p)$ (from the Binomial distribution).
    
- However, a sample proportion is not the _count_ ($X$), but the _ratio_ ($\hat{p} = \frac{X}{n}$).
    

When you divide a random variable by a constant ($n$), the variance is divided by the constant **squared** ($n^2$).

$$Var(\hat{p}) = Var\left(\frac{X}{n}\right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot Var(X)$$

$$Var(\hat{p}) = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot [np(1-p)]$$

$$Var(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}$$

### 3. Substitute into the Sum Law

Now we plug this specific variance formula back into our main equation from Step 1.

$$Var(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \left( \frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} \right) + \left( \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2} \right)$$

### 4. Convert to Standard Deviation (Standard Error)

Finally, take the square root of the variance to get the standard deviation.

$$\sigma_{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}}$$

---

### Important Note: "Pooled" vs. "Unpooled"

When you actually **use** this formula in a hypothesis test, you have to make a choice that doesn't exist for means:

1. **Confidence Intervals (Unpooled):** We assume $p_1$ and $p_2$ are different, so we use the formula exactly as derived above (using sample estimates $\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$).
    
2. Hypothesis Testing (Pooled): If your null hypothesis is that the proportions are equal ($p_1 = p_2$), you assume there is essentially one giant population. You combine the groups to find a "pooled proportion" ($\bar{p}$) and use that for both sides:
    
    $$\sigma_{pooled} = \sqrt{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p}) \left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}$$
