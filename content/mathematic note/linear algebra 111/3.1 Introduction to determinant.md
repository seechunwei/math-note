==The recursive definition==
For $n\geq 2$, the determinant of an $n\times n$ matrix $A=[a_{ij}]$ is the sum of $n$ terms of the form $\pm a_{1j}\det A_{1j}$, with plus and minus signs alternating. In symbols,

$$
\begin{align}
\det A&=a_{11}\det A_{11}-a_{12}\det A_{12}+\dots+(-1)^{1+n}a_{1n}\det A_{1n} \\
&= \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{1+j}a_{1j}\det A_{1j}
\end{align}
$$

Another common notation for the determinant of a matrix uses a pair of vertical lines in place of brackets.

$$
\det A=|A|
$$

==The Laplace Cofactor Expansion==

First we need to introduce the term cofactors

==Definition==
For an $n \times n$ matrix $A=[a_{ij}]$, the $(i-j)$ cofactor of $A$ is the number $C_{ij}$ given by

$$
C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}
$$
where $\det A_{ij}$ called the **minor** of entry $a_{ij}$ of $A$, is the determinant of the submatrix. Thus,

==Theorem 1==
The determinant of an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ can be computed by a cofactor expansion across any row or down any column. 

$$
\det A=a_{i_{1}}C_{i_{1}}+\dots+a_{i n}C_{i n}
$$

or

$$
\det A=a_{1j}C_{1j}+\dots+a_{nj}C_{nj}
$$

==Theorem 2==
If $A$ is a triangular matrix, then $\det A$ is the product of the entries on the main diagonal of $A$.

Proof:
We prove by mathematical induction. Suppose $P(n)$ is a property defined by

$$
P(n): \text{The det of a nxn triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal}
$$

For basis step, we need to show that that $P(2)$ is true. Suppose a $2\times 2$ upper triangular matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & d\end{bmatrix}$, its determinant is

$$
\det A=ad-b(0)=ad
$$
Thus, it is clear that $P(2)$ is true.

For induction step, suppose $n$ is an arbitrary positive number and suppose $P(n)$ is true. Thus, we need to show that $P(n+1)$ is true.

Suppose a triangular matrix $A=[a_{ij}]_{(k+1)\times(k+1)}$. Thus, the determinant of $A$ obtained by cofactor expansion across first column is denoted by

$$
\begin{align}
\det A&=a_{11}C_{11} \\
&=a_{11}\det A_{11}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that $A_{11}$ is a $k\times k$ upper triangular matrix. Thus, by induction hypothesis, 

$$
\det A_{11}=a_{22}\cdot\dots a_{(k+1)(k+1)}
$$


Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\det A=a_{11}\cdot a_{22}\cdot\dots a_{(k+1)(k+1)}
\end{align}
$$
Thus, by mathematical induction $P(n)$ is true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $n\geq 2$.
