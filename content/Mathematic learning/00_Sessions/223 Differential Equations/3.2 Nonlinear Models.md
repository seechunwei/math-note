In this section, we study nonlinear first-order differential equations that model physical, biological, and chemical phenomena where linear models (such as pure exponential growth) break down due to environmental constraints or nonlinear interactions.

---

# 1. Population Dynamics: The Density-Dependent Hypothesis

## From Linear to Nonlinear
In Section 3.1, the linear model for population growth assumed that the rate of growth is directly proportional to the current population:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = kP, \quad k > 0
$$
Dividing both sides by $P$ gives the **relative growth rate** (or **per capita / specific growth rate**):
$$
\frac{dP/dt}{P} = k \qquad \tag{1}
$$

> [!note] Physical Meaning of the Per Capita Rate $\frac{dP/dt}{P}$
> * **$P(t)$**: Total population size at time $t$.
> * **$\frac{dP}{dt}$**: Absolute growth rate (total number of individuals added per unit of time).
> * **$\frac{dP/dt}{P} = \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt}$**: Growth rate **per individual** in the population (births minus deaths per individual per unit time).

In equation $(1)$, the per capita rate is a constant $k$. While this approximates early-stage growth in an uncrowded environment, **unbounded exponential growth cannot continue indefinitely**. Real environments possess limited resources (food, space, nesting sites, accumulation of toxic waste). 

As the population $P$ grows, crowding effects inevitably force the per capita growth rate to **decrease**.

---

## The General Density-Dependent Equation
Assuming that the relative growth rate depends solely on the current population size $P$ (rather than seasonal or explicit time-dependent factors), we state the **density-dependent hypothesis**:

$$
\frac{dP/dt}{P} = f(P) \quad \iff \quad \frac{dP}{dt} = P f(P) \qquad \tag{2}
$$

Here, $f(P)$ represents the per capita growth rate as a function of population density $P$.

---

# 2. The Logistic Equation

## Constructing $f(P)$
Suppose an environment can sustainably support no more than a fixed maximum number $K$ of individuals. The constant $K$ is called the **carrying capacity** of the environment.

We impose two natural biological boundary conditions on $f(P)$:
1. **At low density ($P \to 0$):** Resources are plentiful. Individuals reproduce near their intrinsic physiological maximum rate $r$:
   $$
   f(0) = r > 0
   $$
2. **At carrying capacity ($P = K$):** The environment is saturated. Birth rate balances mortality, so net per capita growth ceases:
   $$
   f(K) = 0
   $$
3. **Overcrowding ($P > K$):** Resources become deficient, mortality exceeds reproduction, so $f(P) < 0$.

The simplest mathematical assumption satisfying these conditions is that $f(P)$ is a **linear function** of $P$:
$$
f(P) = c_1 P + c_2
$$

![[Pasted image 20261024190204.png]]

Applying the boundary conditions:
* $f(0) = c_2 = r$
* $f(K) = c_1 K + r = 0 \implies c_1 = -\frac{r}{K}$

Thus:
$$
f(P) = r - \frac{r}{K}P
$$

Substituting $f(P)$ back into $(2)$ yields:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = P \left( r - \frac{r}{K}P \right) \qquad \tag{3}
$$

Relabeling constants as $a = r$ and $b = \frac{r}{K}$, we obtain the canonical **logistic differential equation**:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP) = aP - bP^2 \qquad \tag{4}
$$
where $a > 0$ and $b > 0$.

> [!remark] Interpretation of the Terms
> * **$+aP$**: The vital reproductive impulse (proportional to individuals present).
> * **$-bP^2$**: The nonlinear **inhibition / competition** term. In a population of $P$ individuals, the number of potential competitive two-body interactions is $\binom{P}{2} = \frac{P(P-1)}{2} \propto P^2$.

The equation (4) known as the **logistic equation**, and its solution is called the logistic function. The graph of a logistic function is called a **logistic curve**.

---

## Analytical Solution via Separation of Variables

To solve the initial value problem:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP), \quad P(0) = P_0
$$

Separate variables:
$$
\frac{dP}{P(a - bP)} = dt
$$

### Step 1: Partial Fraction Decomposition
Decompose the left-hand side:
$$
\frac{1}{P(a - bP)} = \frac{A}{P} + \frac{B}{a - bP}
$$
Multiplying by $P(a - bP)$:
$$
1 = A(a - bP) + BP
$$
* Setting $P = 0 \implies 1 = A(a) \implies A = \frac{1}{a}$
* Setting $P = \frac{a}{b} \implies 1 = B\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) \implies B = \frac{b}{a}$

Thus:
$$
\left( \frac{1/a}{P} + \frac{b/a}{a - bP} \right) dP = dt
$$

### Step 2: Integrate Both Sides
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{a} \int \frac{1}{P}\, dP + \frac{b}{a} \int \frac{1}{a - bP}\, dP &= \int dt \\
\frac{1}{a} \ln|P| - \frac{1}{a} \ln|a - bP| &= t + c \\
\ln\left| \frac{P}{a - bP} \right| &= at + ac \\
\frac{P}{a - bP} &= c_1 e^{at} \quad (c_1 = \pm e^{ac})
\end{align}
$$

### Step 3: Solve for $P(t)$
$$
\begin{align}
P &= c_1 e^{at} (a - bP) \\
P + b c_1 e^{at} P &= a c_1 e^{at} \\
P(1 + b c_1 e^{at}) &= a c_1 e^{at} \\
P(t) &= \frac{a c_1 e^{at}}{1 + b c_1 e^{at}} = \frac{a c_1}{b c_1 + e^{-at}}
\end{align}
$$

### Step 4: Apply Initial Condition $P(0) = P_0$
$$
P_0 = \frac{a c_1}{b c_1 + 1} \implies P_0 (b c_1 + 1) = a c_1 \implies c_1(a - b P_0) = P_0 \implies c_1 = \frac{P_0}{a - b P_0}
$$

Substituting $c_1$ back:
$$
P(t) = \frac{a \left( \frac{P_0}{a - b P_0} \right)}{b \left( \frac{P_0}{a - b P_0} \right) + e^{-at}}
$$
Multiply numerator and denominator by $(a - b P_0)$:
$$
P(t) = \frac{a P_0}{b P_0 + (a - b P_0)e^{-at}} \qquad \tag{5}
$$

In terms of carrying capacity $K = \frac{a}{b}$ and growth rate $r = a$:
$$
P(t) = \frac{K P_0}{P_0 + (K - P_0)e^{-rt}}
$$

---

## Qualitative Properties of the Logistic Curve

### 1. Long-Term Asymptotic Limit (Carrying Capacity)
As $t \to \infty$, $e^{-at} \to 0$ (since $a > 0$):
$$
\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \frac{a P_0}{b P_0} = \frac{a}{b} = K
$$
Regardless of whether $P_0 < K$ or $P_0 > K$, the population asymptotically approaches the stable equilibrium $P = K$.

### 2. Inflection Point and Maximum Growth Rate
Differentiating the autonomous equation $\frac{dP}{dt} = aP - bP^2$ with respect to $t$ using the chain rule:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d^2 P}{dt^2} &= \frac{d}{dt}\left[ aP - bP^2 \right] \\
&= (a - 2bP)\frac{dP}{dt} \\
&= (a - 2bP) P (a - bP) \\
&= 2b^2 P \left( P - \frac{a}{b} \right) \left( P - \frac{a}{2b} \right)
\end{align}
$$

Setting $\frac{d^2 P}{dt^2} = 0$:
* $P = 0$ (trivial extinction boundary)
* $P = \frac{a}{b} = K$ (carrying capacity / horizontal asymptote)
* $P = \frac{a}{2b} = \frac{K}{2}$ (**Point of Inflection**)

> [!remark] Shape of the Solution Curve
> * For $0 < P < \frac{a}{2b}$: $\frac{d^2 P}{dt^2} > 0 \implies$ curve is **concave up** (accelerating growth).
> * For $\frac{a}{2b} < P < \frac{a}{b}$: $\frac{d^2 P}{dt^2} < 0 \implies$ curve is **concave down** (decelerating growth).
> * When $0 < P_0 < \frac{K}{2}$, the curve assumes the characteristic **S-shape (sigmoid curve)**.
> * The population grows fastest precisely when it reaches **half of its carrying capacity**: $P = \frac{K}{2}$.

# 3. Example 1: Spread of an Epidemic

A student carrying a flu virus returns to an isolated college campus of $1000$ students. The rate at which the virus spreads is proportional to the number of infected students $x(t)$ and the number of uninfected students $(1000 - x(t))$. 

Given $x(0) = 1$ and $x(4) = 50$, determine the number of infected students after $6$ days.

### 1. How the Model Equation is Formulated
From the problem description:
1. **Total Population ($N = 1000$):** Since the campus is isolated, the total population $N = 1000$ is constant throughout the disease.
2. **Infected vs. Uninfected:** If $x(t)$ denotes the number of infected students at time $t$, then the number of uninfected (susceptible) students is $(1000 - x(t))$.
3. **Joint Proportionality:** The statement *"the rate at which the virus spreads is proportional not only to the number of infected students but also to the number of students not infected"* means the rate is proportional to their **product**:
   $$
   \frac{dx}{dt} \propto x \cdot (1000 - x) \implies \frac{dx}{dt} = k x (1000 - x)
   $$
4. **Initial Condition ($x(0) = 1$):** The phrase *"a student carrying a flu virus returns..."* specifies that exactly $1$ infected individual introduces the virus to campus at $t = 0$:
   $$
   x(0) = 1
   $$

---

### 2. Two Perspectives on Epidemic Spread (Connection to Population Growth)

#### Perspective 1: Total Rate Perspective (Product of Two Pools / Viewpoint A)
We view the overall infection rate as the interaction between **active carriers** and **available targets**:
$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = k \cdot \underbrace{x}_{\text{Infected Carriers}} \cdot \underbrace{(1000 - x)}_{\text{Remaining Susceptible Targets}}
$$

This has the identical mathematical and conceptual structure as the logistic population model:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = \frac{r}{K} \cdot \underbrace{P}_{\text{Current Parents}} \cdot \underbrace{(K - P)}_{\text{Remaining Capacity}}
$$

| Model                 | Active Entity ($x$ or $P$) | Available Capacity ($N - x$ or $K - P$) | Governing Differential Equation                                    |
| :-------------------- | :------------------------- | :-------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Flu Epidemic**      | Infected students: $x$     | Uninfected students: $(1000 - x)$       | $\frac{dx}{dt} = k \cdot \mathbf{x(1000 - x)}$                     |
| **Population Growth** | Living population: $P$     | Unused carrying capacity: $(K - P)$     | $\frac{dP}{dt} = \left(\frac{r}{K}\right) \cdot \mathbf{P(K - P)}$ |

* **Why a product? (Contact Hypothesis / Law of Mass Action):** Transmission requires a physical encounter between an infected student and a susceptible student. The number of possible distinct pairwise encounters is $x \times (1000 - x)$. If a fraction $k$ of these contacts results in transmission, the total rate is $kx(1000 - x)$.

---

#### Perspective 2: Per-Capita Perspective ($\frac{1}{x}\frac{dx}{dt}$)
Can we view epidemic spread through the per-capita lens? **Yes!** Dividing both sides by $x$ gives the **transmission rate per single infected student**:

$$
\frac{dx/dt}{x} = k(1000 - x)
$$

Compare this directly to per-capita population growth:
$$
\frac{dP/dt}{P} = \frac{r}{K}(K - P)
$$

* **Physical Meaning:** $\frac{1}{x}\frac{dx}{dt}$ measures how many new infections **each sick student transmits per unit of time**.
* **Early Stage ($x \approx 1$):** Healthy individuals are everywhere ($1000 - x \approx 999$). Almost every encounter a sick student has is with an uninfected person, so the per-capita transmission rate is at its absolute maximum ($k \times 1000$).
* **Late Stage ($x \to 1000$):** Healthy students are scarce ($1000 - x \to 0$). Most people a sick student encounters are already infected, so the transmission rate per sick individual plummets toward $0$.

> [!tip] Summary of the Two Perspectives
> * **Perspective 1 (Macro / Total Rate):** $\frac{dx}{dt} = kx(1000 - x)$ describes the **overall speed** of the epidemic across the whole community (which peaks at the inflection point $x = 500$).
> * **Perspective 2 (Micro / Per-Capita):** $\frac{1}{x}\frac{dx}{dt} = k(1000 - x)$ describes the **individual spreading power** of a single carrier, which decreases strictly monotonically from day zero as susceptible targets become exhausted.

---

### 3. Solution of the IVP
From the logistic solution formula $(5)$:
$$
x(t) = \frac{1000(1)}{1 + (1000 - 1)e^{-1000kt}} = \frac{1000}{1 + 999 e^{-1000kt}}
$$

### Finding the Parameter $k$
Use the observed data $x(4) = 50$:
$$
\begin{align}
50 &= \frac{1000}{1 + 999 e^{-4000k}} \\
1 + 999 e^{-4000k} &= \frac{1000}{50} = 20 \\
999 e^{-4000k} &= 19 \\
e^{-4000k} &= \frac{19}{999} \\
-4000k &= \ln\left( \frac{19}{999} \right) \approx -3.9624 \\
1000k &\approx 0.9906
\end{align}
$$

So the formula becomes:
$$
x(t) = \frac{1000}{1 + 999 e^{-0.9906 t}}
$$

### Calculating $x(6)$
$$
x(6) = \frac{1000}{1 + 999 e^{-0.9906(6)}} = \frac{1000}{1 + 999 e^{-5.9436}} \approx \frac{1000}{1 + 999(0.002622)} \approx 276 \text{ students}
$$

## 1. The Gompertz Differential Equation
* **Textbook Location**: Section 3.2 Main Text (p. 97, under subsection *"Modifications of the Logistic Equation"*, Eq. 7) and **Exercises 3.2, Problem 8** (p. 100).
* **Concept**: Named after Benjamin Gompertz (1779–1865), this model replaces the linear decline of $f(P)$ with a logarithmic decline:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - b\ln P) \qquad \tag{6}
$$

### Analytical Solution
Use the substitution $u = \ln P \implies \frac{du}{dt} = \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt}$:
$$
\frac{du}{dt} = a - bu \implies \frac{du}{dt} + bu = a
$$
This is a standard linear first-order DE with integrating factor $e^{bt}$:
$$
u(t) = \frac{a}{b} + c_1 e^{-bt}
$$
Substituting back $u = \ln P$:
$$
\ln P = \frac{a}{b} + c_1 e^{-bt} \implies P(t) = e^{a/b} \exp\left( c_1 e^{-bt} \right) = K \left( \frac{P_0}{K} \right)^{e^{-bt}}
$$
where $K = e^{a/b}$ is the carrying capacity and $c_1 = \ln(P_0/K)$.

### Qualitative Behavior and Graph Geometry
1. **Horizontal Asymptote (Carrying Capacity $K = e^{a/b}$):**
   * As $t \to \infty$, $e^{-bt} \to 0$, so $P(t) \to e^{a/b} = K$.
   * If $P_0 < K$, the solution grows toward $K$.
   * If $P_0 > K$, the solution decays toward $K$.
2. **Concavity & Inflection Point (Asymmetry):**
   Differentiating $\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - b\ln P)$:
   $$
   \frac{d^2 P}{dt^2} = \frac{dP}{dt}(a - b\ln P) + P\left(-\frac{b}{P}\frac{dP}{dt}\right) = \frac{dP}{dt}(a - b - b\ln P)
   $$
   Setting $\frac{d^2 P}{dt^2} = 0$:
   $$
   a - b - b\ln P = 0 \implies \ln P = \frac{a}{b} - 1 \implies P_{\text{inflection}} = e^{a/b - 1} = \frac{K}{e} \approx 0.368 K
   $$
   * **Gompertz vs. Logistic:** While the logistic curve is symmetric with its inflection point precisely at the midpoint $P = 0.5 K$, the Gompertz curve is **asymmetric** with its inflection point occurring much earlier at $P = \frac{K}{e} \approx 36.8\%$.
   * Growth accelerates rapidly in early stages, reaches peak rate early, and then exhibits a prolonged, slow deceleration toward saturation (reflecting tumor nutrient starvation).

## 2. Harvesting and Restocking Models
* **Textbook Location**: Section 3.2 Main Text (p. 97, Eq. 6) and **Exercises 3.2, Problems 5, 6, 7** (Fishery Harvesting) & **Problem 24** (Immigration).
* **Concept**: When an external extraction (harvesting) or replenishment (restocking) rate $h$ is introduced:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP) \pm h
$$
* **Constant harvesting ($ - h $)** (Exercises 3.2, Probs. 5–7):  
  Models fisheries with fixed catch quotas. If $h > \frac{a^2}{4b}$, $\frac{dP}{dt} < 0$ for all $P$, driving the population to **extinction in finite time**.
* **Proportional harvesting ($ - cP $)**:  
  Harvesting effort proportional to population size:
  $$
  \frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP) - cP = P\big((a - c) - bP\big)
  $$
  This simply reduces the effective intrinsic growth rate from $a$ to $(a - c)$.

---

## 3. Doomsday and Threshold Models in Exercises 3.2
* **Doomsday Equation (Problem 21, p. 101)**:
  $$
  \frac{dP}{dt} = kP^{1+c}, \quad c > 0
  $$
  Separating variables shows that $P(t)$ reaches infinity in **finite time** $T = \frac{1}{ck P_0^c}$.
* **Doomsday or Extinction (Problem 22, p. 101)**:
  $$
  \frac{dP}{dt} = P(bP - a) \implies f(P) = bP - a
  $$
  Unlike the logistic model (which has a stable attractor at $K$), this phase portrait has an **unstable critical point** at $P = \frac{a}{b}$:
  * If initial population $P_0 < \frac{a}{b} \implies P(t) \to 0$ (extinction).
  * If initial population $P_0 > \frac{a}{b} \implies P(t) \to \infty$ in finite time (doomsday).
* **Empirical Regression for $f(P)$ (Project Problem 23, p. 101–102)**:
  Approximates the left-hand side per capita rate using historical census intervals:
  $$
  Q(t) = \frac{1}{P(t)}\frac{P(t + h) - P(t)}{h} \approx \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt} = f(P)
  $$
  Fitting a linear regression line $Q = a - bP$ provides the empirical coefficients for the logistic equation.

# 5. Chemical Reactions: Second-Order Kinetics

Nonlinear models also describe chemical reactions governed by the **Law of Mass Action**: the rate of a reaction is proportional to the product of the untransformed reactant concentrations.

Suppose chemicals $A$ and $B$ combine to form $C$. If $a$ and $b$ grams of $A$ and $B$ are initially present, and $X(t)$ is the amount of $C$ produced at time $t$:
$$
\frac{dX}{dt} = k (\alpha - X)(\beta - X) \qquad \tag{7}
$$
where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ represent the stoichiometric maximum amounts of compound $C$ that could be formed from the initial quantities of $A$ and $B$, respectively.

Like the logistic equation, $(7)$ is solved by separation of variables using partial fractions:
$$
\frac{dX}{(\alpha - X)(\beta - X)} = k\, dt
$$
producing asymptotic saturation toward the limiting amount $\min(\alpha, \beta)$ of chemical product formed.
