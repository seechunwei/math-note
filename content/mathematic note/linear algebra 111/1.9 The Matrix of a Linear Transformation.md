
How to determine the standard matrix $A$ for $T(x)=Ax$. The key to finding $A$ is to observe that $T$ is completely determined by what it does to the columns of the $n\times n$ identity matrix $I_{n}$.

==Theorem==
Let $T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}$ be a linear transformation. Then there exist a unique matrix $A$ such that

$$
T(x)=Ax \text{ for all x in }\mathbb{R}^{n}
$$
In fact $A$ is the $m\times n$ matrix whose $j$ th column is the vector $T(e_{j})$, where $e_{j}$ is the $j$ th column of the identity matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$:

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
T(e_{1})&\dots& T(e_{n})
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Proof:

Write $x=I_{n}x=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&\dots&e_{n}\end{bmatrix}x=x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}$.

Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
T(x)&=T(x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}) \\
&=x_{1}T(e_{1})+\dots+x_{n}T(e_{n}) \\
&=\begin{bmatrix}
T(e_{1})&\dots&T(e_{n}) 
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} \\
\dots \\
x_{n}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=Ax
\end{align}
$$

==Remark== ^123
It is a intuitively way to think $A$ is a vector obtained by what it does to the column of identity matrix 
$e_{1}$ is 1 unit toward one of the axes 
$e_{2},\dots,e_{n}$ are the same. Thus, the $x$ act like a input in all axes, and it is a linear combination of $A$ and entries of $x$ is the weight. ^0b3e70

#### **Step A: Decomposition**

$$\mathbf{x} = x_1 e_1 + x_2 e_2 + \dots + x_n e_n$$

**Intuition:** You are taking a complex vector and breaking it back down into its "atomic" parts. You’re saying, "This vector is just a recipe of $x_n$ amounts of the standard directions."

#### **Step B: The Linearity "Jump"**

$$T(\mathbf{x}) = x_1 T(e_1) + x_2 T(e_2) + \dots + x_n T(e_n)$$

**Intuition:** This is the most important leap. Because $T$ is linear, the transformation "distributes" over the sum. You don't have to transform the whole vector at once. You can transform the unit vectors _individually_ and then scale them by the original coordinates.

#### **Step C: The Matrix as a "Lookup Table"**

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} T(e_1) & T(e_2) & \dots & T(e_n) \end{bmatrix}$$

**Intuition:** Now we just pack those results into a box. Each column of the matrix $A$ is literally just the "landing spot" of a unit vector.

- Column 1: Where does $(1, 0, \dots)$ go?
    
- Column 2: Where does $(0, 1, \dots)$ go?


Any vector $\mathbf{x}$ is just a set of instructions: "Go $x_1$ steps along $e_1$, then $x_2$ steps along $e_2$, and so on."

### **3. The "Bridge": They are actually the same!**

Here is the piece that connects them: An elementary matrix **is** the standard matrix of a linear transformation.

Take the row operation: **"Multiply Row 2 by 5."**

- **The Row Way:** Apply this to $I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ to get $E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.
    
- **The Column Way ($T$):** Define a transformation $T$ that scales the vertical component of any vector by 5.
    
    - $T(\mathbf{e}_1) = T\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (The first column)
        
    - $T(\mathbf{e}_2) = T\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$ (The second column)
        
    - Put them together: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.
        

The result is identical. The "row way" of finding $E$ is just a mental shortcut because we are usually using $E$ to fix rows, but $E$ still obeys the "column" rules of the standard matrix.

### The existence and uniqueness question

The onto(existence) is focus on for every $b$ there exist at least one $x$ such that $Ax=b$. Thus, it is determine by the spanning set of column of $A$. Thus, every row of $A$ must have pivot in order to span $\mathbb{R}^{m}$. (must have m pivot)

Notice that pivot is the rank and rank determine how many linearly independent vectors. To span $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ we must have at least $m$ such vectors.

==Definition==
$A$ mapping $T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}$ is said to be onto $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ if each $b\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ is the image of at least one $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$.

Equivalently, $T$ is onto $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ when the range of $T$ is the whole codomain $\mathbb{R}^{m}$.


The one-to-one(uniqueness) is focus on the equation $T(x)=0$ has only trivial solution.
It is equivalent to asking if the column of $A$ are linearly independent. This implies that $Ax=0$ has only trivial solution. Hence, each of column of $A$ must has pivot. (compare to the number of variable)


==Theorem==
Let $T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}$ be a linear transformation. Then, $T$ is one-to-one iff the equation $T(x)=0$ has only the trivial solution.


==Theorem==
Let $T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}$ be a linear transformation, and let $A$ be the standard matrix for $T$. Then:

a. $T$ maps $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ onto $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ iff the columns of $A$ span $\mathbb{R}^{m}$
b. $T$ is one-to-one iff the columns of $A$ are linearly independent.

### Operation of linear transformations

Definition
Let $T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}$, $S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}$ and $U:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to \mathbb{R}^{k}$ .Then the sum, difference, scalar multiplication with $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ and composition of linear transformations are defined as follows:

a) $T+S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}$

$$
(T+S)(x)=T(x)+S(x)
$$
$x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. Proof [[2.1 Matrix Operations#^1f5470]]

b) $T-S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}:$

$$
(T-S)(x)=T(x)-S(x)
$$
c) $\alpha T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}:$

$$
(\alpha T)(x)=\alpha T(x)
$$
d) $U\circ T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{k}$

$$
(U\circ T)(x)=U(T(x))
$$


Are the sum, difference, scalar multiple and composite of linear transformations also linear transformations?

Yes because each of the operation can be represented by a standard matrix.

1) $(A+B)x$
2) $(A-B)x$
3) $\alpha Ax$
4) $(CA)x$

To show that a transformation $T$ is linear, you must prove that it satisfies two fundamental properties for all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ and any scalar $c$:

1. **Additivity:** $T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$
    
2. **Homogeneity (Scalar Multiplication):** $T(c\mathbf{u}) = cT(\mathbf{u})$
    

Here is the step-by-step verification for your specific transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ defined by:

$$T\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x - 3y \\ -2y \\ 4x + 2y \end{pmatrix}$$

---

### **1. Verify Additivity**

Let $\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix}$.

**First, find $T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})$:**

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + x_2 \\ y_1 + y_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} (x_1 + x_2) - 3(y_1 + y_2) \\ -2(y_1 + y_2) \\ 4(x_1 + x_2) + 2(y_1 + y_2) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + x_2 - 3y_1 - 3y_2 \\ -2y_1 - 2y_2 \\ 4x_1 + 4x_2 + 2y_1 + 2y_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Now, find $T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$:**

$$T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 - 3y_1 \\ -2y_1 \\ 4x_1 + 2y_1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} x_2 - 3y_2 \\ -2y_2 \\ 4x_2 + 2y_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 - 3y_1 + x_2 - 3y_2 \\ -2y_1 - 2y_2 \\ 4x_1 + 2y_1 + 4x_2 + 2y_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

By comparing the components, we see that $T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$.

---

### **2. Verify Homogeneity**

Let $c$ be a scalar and $\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$.

**First, find $T(c\mathbf{u})$:**

$$c\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} cx \\ cy \end{pmatrix}$$

$$T(c\mathbf{u}) = \begin{pmatrix} (cx) - 3(cy) \\ -2(cy) \\ 4(cx) + 2(cy) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c(x - 3y) \\ c(-2y) \\ c(4x + 2y) \end{pmatrix}$$

**Now, find $cT(\mathbf{u})$:**

$$cT(\mathbf{u}) = c \begin{pmatrix} x - 3y \\ -2y \\ 4x + 2y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c(x - 3y) \\ c(-2y) \\ c(4x + 2y) \end{pmatrix}$$

Again, the results match perfectly: $T(c\mathbf{u}) = cT(\mathbf{u})$.
