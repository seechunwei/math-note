# 2.1.1 Direction Fields

In this chapter we shall see other property of solutions:
How does a solution behave near a certain point?
How does a solution behave as $x\to \infty$?

We start we a simple concept from calculus:
**A derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$ of a differentiable function $y=y(x)$ gives slopes of tangent lines at points on its graph**

A solution $y=y(x)$ of a first-order differential equation
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y) &&(1)
\end{align}
$$
is necessarily a differentiable function on its interval $I$ of definition, and thus it is continuous on $I$.

Thus, the solution curve on $I$ have no breaks and must possess a tangent line at each point $(x,y(x))$. The function $f$ in the normal form $(1)$ is called the **slope function**.

Thus, the value of $f(x,y(x))$ represent the slop of the tangent line at point $(x,y(x))$. We envision it, a line segment called **linear element**.(It is the arrow in direction field)

![[Pasted image 20261011180444.png]]

## Direction Field

> [!definition]
> If we evaluate $f$ over a rectangular grid of points in the $xy-$plane and draw a line element at each point $(x,y)$ of the grid with slope $f(x,y)$, then the collection of all these line elements is called a **direction field** or a **slop field** of the differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y)$. 

It suggest the shape of a family of solution curves of the DE. A single solution curve must follow the flow pattern of the field.

![[Pasted image 20261011181312.png]]

### Example 1
DE: $\frac{dy}{dx}=0.2xy$. The direction field of the DE is shown in below
![[Pasted image 20261011181432.png]]

Any point along the $x-$axis and $y-$axis, the slope are $f(x,0)=0$ and $f(0,y)=0$ respectively, so the linear elements are horizontal.

First Quadrant:
Fix $y$: $x$ increase $\implies$ $f(x,y)=0.2xy$ increase. It means that $f'(x,y)> 0$. 

Fix $x$: The same thing happen. 
Thus, as both $x$ and $y$ increase, the linear element become more vertical and have positive slope.

Second Quadrant:
$|f(x,y)|$ increase as $|x|$ and y increase (see from right to left), so the linear element become almost vertical with negative slope.

Third and fourth quadrant:
$f(x,y)=0.2xy>0$ and $f(x,y)=0.2xy<0$ respectively.

### Increasing/Decreasing
If $\frac{dy}{dx}>0$ (or $\frac{dy}{dx}<0$) for all $x\in I$, then a differentiable function $y=y(x)$ is increasing (decreasing) on $I$.

# Autonomous First-Order Des

An ordinary differential equation in which the independent variable does not appear explicitly is said to be autonomous.

An autonomous first-order differential equation can be written as $f(y,y')$ or in normal form as

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=f(y)
$$

Example

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=1+y^{2}\text{ and } \frac{dy}{dx}=0.2xy
$$
are autonomous and nontumorous, respectively.

Many differential equations encountered in applications or equations that are models of physical laws that do not change over time are autonomous.

#### Defining Characteristics
- **Time-Translation Invariance**. If $y(t)$ is a solution, then $y(t - t_0)$ is also a solution for any constant shift $t_0 \in \mathbb{R}$. Delaying the starting time simply shifts the trajectory horizontally in the $(t, y)$-plane without altering its shape.
- **Horizontal Uniformity in Slope Fields:** At any fixed value $y = c$, the slope $y'(t) = f(c)$ is constant for all $t$. Isoclines corresponding to constant slopes are always horizontal lines.

##### Why Are Isoclines Always Horizontal Lines?

> [!definition] Isocline
> An **isocline** (*iso* = same, *cline* = slope) is a curve along which all tangent line segments have the exact same constant slope $k$:
> $$
> \frac{dy}{dt} = k \quad \left(\text{or } \frac{dy}{dx} = k\right)
> $$

1. **Why they are horizontal for autonomous DEs:**
   In an autonomous equation $\frac{dy}{dt} = f(y)$, the independent variable does not appear on the right-hand side. Setting the slope to a constant $k$:
   $$
   f(y) = k \implies y = c
   $$
   Solving for $y$ yields a constant height $y = c$. Because $t$ (or $x$) is completely absent from the formula, $t$ can be any value. In the coordinate plane, the equation $y = c$ is by definition a **horizontal straight line**.

2. **Concrete Example:**
   * **Autonomous:** $\frac{dy}{dt} = 2y$.
     To find where the slope is $6$, set $2y = 6 \implies y = 3$. 
     Along the horizontal line $y = 3$, every single point $(t, 3)$ has slope $2(3) = 6$, regardless of $t$.
   * **Non-Autonomous (Contrast):** $\frac{dy}{dt} = t + y$.
     To find where the slope is $6$, set $t + y = 6 \implies y = -t + 6$.
     Here the isocline is a **slanted line**, because $t$ and $y$ must vary together to maintain the constant sum of $6$.

3. **Connection to Equilibrium Solutions:**
   When the target slope is $k = 0$:
   $$
   f(y) = 0 \implies y = c
   $$
   The isocline $y = c$ has slope $0$ everywhere. Since a horizontal line has slope $0$, the line $y(t) = c$ itself is tangent to the direction field at every point, making it an **equilibrium solution** (constant solution).

- **Separability:** Every first-order scalar autonomous ODE is separable:
    
    $$\int \frac{1}{f(y)} \, dy = \int dt = t + C$$
    
- **Non-Intersecting Trajectories:** Assuming $f$ is continuously differentiable ($C^1$), the Picard–Lindelöf theorem guarantees unique solutions through any initial condition $(t_0, y_0)$. Consequently, distinct solution curves can never intersect, touch, or cross each other.

### Qualitative Analysis & The 1D Phase Line

Consider the classic autonomous model, the **Logistic Differential Equation**:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP) = f(P) \quad (a, b > 0)
$$
Here, $t$ (time) and $P$ (population) play the roles of the independent and dependent variables. Because $t$ does not appear explicitly on the right-hand side, the equation is autonomous.

#### 1. Critical Points & Equilibrium Solutions
Setting $f(P) = 0$:
$$
P(a - bP) = 0 \implies P = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad P = \frac{a}{b}
$$
These values are the **critical points** of the differential equation, corresponding to two constant **equilibrium solutions**:
$$
P(t) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad P(t) = \frac{a}{b}
$$

#### 2. The 1D Phase Line
Placing these critical points on a vertical $P$-axis divides the line into three distinct intervals:
1. $(-\infty, 0)$, or $P < 0$
2. $\left(0, \frac{a}{b}\right)$, or $0 < P < \frac{a}{b}$
3. $\left(\frac{a}{b}, \infty\right)$, or $P > \frac{a}{b}$

![[Pasted image 20261011202444.png]]

#### 3. Sign Analysis & Solution Behavior
The algebraic sign of $f(P) = P(a - bP)$ determines whether a solution $P(t)$ is increasing or decreasing on each interval:

| Interval | Test / Factors | Sign of $\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP)$ | Arrow Direction | Behavior of $P(t)$ |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **$P > \frac{a}{b}$** | $P > 0, \; (a - bP) < 0$ | $(+)(-) = \mathbf{-}$ | **Down ($\downarrow$)** | **Decreasing:** Overpopulation causes $P(t)$ to decline asymptotically toward $\frac{a}{b}$. |
| **$0 < P < \frac{a}{b}$** | $P > 0, \; (a - bP) > 0$ | $(+)(+) = \mathbf{+}$ | **Up ($\uparrow$)** | **Increasing:** Population grows toward the carrying capacity $\frac{a}{b}$. |
| **$P < 0$** | $P < 0, \; (a - bP) > 0$ | $(-)(+) = \mathbf{-}$ | **Down ($\downarrow$)** | **Decreasing:** Population drops toward $-\infty$ (non-physical). |

#### 4. Stability Classification & Asymptotic Behavior

From the direction arrows on the phase line:
* **Approaching $P = \frac{a}{b}$:** Arrows from both above and below point toward $P = \frac{a}{b}$. For any initial population $P_0 > 0$, $\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \frac{a}{b}$.
* **Departing from $P = 0$:** Solutions on both sides flow away from $0$.
* **Trapped Monotonic Solutions:** By the **Non-Intersecting Trajectories** property, a solution starting at $0 < P(0) < \frac{a}{b}$ can never reach or cross $P = 0$ or $P = \frac{a}{b}$. Because $\frac{dP}{dt} > 0$ throughout this interval, $P(t)$ is **strictly increasing**, generating an S-shaped logistic curve.

> [!tip] Deep Dive: Formal Classification of Critical Points
> In this example, $P = \frac{a}{b}$ is an **attractor** (stable sink) and $P = 0$ is a **repeller** (unstable source).  
> For the complete formal classification of all 3 equilibrium types (including **semi-stable** points) and the shortcut derivative test $f'(c)$, see [[#Classification of Critical Points: Attractors, Repellers, and Semi-Stable|Section 7: Classification of Critical Points: Attractors, Repellers, and Semi-Stable]].

### Behavior of Nonconstant Solutions

In an autonomous equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(y)$, solutions fall into two categories:
1. **Constant Solutions (Equilibria):** The horizontal lines $y(x) = c$ where $f(c) = 0$.
2. **Nonconstant Solutions:** Any solution curve $y(x)$ with an initial condition $y(x_0) \neq c$.

#### The Horizontal Strip and Uniqueness

In Theorem 1.2.1, existence and uniqueness require $f$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ to be continuous on a region $R$:
* **In a general DE $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$:** Because the equation involves both $x$ and $y$, $R$ is typically a finite rectangle: $a \le x \le b, \; c \le y \le d$.
* **In an autonomous DE $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(y)$:** The independent variable $x$ is completely absent!  
  If $f(y)$ and $f'(y)$ are continuous on an interval $I$ on the $y$-axis, then the conditions hold for **all $x \in (-\infty, \infty)$**.  
  Geometrically, the region $R$ expands into an **infinitely long horizontal strip**:
  $$
  R = \{(x, y) \mid -\infty < x < \infty, \; y \in I\}
  $$

Because Theorem 1.2.1 holds throughout this entire horizontal strip, **through any point $(x_0, y_0)$ in $R$, there passes only ONE solution curve**.

![[Pasted image 20261011205043.png]]
#### Partitioning into Subregions
If the equation possesses consecutive critical points $c_1 < c_2$, the equilibrium solutions $y(x) = c_1$ and $y(x) = c_2$ are horizontal lines that partition the strip $R$ into three horizontal subregions:
* $R_1$: $y < c_1$
* $R_2$: $c_1 < y < c_2$
* $R_3$: $y > c_2$

![[Pasted image 20261011203332.png]]

Every **nonconstant solution** $y(x)$ is governed by three fundamental geometric rules:

#### 1. Trapped in its Subregion (No Crossing)
* By the **Uniqueness Theorem (Theorem 1.2.1)**, distinct solution curves can never intersect, touch, or cross each other.
* Because the boundaries $y = c_1$ and $y = c_2$ are themselves valid equilibrium solution curves, they act as impenetrable barriers.
* A nonconstant solution starting in subregion $R_i$ **remains in $R_i$ for all $x$**.  
  For example, in $R_2$: $c_1 < y(x) < c_2$ for all $x \in (-\infty, \infty)$.

> [!question] The theorem only guarantees uniqueness on a local interval $(x_0 - h, x_0 + h)$. Why does this prevent crossing everywhere?
> 1. **Local uniqueness is already enough to prevent touching (The Touchpoint Contradiction):**  
>    To cross the boundary line $y = c_1$, the curve $y(x)$ must first **touch** it at some point $(x^*, c_1)$. At that touchpoint, two solutions would pass through $(x^*, c_1)$: the curve $y(x)$ and the flat equilibrium line $y = c_1$.  
>    By the local uniqueness theorem, only *one* solution can pass through $(x^*, c_1)$ on $(x^* - h, x^* + h)$. Having two curves through the same point is an immediate contradiction. Therefore, the curve can never even touch the boundary, making crossing physically impossible!
> 
> 2. **Why the solution exists for ALL $x \in (-\infty, \infty)$ in $R_2$ (Global Continuation):** (The theorem only state there exist some interval) 
>    In general DEs, solutions can blow up to $\pm\infty$ in finite time (e.g., $y = \frac{1}{1-x}$). But in $R_2$, $y(x)$ is trapped between $c_1$ and $c_2$, so it can **never blow up to $\pm\infty$**. Because $y$ is bounded and $f(y)$ is continuous, the solution never breaks down. We can repeatedly extend the local interval indefinitely, guaranteeing the solution exists for all $x \in (-\infty, \infty)$.

#### 2. Strictly Monotonic (No Turning Around)
* The slope is given by $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(y)$.
* Inside any subregion between consecutive critical points, $f(y) \neq 0$. By continuity, $f(y)$ cannot change sign without passing through zero. (if it pass zero then it there exist a constant solution in that region which contradict the assumption)
* Therefore, $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is **strictly positive** or **strictly negative** throughout the entire subregion:
  * $y(x)$ is **strictly increasing** everywhere in $R_i$, OR
  * $y(x)$ is **strictly decreasing** everywhere in $R_i$.
* **Direct Consequences:**
  * **No local extrema:** $y(x)$ cannot have a relative maximum or minimum (a peak or valley requires $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$, which only exists on the boundary equilibria).
  * **No oscillations:** $y(x)$ cannot wave up and down.

#### 3. Bounded Monotonicity Forces Horizontal Asymptotes
* By the **Monotone Convergence Theorem**, any function that is both **bounded** and **strictly monotonic** must level off and converge to a limit as $x \to \pm\infty$.
* **In a bounded subregion ($R_2$):** Since $c_1 < y(x) < c_2$ and $y(x)$ is strictly monotonic, the graph must asymptotically approach the equilibrium lines: one as $x \to \infty$, and the other as $x \to -\infty$.
* **In an unbounded subregion ($R_1$ or $R_3$):** The curve approaches the boundary equilibrium line in one direction ($x \to \infty$ or $x \to -\infty$) and diverges toward $\pm\infty$ in the other direction.

| Property of Nonconstant Solution $y(x)$ | Can it happen? | Mathematical Reason |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Cross an equilibrium line?** | ❌ **No** | Violates Uniqueness (Picard–Lindelöf). |
| **Have a local peak or valley (extrema)?** | ❌ **No** | $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$ only exists on boundary equilibria. |
| **Oscillate / wave?** | ❌ **No** | $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(y)$ cannot change sign within a subregion. |
| **Flatten into horizontal asymptotes?** | ✅ **Yes** | Bounded + monotonic forces asymptotic approach to the critical lines. |
| **Shift horizontally by $k$ ($y(x-k)$)?** | ✅ **Yes** | Translation property: $f(y)$ has no $x$, so shifted curve is also a solution. |

#### 4. Translation Property (Time-Invariance)
> [!property] Translation Property of Autonomous DEs
> If $y(x)$ is a solution of an autonomous differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(y)$, then for any constant $k$:
> $$
> y_1(x) = y(x - k)
> $$
> is also a valid solution.

1. **Why It Works (Calculus Proof):**
   By the chain rule:
   $$
   \frac{d}{dx}[y(x - k)] = y'(x - k) \cdot 1 = f\big(y(x - k)\big)
   $$
   Because the formula $f(y)$ does not contain $x$, shifting the $x$-coordinate by $k$ does not change the slope function.

2. **Geometric Meaning (Slope Field):**
   In an autonomous ODE, slopes depend only on vertical height $y$. Every horizontal row of slope marks is identical. Sliding any solution curve horizontally left or right keeps every point at its original height, so it still matches the slope field perfectly.

3. **Behavior Across the 3 Subregions:**
   * **Constant solutions don't move:** A flat line $y(x) = c$ shifted horizontally is $y(x - k) = c$—it simply slides along itself and stays the exact same boundary line.
   * **Curves stay trapped in their subregion:** Shifting horizontally ($x \to x - k$) changes the $x$-position, but **never changes the $y$-height**. A curve in $R_2$ remains in $R_2$.
   * **One "Mother Curve" per subregion:** In any given subregion (such as $0 < y < c$), all solution curves have the **exact same shape**—they are just horizontal shifts of a single curve across time.

### Connecting the 1D Phase Line to Solution Curves in the $tP$-Plane

Now, we take the 1D phase line on the vertical $P$-axis and expand it across time ($-\infty < t < \infty$) into the 2D $tP$-plane. The three intervals on the phase line correspond to three horizontal subregions:
* **$R_3$:** $\frac{a}{b} < P < \infty$
* **$R_2$:** $0 < P < \frac{a}{b}$
* **$R_1$:** $-\infty < P < 0$

The direction arrows on the 1D phase line determine the exact behavior of solution curves for any initial value $P(0) = P_0$:
![[Pasted image 20261011211357.png]]
#### (i) Subregion $R_1$ ($P_0 < 0$)
* **Boundedness:** $P(t)$ is bounded above by $0$ ($P(t) < 0$).
* **Monotonicity:** $\frac{dP}{dt} < 0$, so $P(t)$ is strictly decreasing.
* **Asymptotic Behavior:** 
  * As $t$ increases ($t \to \infty$), $P(t)$ plunges downward toward $-\infty$.
  * As $t \to -\infty$ (tracing backwards into the past), $P(t)$ must rise and level off:
    $$
    \lim_{t \to -\infty} P(t) = 0
    $$
  * Thus, the horizontal line $P(t) = 0$ (the negative $t$-axis) is a **horizontal asymptote** for solution curves in $R_1$ as $t \to -\infty$.

#### (ii) Subregion $R_2$ ($0 < P_0 < \frac{a}{b}$)
* **Boundedness:** $P(t)$ is bounded both below by $0$ and above by $\frac{a}{b}$:
  $$
  0 < P(t) < \frac{a}{b} \quad \text{for all } t \in (-\infty, \infty)
  $$
* **Monotonicity:** $\frac{dP}{dt} > 0$, so $P(t)$ is strictly increasing.
* **Asymptotic Behavior:** 
  * As $t \to +\infty$ (into the future), $P(t)$ climbs and flattens against the carrying capacity:
    $$
    \lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \frac{a}{b}
    $$
  * As $t \to -\infty$ (into the past), $P(t)$ drops and flattens against zero:
    $$
    \lim_{t \to -\infty} P(t) = 0
    $$
  * Both equilibrium lines, $P(t) = 0$ and $P(t) = \frac{a}{b}$, are **horizontal asymptotes** for every solution curve in this region. This produces the characteristic **S-shaped logistic growth curve**.

#### (iii) Subregion $R_3$ ($P_0 > \frac{a}{b}$)
* **Boundedness:** $P(t)$ is bounded below by $\frac{a}{b}$ ($P(t) > \frac{a}{b}$).
* **Monotonicity:** $\frac{dP}{dt} < 0$, so $P(t)$ is strictly decreasing.
* **Asymptotic Behavior:** 
  * As $t \to +\infty$, the overpopulated system decreases and flattens against the carrying capacity:
    $$
    \lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \frac{a}{b}
    $$
  * Thus, the equilibrium line $P(t) = \frac{a}{b}$ is a **horizontal asymptote** as $t \to \infty$.

---

#### Summary: What "Horizontal Asymptote" Means for a Solution Curve

In this context, saying an equilibrium solution $P = L$ is a **horizontal asymptote** means:
> As time goes to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$, the solution curve glides **infinitely close** to the horizontal line $P = L$ without ever touching or crossing it ($\lim_{t \to \pm\infty} P(t) = L$).

| Subregion | Initial Condition $P_0$ | Monotonicity | Behavior as $t \to +\infty$ (Future) | Behavior as $t \to -\infty$ (Past) | Horizontal Asymptotes |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$R_3$** | $P_0 > \frac{a}{b}$ | Decreasing ($\downarrow$) | Levels off $\to \frac{a}{b}$ | Shoots up to $+\infty$ | $P = \frac{a}{b}$ (as $t \to +\infty$) |
| **$R_2$** | $0 < P_0 < \frac{a}{b}$ | Increasing ($\uparrow$) | Levels off $\to \frac{a}{b}$ | Levels off $\to 0$ | **Both** $P = \frac{a}{b}$ ($t \to +\infty$) and $P = 0$ ($t \to -\infty$) |
| **$R_1$** | $P_0 < 0$ | Decreasing ($\downarrow$) | Plunges to $-\infty$ | Levels off $\to 0$ | $P = 0$ (as $t \to -\infty$) |

> [!tip] Dual Identity of Equilibrium Lines
> An equilibrium line $y = c$ (where $f(c) = 0$) wears **two hats at the same time**:
> 1. **From its own perspective:** It is an actual, legitimate **constant solution** to the differential equation ($y(x) = c$ for all $x$).
> 2. **From the perspective of other solutions:** It acts as the **horizontal asymptote** for neighboring nonconstant solutions ($y(x) \to c$ as $x \to \pm\infty$).
> 
> **Why must asymptotes be equilibrium lines?**  
> For any curve to flatten horizontally as $x \to \pm\infty$, its slope must level off to zero: $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{dy}{dx} = 0$. In an autonomous equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(y)$, the slope can *only* equal zero when $f(y) = 0$. Therefore, **nonconstant solutions can only ever flatten out towards constant solutions**—the equilibrium lines are the *only* horizontal asymptotes that can possibly exist in an autonomous system!

### Finite-Time Blow-Up vs. Infinite Time (Vertical Asymptotes)

> [!warning] Common Misconception: "Does plunging to $-\infty$ take until $t = \infty$?"
> **No!** When an unbounded solution decreases toward $-\infty$ (as in $R_1$), do **not** assume $P(t) \to -\infty$ as $t \to \infty$.  
> Nonlinear differential equations can accelerate so rapidly that the solution plunges to $-\infty$ in a **finite amount of time** $t = T > 0$!

#### 1. Dynamic & Graphical Meaning
* **In dynamic terms:** The system **"blows up"** in finite time.
* **In graphical terms:** The solution curve has a **vertical asymptote** at $t = T$. The solution literally ceases to exist past time $T$!

#### 2. Why Does This Happen in Region $R_1$ ($P_0 < 0$)?
In the logistic equation:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP) = aP - bP^2
$$
When $P$ is negative, the quadratic term $-bP^2$ dominates:
$$
\frac{dP}{dt} \approx -b P^2 \quad (\text{quadratic downward acceleration})
$$
Just like $\frac{dy}{dt} = y^2$ blows up to $+\infty$ at finite time $t = 1$ (since $y = \frac{1}{1-t}$), the $-bP^2$ term causes $P(t)$ to plunge to $-\infty$ at a finite time $T > 0$ depending on the initial condition $P_0$:
$$
\lim_{t \to T^-} P(t) = -\infty \quad \text{for some finite } T > 0
$$

#### 3. What About Region $R_3$ ($P_0 > \frac{a}{b}$)?
* **Forward in time ($t > 0$):** $P(t)$ decreases safely toward the horizontal asymptote $P = \frac{a}{b}$ as $t \to \infty$. It does *not* blow up going forward.
* **Backward in time ($t < 0$):** Tracing the curve backwards into the past, $P(t)$ was larger and larger. The $-bP^2$ term causes $P(t)$ to shoot up to $+\infty$ at a **finite negative time** $t = -T < 0$ (a vertical asymptote in the past).

#### Comparison Across Subregions

| Subregion | Boundedness | Finite-Time Blow-Up / Vertical Asymptote? | Lifespan of Solution $t$ |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **$R_2$** ($0 < P < \frac{a}{b}$) | **Bounded** between $0$ and $\frac{a}{b}$ | ❌ **Never** (No vertical asymptotes) | Exists globally for all $t \in (-\infty, \infty)$ |
| **$R_1$** ($P < 0$) | **Unbounded below** | ⚠️ **Yes: as $t \to T > 0$** (Future vertical asymptote) | Exists only on $(-\infty, T)$ |
| **$R_3$** ($P > \frac{a}{b}$) | **Unbounded above** | ⚠️ **Yes: as $t \to -T < 0$** (Past vertical asymptote) | Exists only on $(-T, \infty)$ |

#### Simple Example to See "Finite-Time Blow-Up"

Consider the simple differential equation:
$$
\frac{dy}{dt} = y^2, \quad y(0) = 1
$$

Solving by separation of variables:
$$
\int \frac{dy}{y^2} = \int dt \implies -\frac{1}{y} = t - 1 \implies y(t) = \frac{1}{1 - t}
$$

Look at what happens as time ticks forward from $t = 0$:
* At $t = 0$: $y = 1$
* At $t = 0.5$: $y = 2$
* At $t = 0.9$: $y = 10$
* At $t = 0.999$: $y = 1000$
* **As $t \to 1^-$:** $y(t) \to +\infty$!

The curve doesn't wait until $t = \infty$ to blow up—it shoots to $+\infty$ at the exact finite clock time **$t = 1$**!  
The line $t = 1$ is a **vertical asymptote**, and the solution literally ceases to exist past $t = 1$.

### Classification of Critical Points: Attractors, Repellers, and Semi-Stable

In an autonomous differential equation $\frac{dy}{dt} = f(y)$, a critical point $c$ where $f(c) = 0$ represents an equilibrium state. Depending on the direction of neighboring solution curves as $t \to \infty$, critical points are classified into three types:

![[Pasted image 20261011214537.png]]
#### 1. Attractor (Asymptotically Stable)
* **Arrow Pattern:** Arrows from both above and below point **TOWARD** $c$ ($\to \bullet \leftarrow$).
* **Definition:** Solutions starting on either side approach $c$ as $t \to \infty$:
  $$
  \lim_{t \to \infty} y(t) = c
  $$
* **Intuition (Marble in a bowl):** If you disturb the system slightly away from $c$, it returns to $c$.
* **Example:** In the logistic model $\frac{dP}{dt} = P(a - bP)$, the carrying capacity $P = \frac{a}{b}$ is an attractor.

#### 2. Repeller (Unstable)
* **Arrow Pattern:** Arrows on both sides point **AWAY** from $c$ ($\leftarrow \circ \to$).
* **Definition:** Any solution starting at $y_0 \neq c$ moves away from $c$ as $t$ increases.
* **Intuition (Marble on a hill):** If you nudge the system even slightly away from $c$, it rolls away and never comes back.
* **Example:** In the logistic model, extinction $P = 0$ is a repeller.

#### 3. Semi-Stable (One-Sided Attractor)
* **Arrow Pattern:** Arrows point in the same direction on both sides of $c$ ($\uparrow \bullet \uparrow$ or $\downarrow \bullet \downarrow$).
* **Definition:** Solutions on one side are attracted toward $c$, while solutions on the other side are repelled away from $c$.
* **Example:** $\frac{dy}{dt} = (y - 1)^2$.  
  Since $(y - 1)^2 > 0$ for all $y \neq 1$, arrows point UP on both sides of $c = 1$. Solutions below $1$ climb toward $1$ (attracted), while solutions above $1$ climb away toward $+\infty$ (repelled).

---

#### The Derivative Test for Stability: $f'(c)$

Instead of testing signs of $f(y)$ on intervals, you can instantly determine the stability of a critical point $c$ using the derivative $f'(c) = \left.\frac{df}{dy}\right|_{y=c}$:

| Value of $f'(c)$ | Classification | Geometric / Physical Reason |
| :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **$f'(c) < 0$** | **Attractor** (Asymptotically Stable) | $f(y)$ slopes downward through zero: positive below $c$ ($\uparrow$) and negative above $c$ ($\downarrow$). |
| **$f'(c) > 0$** | **Repeller** (Unstable) | $f(y)$ slopes upward through zero: negative below $c$ ($\downarrow$) and positive above $c$ ($\uparrow$). |
| **$f'(c) = 0$** | **Inconclusive** (Often Semi-Stable) | $f(y)$ touches zero without crossing (e.g., repeated root $(y - c)^2$), keeping the same sign on both sides. |

##### Why Does This Test Work? (The Connection to Calculus)

Consider $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2 - y$, where the critical point is $c = 2$ ($f(2) = 0$):

* **Below $2$ (e.g., $y = 1$):** $f(1) = 2 - 1 = \mathbf{+1} > 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} > 0 \implies y$ climbs **UP** ($\uparrow$) toward $2$.
* **Above $2$ (e.g., $y = 3$):** $f(3) = 2 - 3 = \mathbf{-1} < 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} < 0 \implies y$ falls **DOWN** ($\downarrow$) toward $2$.

Notice the values of $f(y)$ as $y$ increases:
$$
+1 \longrightarrow 0 \longrightarrow -1
$$
The values of $f(y)$ are **decreasing (dropping)** through zero!

In calculus, a function is decreasing if its derivative is **negative**:
$$
f'(y) = \frac{d}{dy}(2 - y) = -1 < 0
$$

* **When $f'(c) < 0$:** $f(y)$ must drop from positive to negative ($+ \to 0 \to -$). This forces solutions below to climb UP and solutions above to drop DOWN ($\uparrow c \downarrow$). Both sides push into $c \implies$ **Stable Attractor (Sink)**.
* **When $f'(c) > 0$:** $f(y)$ must rise from negative to positive ($- \to 0 \to +$). Solutions below drop DOWN and solutions above climb UP ($\downarrow c \uparrow$). Both sides push away from $c \implies$ **Unstable Repeller (Source)**.

### Second Derivative $y''$ vs. Stability Derivative $f'(c)$

It is very common to confuse the roles of $y''$ and $f'(c)$. They serve two completely different mathematical purposes:

| Concept                    | Mathematical Formula                           | What It Tells You                                                                      | Where It Is Evaluated                                          |
| :------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Stability Test**         | $f'(c) = \left.\frac{df}{dy}\right\vert_{y=c}$ | Whether an equilibrium $y = c$ is an **Attractor (Stable)** or **Repeller (Unstable)** | Evaluated **only at the critical points** $c$ where $f(c) = 0$ |
| **Concavity & Inflection** | $y'' = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = f'(y) \cdot f(y)$   | How the solution curve **bends** in the $xy$-plane ($\smile$ vs. $\frown$)             | Evaluated across **subregions** between equilibria             |

#### Why Can't $y''$ Test Stability Directly?

By the Chain Rule:
$$
y'' = \frac{d}{dx}[f(y)] = f'(y) \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = f'(y) \cdot f(y)
$$

At any equilibrium solution $y = c$, we have $f(c) = 0$ by definition. Therefore:
$$
y''\Big|_{y=c} = f'(c) \cdot 0 \equiv \mathbf{0}
$$

Because $y''$ is identically zero along every horizontal equilibrium solution line, evaluating $y''$ directly at $y = c$ provides **no information** about stability.

Instead, notice how the formula splits:
$$
y'' = \underbrace{f'(y)}_{\substack{\text{Evaluated at } y=c \\ \implies \text{Stability Test}}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{f(y)}_{\substack{\text{Sign of slope } y' \\ (+ \text{ or } -)}}
$$

* The factor **$f'(c)$** governs **stability** (whether slopes get more positive or more negative as you cross $c$).
* The full product **$y'' = f'(y) \cdot f(y)$** governs the **concavity** of curves moving through the plane.

---

### The Role of $y''$ in Sketching Solution Curves

The second derivative $y''$ dictates the geometric bending of the curves:

1. **Concavity:**
   * **$y'' > 0 \implies$ Concave Up ($\smile$):** Slopes $\frac{dy}{dx}$ are increasing as $x$ moves to the right.
   * **$y'' < 0 \implies$ Concave Down ($\frown$):** Slopes $\frac{dy}{dx}$ are decreasing as $x$ moves to the right.

2. **Inflection Points ($y'' = 0$):**
   * For nonconstant solutions (where $f(y) \neq 0$), $y'' = 0$ occurs wherever:
     $$
     f'(y) = 0
     $$
   * In a quadratic or polynomial DE, the horizontal line $y = y_{\text{inflection}}$ where $f'(y) = 0$ acts as an **Inflection Line**.
   * When a solution curve crosses this line, its concavity flips from $\frown$ to $\smile$ (or vice versa), creating a smooth **S-curve**.

---

### Step-by-Step Method to Sketch Solution Curves

To sketch the family of solution curves for any autonomous differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(y)$ without solving it:

#### Step 1: Find Critical Points (Equilibrium Lines)
* Set $f(y) = 0$ and solve for roots $y = c_1, c_2, \dots$
* Draw horizontal lines $y = c_k$ across the $xy$-plane. These are the constant solutions and horizontal asymptotes.

#### Step 2: Construct the Phase Line & Determine Stability
* Find the sign of $y' = f(y)$ in each interval:
  * $f(y) > 0 \implies y$ is **increasing** ($\uparrow$)
  * $f(y) < 0 \implies y$ is **decreasing** ($\downarrow$)
* Classify each critical point using the **Derivative Test** $f'(c)$:
  * $f'(c) < 0 \implies$ **Attractor (Stable Sink)** ($\to \bullet \leftarrow$)
  * $f'(c) > 0 \implies$ **Repeller (Unstable Source)** ($\leftarrow \circ \to$)
  * $f'(c) = 0 \implies$ **Semi-Stable** or inconclusive

#### Step 3: Compute $y''$ and Locate Inflection Lines
* Calculate $y'' = f'(y) \cdot f(y)$ using the Chain Rule.
* Set $f'(y) = 0$ to find the inflection lines $y = y_{\text{inflection}}$.
* Build a sign chart for $y''$ across each subregion to determine whether curves are **Concave Up ($\smile$)** or **Concave Down ($\frown$)**.

#### Step 4: Sketch Solution Curves in Each Subregion
* **Bounded regions (between two equilibria):**
  * Curves flow from the Repeller toward the Attractor.
  * Start asymptotically near the repeller, bend across the inflection line with changing concavity ($\frown \to \smile$), and level off asymptotically into the attractor.
* **Unbounded regions (outside equilibria):**
  * Curves peel away from repellers toward $\pm\infty$ or approach attractors from $\pm\infty$, bending according to the sign of $y''$.
* **Properties to keep in mind:**
  * **Translation Invariance:** All nonconstant curves in the same subregion are horizontal shifts $y(x - k)$ of each other.
  * **Uniqueness (Picard–Lindelöf):** Curves never cross or touch the equilibrium lines, nor do they ever cross each other.

---

#### Example Walkthrough: $\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2 - 2y = y(y - 2)$

1. **Equilibria:** $f(y) = y(y-2) = 0 \implies y = 0$ and $y = 2$.
2. **Stability:**
   * $f'(y) = 2y - 2$
   * At $y = 2$: $f'(2) = +2 > 0 \implies$ **Repeller (Unstable)**.
   * At $y = 0$: $f'(0) = -2 < 0 \implies$ **Attractor (Stable)**.
3. **Concavity & Inflection:**
   * $y'' = f'(y) \cdot f(y) = 2(y - 1)y(y - 2)$.
   * Inflection level: $f'(y) = 0 \implies y = 1$.
   * Signs:
     * $y > 2$: $y' > 0, y'' > 0 \implies$ Increasing, Concave Up ($\smile$).
     * $1 < y < 2$: $y' < 0, y'' < 0 \implies$ Decreasing, Concave Down ($\frown$).
     * $0 < y < 1$: $y' < 0, y'' > 0 \implies$ Decreasing, Concave Up ($\smile$).
     * $y < 0$: $y' > 0, y'' < 0 \implies$ Increasing, Concave Down ($\frown$).
