### Determinants as Area or Volume

If $A$ is a $n\times n$ matrix, the hypervolume of the parallelepiped determined by the columns of $A$ is $|\det A|$.  

Why it is not $\det A$, because area is always positive while det might be negative, thus we need to take the absolute value of $\det A$.

How to prove??
Consider case for $2\times 2$. we know that the area of a rectangular is base multiply with height. Thus, it is suffice to show that any $2 \times 2$ matrix can be transformed into diagonal matrix.

$$
\det \begin{bmatrix}
a & 0 \\
0 & b
\end{bmatrix}=|ad|=\text{area of rectangle}
$$
Notice that for any $2\times 2$ matrix we can perform column replacement, interchange(or row replacement, interchange) to deduce it to diagonal matrix. (why because for a invertible matrix, its pivot is always on diagonal). The absolute value of determinant remained unchanged.

So it suffices to prove the following simple geometric observation that applies to vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{3}$:


> [!note]
Let $a_{1}$ and $a_{2}$ be nonzero vectors. Then for any scalar $c$, the area of the parallelogram determined by $a_{1}$ and $a_{2}$ equals the area of the parallelogram determined by $a_{1}$ and $a_{2}+ca_{1}$.


First we need to assume $a_{2}\not\in Span\{ a_{1} \}$, otherwise the area will be 0. 

Consider a matrix below

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
5 & 1 \\
1 & 4
\end{bmatrix}
$$
We try to make the first column become $\begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ how it look in geometry ? 

We suppose a line $L$ that pass though $0$ and $a_{1}$, then $a_{2}+L$ is the line through $a_{2}$ parallel to $L$. **Thus, point $a_{2}$ and $a_{2}+ca_{1}$ have the same perpendicular distance to $L$**. Thus, the area of this two parallelogram is the same.  (Notice that this is column operation, but since $\det A=\det(A^{T})$, thus it is the same for row operation)

### Linear Transformation

==Theorem==
Let $T:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to \mathbb{R}^{2}$ be the linear transformation determined by a $2\times 2$ matrix $A$. If $S$ is a parallelogram in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, then

$$
\text{area of }T(s)=|\det A|\cdot \text{ area of }S
$$
Proof:
Consider the $2\times 2$ case, with $A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1} & a_{2}\end{bmatrix}$. A parallelogram at the origin in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ determined by vectors $b_{1}$ and $b_{2}$ has the form

$$
S=\{ s_{1}b_{1}+s_{2}b_{2}:0\leq s_{1},s_{2}\leq 1 \}
$$

The image of $S$ under $T$ consists of points of the form

$$
\begin{align}
T(s_{1}b_{1}+s_{2}b_{2})&=T(s_{1}b_{1})+T(s_{2}b_{2}) \\
&=s_{1}Ab_{1}+s_{2}Ab_{2}
\end{align}
$$
It follow that $T(S)$ is the parallelogram determined by the columns of the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}Ab_{1} & Ab_{2}\end{bmatrix}$. This matrix can be written as $AB$, where $B=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1} & b_{2}\end{bmatrix}$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\text{area of }T(S)&=|\det AB| \\
&=|\det A||\det B| \\
&=|\det A|\cdot \text{ area of }S
\end{align}
$$
(Notice that the input vector in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ here is actually $\begin{bmatrix}s_{1} \\  s_{2}\end{bmatrix}$) 

An arbitrary parallelogram has the form $p+s$, where $p$ is a vector and $S$ is a parallelogram at the origin. [[3.3 Cramer's Rule#^b1b57d]]

==Remark==

A linear transformation only map a vector to a vector, a matrix to a matrix (the column are the same.).

In this case , we call this a **degenerate parallelogram**—a parallelogram with a height of zero. The logic still works flawlessly because a line segment is technically a limiting case of a parallelogram.

**Problem:**
Let $T:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to \mathbb{R}^{n}$ be a linear transformation, and let $p$ be a vector and $S$ a set in $\mathbb{R}^{m}$. Show that
$$
T(p+S) = T(p) + T(S)
$$

**Proof:**
To show that the two sets are equal, we show mutual inclusion: $T(p+S) \subseteq T(p) + T(S)$ and $T(p) + T(S) \subseteq T(p+S)$.

**Definitions of the sets:**
- $p+S = \{ p+s : s \in S \}$
- $T(p+S) = \{ T(x) : x \in p+S \}$
- $T(p) + T(S) = \{ T(p) + T(s) : s \in S \}$

---

#### 1. Forward Direction: $T(p+S) \subseteq T(p) + T(S)$
Let $y \in T(p+S)$. 

By the definition of the image of a set, there exists a vector $x \in p+S$ such that:
$$
y = T(x)
$$
Since $x \in p+S$, there exists some $s \in S$ such that $x = p+s$. Thus:
$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= T(p+s) \\
&= T(p) + T(s) \quad (\text{by linearity of } T)
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $s \in S$, it follows that $T(s) \in T(S)$. Therefore:
$$
y \in T(p) + T(S)
$$
This establishes that $T(p+S) \subseteq T(p) + T(S)$.

---

#### 2. Backward Direction: $T(p) + T(S) \subseteq T(p+S)$
Conversely, let $y \in T(p) + T(S)$. 

By definition, there exists some $s \in S$ such that:
$$
y = T(p) + T(s)
$$
By the linearity of $T$, we can combine these terms:
$$
y = T(p+s)
$$
Let $x = p+s$. Since $s \in S$, it follows that $x \in p+S$. 

Thus, $y = T(x)$ for some $x \in p+S$, which means:
$$
y \in T(p+S)
$$
This establishes that $T(p) + T(S) \subseteq T(p+S)$.

---

**Conclusion:**
Since both inclusions $T(p+S) \subseteq T(p) + T(S)$ and $T(p) + T(S) \subseteq T(p+S)$ hold, we conclude:
$$
T(p+S) = T(p) + T(S)
$$
