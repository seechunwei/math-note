
==Theorem 3==
Row Operations
Let $A$ be a square matrix

a) If a multiple of one row of $A$ is added to another row to produce a matrix $B$, then $\det B=\det A$.

b) If two rows of $A$ are interchanged to produce $B$, then $\det B=-\det A$.

c) If one row of $A$ is multiplied by $k$ to produce $B$, then $\det B=k\det A$

$$
\begin{align}
\det E_{i}(\alpha)A&=\alpha \det A \\
\det E^{i}_{j}A&=-\det A \\
\det E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)A&=\det A
\end{align}
$$

==Theorem 4==
A square matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$.

Proof:
Suppose $A$ is invertible. By theorem in 3.1, we know that determinant of $A$ is the product of diagonal of $ref(A)$ and $\det E$ that reduce $A$ to $ref(A)$.  By theorem, $A$ is row equivalent to $I$. Thus, the pivot position of $A$ are at diagonal. Therefore, $\det A \neq 0$.

Conversely suppose that $\det A \neq 0$ and assume $A$ is not invertible. Then, by the theorem above, $\det A=0$, a contradiction. Hence, $A$ is invertible.


==Corollary==
$\det A=0$ iff the column of $A$ is linearly dependent and the row of $A$ is linearly dependent.

### Column Operations

==Theorem 5==
If $A$ is an $n\times n$ matrix, then $\det A^{T}=\det A$.

Proof:
We prove by mathematical induction. Suppose $P(n)$ is defined as 

$$
\begin{align}
P(n): A=[a_{ij}]_{n\times n} \text{ and } \det A=\det A^{T}
\end{align}
$$


For basis step, it is clear that $P(1)$ is true.

For induction step, suppose $n$ is an arbitrary positive number and suppose $P(n)$ is true.

Thus, suppose $A=[a_{ij}]_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}$. Thus, the cofactor expansion across the first row of $A$ is 
$$
a_{1j}C_{1j}=a_{1j}(-1)^{1+j}\det A_{1j}
$$

Notice that $A^{T}=[a_{ji}]_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}$Now consider the cofactor expansion across the first column of $A^{T}$ denoted by

$$
a_{j 1}C_{j 1}=a_{j 1}(-1)^{j+1}\det (A^{T})_{j1}
$$

Notice that $a_{1j}(-1)^{1+j}=a_{j 1}(-1)^{j+1}$. Since $A_{1{j}}$ is a $n\times n$ matrix, by induction hypothesis it follow that

$$
\det A_{1j}=\det (A^{T})_{j 1}
$$

Hence, the cofactor expansion of $\det A$ along the first row equals the cofactor expansion of $\det A^{T}$ down the first column. That is
$$
\det A=\det(A^{T})
$$
Thus, by mathematical induction, $P(n)$ is true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

==Remark==
The above theorem tells us that column operations have the same effects on determinants as row operations.

Let $C_{i}(\alpha)$, $C^{i}_{j}$ and $C^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$ be the elementary column operations corresponding to $R_{i}(\alpha),R^{i}_{j}$ and $R^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$, respectively.

Let $F_{i}(\alpha),F^{i}_{j},F^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$ be the elementary column matrix. 

$$
\begin{align}
\det AF_{i}(\alpha)&=\alpha \det A \\
\det AF^{i}_{j}&=-\det A \\
\det AF^{i}_{j}(\alpha)&=\det A
\end{align}
$$

### A multilinearity Property of the Determinant Function

For an $n\times n$ matrix $A$, we can consider $\det A$ as a function of the $n$ column vectors in $A$.

If all column is being fixed except for one column, then $\det A$ is a linear transformation of that column.

Suppose $jth$ column of $A$ is allowed to vary. Thus, we can define a linear transformation $T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$
T(x)=\det \begin{bmatrix}
a_{1} & \dots & a_{j-1} & x & a_{j+1} & \dots & a_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then,

$$
\begin{array}
\ T(cx)=cT(x) \text{ for all scalars c and all }x \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \\
T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v) \text{ for all }u,v \in \mathbb{R}^{n}
\end{array}
$$


Since $\det A=\det A^{T}$, it follow that $\det A$ can be viewed as a linear transformation for a row of $A$.
