### Why only square matrix have inverse?
 2. The Dimension Problem

Suppose $A$ is a rectangular matrix of size $m \times n$ (where $m \neq n$).

- If you multiply $A$ ($m \times n$) by another matrix $B$ ($n \times m$) to get a "left inverse," the result $BA$ is an $n \times n$ identity matrix.
    
- If you multiply them the other way, $AB$, the result is an $m \times m$ identity matrix.
    

Because $m \neq n$, these two identity matrices are different sizes. In the world of formal inverses, $A^{-1}$ must be a single, unique matrix that works on both sides. Rectangular matrices fail this "one-size-fits-all" requirement.

### The Calculation of $A^{-1}$: The Gauss-Jordan Method

Consider the equation $AA^{-1}=I$. Each column of $I$ can be view as $e_{i}$ where $e_{i}$ is a vector with $i-$entry equal to 1 and other is 0.

Where $x_{i}$ is the column vector of $A^{-1}$. Thus, we solve all system of equation in one shot by reduce $A$ to reduced echelon form or identity matrix(if it is non singular). This is called the Gauss-Jordan method.

#### Symmetric Matrices

A symmetric matrix is a matrix that equals its own transpose $A=A^{T}$.

(The diagonal act as a mirror, or a line of reflection and any image match their object)

$a_{ij}=a_{ji}$

What is the relationship between invertibility and symmetry?

The text points out that being symmetric doesn't automatically mean a matrix is "useful" in terms of division (inversion).

If $A$ is symmetric $A^{-1}$ is also symmetric

Proof:
- **The Property:** For any invertible matrix, $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$.
    
- **The Condition:** If $A$ is symmetric, then $A = A^T$.
    
- **The Substitution:** Substitute $A$ for $A^T$ in the property above:
    
    $$(A^{-1})^T = (A)^{-1}$$
    
- **The Conclusion:** Since $A^{-1}$ equals its own transpose, **$A^{-1}$ is symmetric.**


### Symmetric Products $R^{T}R$ , $RR^{T}$, and $LDL^{T}$

The transpose of $R R^{T}$ is $RR^{T}$

Why $RR^T$ is always symmetric:

To prove a matrix is symmetric, we transpose the whole thing and see if we get the original back. We use the rule that $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ (the "socks and shoes" rule—you reverse the order when you undo the operation).

- Let $M = RR^T$.
    
- Transpose it: $M^T = (RR^T)^T$.
    
- Apply the rule: $M^T = (R^T)^T R^T$.
    
- Since the transpose of a transpose is the original matrix ($(R^T)^T = R$), we get:
    
    $$M^T = RR^T$$
    

Because $M^T = M$, the result is guaranteed to be symmetric.

It is same as $R^{T}R$.

Thus, the product of a matrix with its transpose is symmetric. But notice that is is not likely that $R^{T}R=R R^{T}$.

## 1. The Logic: Why $A = LDL^T$?

Normally, a matrix $A$ is factored into $L$ (lower triangular), $D$ (diagonal), and $U$ (upper triangular). This is the $LDU$ factorization.

If the matrix is symmetric ($A = A^T$), we can prove that $U$ is simply the transpose of $L$:

1. **Start with the standard form:** $A = LDU$
    
2. **Transpose both sides:** $A^T = (LDU)^T$
    
3. **Apply the product rule for transposes:** $A^T = U^T D^T L^T$
    
4. **Simplify:** Since $D$ is diagonal, $D^T = D$. Since $A$ is symmetric, $A^T = A$.
    
    - This gives us: $A = U^T D L^T$
        
5. **The Uniqueness Principle:** In linear algebra, the $LDU$ factorization is unique. Since we now have two ways to write $A$ ($L \cdot D \cdot U$ and $U^T \cdot D \cdot L^T$), the corresponding parts must be identical.
    
    - Therefore, **$U = L^T$** and **$L = U^T$**.
        

This results in the specialized symmetric formula:

$$A = LDL^T$$
