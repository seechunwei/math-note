Weierstrass Substitution ($t$-substitution)

If you have an integrand that is a rational function of $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$, this substitution is mathematically guaranteed to convert it into a rational function of $t$ (a ratio of polynomials), which can always be solved using standard algebraic techniques like partial fractions.

## 1. The Geometric Foundation (Why it works)

Instead of just memorizing what $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ equal in terms of $t$, we can derive them intuitively using a single right-angled triangle.

We define our substitution variable as:

$$t = \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right)$$

If we construct a right-angled triangle where the angle is $\frac{\theta}{2}$, the definition of tangent ($\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$) allows us to set the opposite side to $t$ and the adjacent side to $1$.

Using the Pythagorean theorem, the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{1 + t^2}$. From this single triangle, we can immediately read off the basic sine and cosine values for the _half-angle_:

$$\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right) = \frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}$$

$$\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}$$


### Deriving $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$

To find the expressions for the _full_ angle $\theta$, we simply apply the standard trigonometric double-angle identities:

- **For $\sin\theta$:**
    
    $$\sin\theta = 2\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right) = 2\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}\right) = \frac{2t}{1+t^2}$$
    
- **For $\cos\theta$:**
    
    $$\cos\theta = \cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right) - \sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}\right)^2 = \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}$$

### Deriving the Differential $d\theta$

We also need to replace $d\theta$ in our integral. We can solve for $\theta$ using our original definition:

$$t = \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right) \implies \frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2} = \tan^{-1}(t) \implies \theta = 2\tan^{-1}(t)$$

Now, take the derivative with respect to $t$:

$$d\theta = \frac{2}{1+t^2}\,dt$$
### 2. The $t$-Substitution Dictionary

Whenever you perform a Weierstrass substitution, you replace the trigonometric terms with these algebraic equivalents:

|**Trigonometric Term**|**Algebraic Equivalent**|
|---|---|
|**$\sin\theta$**|$\displaystyle\frac{2t}{1+t^2}$|
|**$\cos\theta$**|$\displaystyle\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}$|
|**$d\theta$**|$\displaystyle\frac{2}{1+t^2}\,dt$|
## 3. Step-by-Step Example

Let's evaluate an integral that looks messy using traditional methods:

$$\int \frac{1}{1 + \sin\theta + \cos\theta}\,d\theta$$

### Step 1: Substitute the components

Using our dictionary, replace $\sin\theta$, $\cos\theta$, and $d\theta$ with their expressions in terms of $t$:

$$\int \frac{1}{1 + \left(\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\right) + \left(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\right)} \cdot \left(\frac{2}{1+t^2}\,dt\right)$$

### Step 2: Simplify the denominator

Let's find a common denominator for the terms inside the main fraction's denominator:

$$1 + \frac{2t}{1+t^2} + \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2} = \frac{(1+t^2) + 2t + (1-t^2)}{1+t^2}$$

Notice how the $t^2$ and $-t^2$ cancel each other out perfectly:

$$= \frac{2 + 2t}{1+t^2} = \frac{2(1+t)}{1+t^2}$$

### Step 3: Combine and clean up the integrand

Now insert this back into the integral:

$$\int \frac{1}{\frac{2(1+t)}{1+t^2}} \cdot \frac{2}{1+t^2}\,dt$$

Multiply by the reciprocal of the denominator:

$$\int \frac{1+t^2}{2(1+t)} \cdot \frac{2}{1+t^2}\,dt$$

Watch how beautifully the terms cancel out: the $(1+t^2)$ terms cancel, and the constants $2$ cancel. We are left with an incredibly simple algebraic integral:

$$\int \frac{1}{1+t}\,dt$$

### Step 4: Integrate

This is a standard natural logarithm forms:

$$= \ln|1+t| + C$$

### Step 5: Substitute back to the original variable

Finally, replace $t$ with its original definition, $\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$:

$$= \ln\left|1 + \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{\vphantom{1}2}\right)\right| + C$$

Why Weierstrass Substitution (t-substitution) cannot use $t=\tan \theta$?

That is a fantastic question. It feels like using $t = \tan\theta$ would be much simpler and more direct than messing around with half-angles like $t = \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$.

However, if you try to use $t = \tan\theta$, you run into two major mathematical roadblocks that completely defeat the purpose of the Weierstrass substitution.

### 1. It introduces square roots and $\pm$ signs (The "Rationality" Problem)

The main goal of the Weierstrass substitution is to convert a tricky trigonometric integral into a **pure algebraic rational function** (a fraction made of ordinary polynomials). Once it's a rational function, you can easily solve it using partial fractions.

If you set $t = \tan\theta$, look at what happens when you try to express $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ in terms of $t$ using a right triangle:

- $\cos\theta = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}$
    
- $\sin\theta = \pm\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}$
    

This introduces two massive headaches:

1. **The Square Roots:** You no longer have a rational function. Integrating fractions containing $\sqrt{1+t^2}$ is often just as hard (or harder) than the original trigonometric integral.
    
2. **The $\pm$ Ambiguity:** You have to manually keep track of which quadrant $\theta$ is in to decide whether the term is positive or negative.







---

There are the precise conditions for when you can use it, and why it **does not** apply to the composition of trigonometric functions.

## 1. The Strict Condition: "Rational Functions" Only

The algebraic requirement is that the integrand must be a **rational function of sine and cosine**.

In plain terms, this means the terms $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ can only be combined using standard arithmetic: **addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division**. They cannot be trapped inside other functions.

Mathematically, it looks like this:

$$\int R(\sin x, \cos x) \, dx$$

### Examples that meet the condition (Perfect for $t$-sub):

- **$\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{3 + 2\sin x} \, dx$** (Simple addition and division)
    
- **$\displaystyle\int \frac{\cos x}{1 + \sin x + \cos x} \, dx$** (A mix of arithmetic combinations)
    
- **$\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sin^2 x - 3\cos x} \, dx$** (Even powers work, because $\sin^2 x = (\sin x) \cdot (\sin x)$, which is just multiplication)
    

### Another Hidden Condition: Matching Arguments

All trigonometric arguments must be the same single variable (e.g., all $x$). If you have an integral with mixed frequencies like $\int \frac{1}{\sin(2x) + \cos x} \, dx$, you cannot use $t$-substitution directly. You must first use a double-angle identity to turn $\sin(2x)$ into $2\sin x\cos x$ so that every trig function shares the exact same input argument.

## The Foundation: Cosine Double-Angle Formulas

Everything starts with the standard double-angle identity for cosine. You might remember that $\cos(2A)$ can be written in three completely equivalent ways. The two forms we care about here are:

1. **Cosine Form:** $\cos(2A) = 2\cos^2 A - 1$
    
2. **Sine Form:** $\cos(2A) = 1 - 2\sin^2 A$
    

## The Secret Shortcut: Variable Substitution

The core trick to getting a "half-angle" identity is to realize that the relationship is simply between **an angle** ($2A$) and **half of that angle** ($A$).

To make this visually obvious, let's change the variables:

- Let $2A = \theta$
    
- This means $A = \frac{\theta}{2}$
    

Now, we just substitute these into our two foundational formulas.

### Deriving $1 + \cos \theta = 2\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$

Take the **Cosine Form** of the double-angle identity:

$$\cos(2A) = 2\cos^2 A - 1$$

Replace $2A$ with $\theta$, and replace $A$ with $\frac{\theta}{2}$:

$$\cos \theta = 2\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) - 1$$

Now, simply isolate the squared term by adding $1$ to both sides of the equation:

$$1 + \cos \theta = 2\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

### Deriving $1 - \cos \theta = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$

Take the **Sine Form** of the double-angle identity:

$$\cos(2A) = 1 - 2\sin^2 A$$

Replace $2A$ with $\theta$, and replace $A$ with $\frac{\theta}{2}$:

$$\cos \theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

To get the sine term by itself with a positive sign, move $-2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$ to the left side, and move $\cos \theta$ to the right side:

$$2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = 1 - \cos \theta$$

Swapping the sides gives you the exact identity:

$$1 - \cos \theta = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$**Summary Checklist**

- If you see **$1 + \cos \theta$**, think: _"This comes from the $\cos(2A) = 2\cos^2 A - 1$ form, so the $1$ cancels out."_
    
- If you see **$1 - \cos \theta$**, think: _"This comes from the $\cos(2A) = 1 - 2\sin^2 A$ form, so the $1$ subtracts away."_
