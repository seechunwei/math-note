
This section investigate the matrix analogue of the multiplicative inverse.

==Definition==
An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is said to be invertible if there is an $n\times n$ matrix $A^{-1}$ such that

$$
\begin{align}
AA^{-1}=I_{n} \\
A^{-1}A=I_{n}
\end{align}
$$
A matrix that is not invertible is sometimes called a singular matrix, and an invertible matrix is called a nonsingular matrix.

> [!NOTE]
> The inverse of a matrix is unique. Why?
> Suppose $CA=I$ and $AB=I$, then
> 
> $$
> C=CI=CAB=IB=B
> $$
> $\blacksquare$
> 


==Theorem 4==
The inverse of $2\times 2$ matrix. Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$. If $ad-bc\neq 0$, then $A$ is invertible and

$$
A^{-1}= \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}
d & -b \\
-c & a
\end{bmatrix}
$$
If $ad-bc=0$, then $A$ is not invertible.


The determinant of $2\times 2$ matrix is

$$
\det A=ad-bc
$$

$A$ is invertible iff $\det A\neq 0$.


==Theorem 5==
if $A$ is an invertible $n\times n$ matrix, then for each $b$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, the equation $Ax=b$ has the unique solution $x=A^{-1}b$. ^2cd393

Proof:
Suppose $Au=b$

$$
\begin{align}
A^{-1}Au&=A^{-1}b \\
u&=A^{-1}b
\end{align}
$$

(Because we started with an arbitrary solution $u$ and proved that $u$ **must** equal $A^{-1}b$, it is impossible for any different solution to exist. Thus, the solution is unique.)

(If we view invertible in the perspective of linear transformation, then it is onto and one-to-one )

==Elementary Matrices==
An elementary matrix is one that is obtained by performing a single elementary row operation on an identity matrix.


> [!info]
> If an elementary row operation is performed on an $m\times n$ matrix $A$, the resulting matrix can be written as $EA$, where the $m\times m$ matrix $E$ is created by performing the same row operation on $I_{m}$.

Each elementary matrix $E$ is invertible. The inverse of $E$ is the elementary matrix of the same type that transforms $E$ back into $I$.


==Theorem 7==
An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if $A$ is row equivalent to $I_{n}$, and in this case, any sequence of elementary row operations that reduces $A$ to $I_{n}$ also transforms $I_{n}$ into $A^{-1}$.

Proof:
Suppose $A$ is invertible. By theorem 5, $Ax=b$ has a unique solution for each $b$. $A$ has a pivot in every row (Theorem in 1.4). Since $A$ is a square matrix, it follow that the pivot must on the diagonal. Thus $rref(A)=I_{n}$.

Suppose conversely, $A\sim I_{n}$. Then there exist an elementary matrix $E$ such that

$$
EA=I
$$

Since $E$ is invertible, it follow that there exist $E^{-1}$ such that
$$
\begin{align}
E^{-1}EA&=E^{-1}I \\
A&=E^{-1}
\end{align}
$$

By theorem 6b), $E^{-1}$ is invertible, thus $A$ is invertible. Notice that $A^{-1}=E$.


1) $E_{i}(\alpha)A$ is the matrix obtained when performing the elementary row operation $R_{i}(\alpha)$ on $A$.
2) $E^{i}_{j}A$ is.... $R^{i}_{j}$ on $A$
3) $E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$ is.... $R^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$ on $A$

Proof 1):
Let $E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$ be the elementary matrix obtained from performing the row operation $R^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$ on the identity matrix $I_{m}$. Write

$$
\begin{align}
E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)=[e_{rs}]_{m\times m}
\end{align}
$$
and 

$$
A=[a_{rs}]_{m\times n}
$$
Then, the $(r,s)-entry$ of $E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)A$ is

$$
(E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)A)_{rs}=\sum_{k=1}^{n} e_{rk}a_{ks}
$$

(dot product).

How to define $E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)$?
Notice that the entry being change is $e_{ji}$, row $j$ and column $i$. Why is column $i$? Because we take from row $i$ and the only non zero entry in row $i$ is $e_{ii}$ which is the diagonal at column $i$. Thus, the column $i$ of row $j$ will be change.

This happen when $r=j,s=i$. 

What about other entry?
So the diagonal is 1 and other entries are 0. Thus,

$$
e_{rs}=\begin{cases}
\alpha, & \text{if }  r=j,s=i, \\
1, & \text{if } r=s \\
0, & otherwise
\end{cases}
$$

Thus, let's consider the entry of the product 

Case1 : any row that isn't row $j$ ($r\neq j$)
- The sum $\sum_{k=1}^n e_{rk}a_{ks}$ becomes $0 + 0 + \dots + (1 \cdot a_{rs}) + \dots + 0$.

Why?
$$(e_{r1} \cdot a_{1s}) + (e_{r2} \cdot a_{2s}) + \dots + (e_{rr} \cdot a_{rs}) + \dots + (e_{rn} \cdot a_{ns})$$

- Result: $a_{rs}$.
- **Intuition:** The rows you didn't intend to change stay exactly as they were in matrix $A$.


 **Case 2: The target row ($r = j$)**

This is the only row where $E$ has **two** non-zero entries. As the "scanner" moves across row $j$ of $E$, it hits:

1. The value **$\alpha$** at position $k=i$ (the $i$-th column).
    
2. The value **$1$** at position $k=j$ (the diagonal).
    

So the sum for row $j$ becomes:

$$(E_j^i(\alpha)A)_{js} = (e_{ji} \cdot a_{is}) + (e_{jj} \cdot a_{js})$$

Substituting the values ($e_{ji} = \alpha$ and $e_{jj} = 1$):

$$(E_j^i(\alpha)A)_{js} = \alpha a_{is} + a_{js}$$
Therefore, the entries of A and $E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)A$ are the same except on the j-th row,of which the j-th row of $E^{i}_{j}(\alpha)A$ is the result of adding α times the i-th row of A on the jth row of A. Q.E.D

### Algorithm for finding $A^{-1}$

Theorem 7 reveal a way to find inverse of $A$ which is by determining the elementary matrix that reduce $A$ into $I$.

$$
\begin{align}
\begin{bmatrix}
A & I
\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}
I & A^{-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

Still remember how to obtain the elementary matrix? We perform the same row operation to identity matrix.

By the definition of inverse, it show that the elementary matrix that reduce $A$ to $I$ is the inverse of $A$.

### Another View of Matrix Inversion

^47d9a9

Denote the columns of $I_{n}$ by $e_{1}\dots e_{n}$. Then, row reduction of $\begin{bmatrix}A & I\end{bmatrix}$ to $\begin{bmatrix}I & A^{-1}\end{bmatrix}$ can be view as the simultaneous solution of the $n$ system

$$
Ax=e_{1},Ax=e_{2},\dots,Ax=e_{n}
$$

The equation $AA^{-1}=I$ and the definition of matrix multiplication show that the columns of $A^{-1}$ are precisely the solutions of the systems.

$$
AA^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}
Ax_{1} & Ax_{2} & \dots & Ax_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Separately, we are solving for 
$Ax_{1}=e_{1},Ax_{2}=e_{2},\dots,Ax_{n}=e_{n}$.

And this is equivalent to reduce $\begin{bmatrix}A & I\end{bmatrix}$ to $\begin{bmatrix}I & A^{-1}\end{bmatrix}$.
