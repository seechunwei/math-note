> [!info] The law of cosines
In ant triangle $ABC$ we have
>$$
\begin{align}
a^{2}&=b^{2}+c^{2}-2bc\cos A \\
b^{2}&=a^{2}+c^{2}-2ac\cos B \\
c^{2}&=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos C
\end{align}
$$

Notice that The Law of Cosine is the general form of Pythagorean Theorem. Thus, to begin with the proof, we suppose a triangle that is not right angle and we use Pythagorean theorem to prove.

Proof:
Place triangle $ABC$ so that $\angle A$ is at the origin. The coordinates of vertices $B$ and $C$ are $(c,0)$ and $(b\cos A,b\sin A)$, respectively. (it is the same whether $\angle A$ is acute or obtuse, the obtuse case is shown in diagram below). Using the Distance formula, we get

$$
\begin{align}
a^{2}&=(b\cos A-c)^{2}+(b\sin A-0)^{2} \\
&=b^{2}\cos ^{2}A-2bc\cos A+c^{2}+b^{2}\sin ^{2}A \\
&=b^{2}(\cos ^{2}A+\sin ^{2}A)+c^{2}-2bc\cos A \\
&=b^{2}+c^{2}-2bc\cos A
\end{align}
$$

### The area of a triangle

> [!info] Heron's Formula
The area of triangle $ABC$ is given by
>$$
A=\sqrt{ s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c) }$$
where $s=\frac{1}{2}(a+b+c)$ is the semi perimeter of the triangle. 

Proof:
We start with the formula $A= \frac{1}{2}ab \sin C$ from section 6.3. Thus,

$A^{2}=\frac{1}{4}a^{2}b^{2}\sin ^{2}C$


How to understand it intuitively?


Consider the incircle (the largest circle that can fit inside the triangle)

If we connect the incenter to the three vertices, we split the original triangle into 3 small triangle and the height of each small triangle is exactly the radius $r$. Thus, the total area is

$$A = \frac{1}{2}ar + \frac{1}{2}br + \frac{1}{2}cr = r\left(\frac{a+b+c}{2}\right) = rs$$

The question is how to determine the $r$?

### Step 1: The Principle of Two Tangents

If you stand at any point outside a circle and draw two straight lines that just touch the circle (tangents), the distance from your starting point to both touchpoints is **exactly the same**.

Because a triangle has three vertices, this rule happens three times simultaneously:

- From vertex $A$, the two tangent segments to the circle have equal length. Let’s call this length **$x$**.
    
- From vertex $B$, the two tangent segments have equal length. Let’s call this length **$y$**.
    
- From vertex $C$, the two tangent segments have equal length. Let’s call this length **$z$**.
    

### Step 2: Relate $x, y, z$ to the Main Side Lengths $a, b, c$

Look closely at the sides of the triangle in the diagram. Each full side is made up of two of our tangent pieces combined:

- Side $a$ (opposite vertex $A$) is made of $y$ and $z$:
    
    $$a = y + z$$
    
- Side $b$ (opposite vertex $B$) is made of $x$ and $z$:
    
    $$b = x + z$$
    
- Side $c$ (opposite vertex $C$) is made of $x$ and $y$:
    
    $$c = x + y$$
    

### Step 3: Find the Total Perimeter and Semiperimeter $s$

If we add all three sides together to get the total perimeter, we are just adding up all six tangent segments:

$$\text{Perimeter} = a + b + c = (y + z) + (x + z) + (x + y)$$

Notice that $x$, $y$, and $z$ each appear exactly twice:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 2x + 2y + 2z = 2(x + y + z)$$

The **semiperimeter ($s$)** is exactly half of the total perimeter. So, if we divide the total perimeter by 2, we get a beautifully clean algebraic relationship:

$$s = x + y + z$$

> 💡 **Key Realization:** The semiperimeter $s$ is simply the sum of one copy of each internal tangent segment.

### Step 4: The Clean Substitution—Revealing $(s-a)$

Now, let's play a simple game of elimination.

We know that $s = x + y + z$.

What happens if we subtract the full side length $a$ from our semiperimeter $s$?

$$s - a = (x + y + z) - a$$

But look back at Step 2! We already know that side $a$ is exactly equal to $y + z$. Let's substitute $(y + z)$ in place of $a$:

$$s - a = (x + y + z) - (y + z)$$

The $+y$ and $-y$ cancel out. The $+z$ and $-z$ cancel out. Look at what survives:

$$s - a = x$$

By applying this exact same subtraction step to the other two sides, the entire triangle decodes cleanly:

- $s - a = x$ (The tangent segments sticking out of vertex $A$)
    
- $s - b = y$ (The tangent segments sticking out of vertex $B$)
    
- $s - c = z$ (The tangent segments sticking out of vertex $C$)

Our goal is to calculate the area of a triangle without knowing any internal angles or height. 

Using a clever geometric theorem (known as **Heron's Lemma** involving cyclic quadrilaterals), he proved that the radius of the inner circle always maintains this exact structural ratio with the internal tangent segments:

$$r^2 = \frac{x \cdot y \cdot z}{s}$$

Because Heron could not physically measure the internal pieces $x, y,$ and $z$, he used the algebraic substitution we just did to swap out the hidden internal lengths for external measurements he _could_ measure:

$$r^2 = \frac{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}{s}$$
