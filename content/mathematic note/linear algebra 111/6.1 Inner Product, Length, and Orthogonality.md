
Geometric concept of length, distance and perpendicularity, which are all well known for $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ are defined here for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

The Inner Product

> [!definition] Definition (The Inner Product)
Suppose $u$ and $v$ are vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, then we regard $u$ and $v$ as $n\times 1$ matrices, the inner product $u\cdot v$ is defined as
>
>$$
\begin{align}
u^{T}v&=\begin{bmatrix}
u_{1} & \dots & u_{n}
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
v_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
v_{n}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=u_{1}v_{1}+\dots+u_{n}v_{n}
\end{align}
>$$

Notice that

$$
\begin{align}
v^{T}u&=\begin{bmatrix}
v_{1} & \dots & v_{n}
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
u_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
u_{n}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= v_{1}u_{1}+\dots+v_{n}u_{n}
\end{align}
$$

Thus, it is clear that $u\cdot v=v\cdot u$

Remark




> [!theorem] Theorem 1
> Let $u,v$ and $w$ be vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, and let $c$ be a scalar. Then
> 
> a. $u\cdot v=v\cdot u$
> b. $(u+v)\cdot w=u\cdot w+v\cdot w$
> c. $(cu)\cdot v=c(u\cdot v)=u\cdot(cv)$
> d. $u\cdot u\geq 0$, and $u\cdot u=0$ iff $u=0$

Properties b) and c) can be combined together several times:

$$
(c_{1}u_{1}+\dots+c_{p}u_{p})\cdot w=c_{1}(u_{1}\cdot w)+\dots+c_{p}(u_{p}\cdot w)
$$

Notice that the $u_{1}\dots u_{p}$ is the vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

> [!definition] The length of a vector
The length (or norm) of $v$ is the nonnegative scalar $||v||$ defined by
>
>$$
||v||=\sqrt{ v \cdot v }=\sqrt{ v_{1}^{2}+\dots+v_{n}^{2} }$$
and 
>$$
||v||^{2}=v\cdot v$$

Notice that square root of $v\cdot v$ is defined because $v\cdot v$ is nonnegative. 

Why it is defined like this?
Suppose $v \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$, say $v=\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix}$.  Then $||v||$ coincides with the standard notion of the length of the line segment from origin to $v$.
This follow by Pythagorean Theorem.

![[Pasted image 20260618162830.png]]


For any scalar $c$, the length of $cv$ is $|c|$ times the length of $v$. That is

$$
||cv||=|c|||v||
$$

A vector whose length is 1 is called a **unit vector**. How to obtain it?

If we divide a non zero vector $v$ by its length 
$$
u= \frac{v}{||v||}
$$
then such $u$ is a unit vector. (The process of creating $u$ from $v$ is called **normalizing** $v$ ) and $u$ is in the same direction as $v$.

### Orthogonal Vectors

The concept of perpendicular lines in ordinary Euclidean geometry has an analogue in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

(it is a line that form 2 equal angle for both side)

Consider $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and 2 lines through the origin determined by vectors $u$ and $v$. They are perpendicular if the distance from $u$ to $v$ is the same as from $u$ to $-v$.

![[Pasted image 20260618165442.png]]


$$
\begin{align}
[dist(u,-v)]^{2}&=||u-(-v)||^{2}=||u+v||^{2} \\
&= (u+v)\cdot(u+v) \\
&=u\cdot(u+v)+ v\cdot(u+v) \\
&= ||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}+2u\cdot v
\end{align}
$$

$$
\begin{align}
[disc(u,v)]^{2}&=||u-v||^{2} \\
&=(u-v)\cdot(u-v) \\
&=u\cdot(u-v)-v\cdot(u-v) \\
&=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}-2u\cdot v
\end{align}
$$

To make it equal 
$$
\begin{align}
2u\cdot v&=-2u\cdot v \\
u\cdot v&=-(u\cdot v)
\end{align}
$$

It implies that $u\cdot v=0$. Thus

> [!definition] Definition
Two vectors $u$ and $v$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ are orthogonal to each other iff $u\cdot v=0$


> [!theorem] Theorem 2 The Pythagorean 
> Two vectors $u$ and $v$ are orthogonal iff $||u+v||^{2}=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}$.
> 

> [!remark]
> Notice that this is related to triangle inequality
> 
> $$
> ||u+v||\leq||u||+||v||
> $$

Proof:
Suppose $u$ and $v$ are orthogonal. Then $u\cdot v=v\cdot u=0$. So,

$$
\begin{align}
||u+v||^{2}&=(u+ v)\cdot(u+v) \\
&=(u\cdot(u+ v))+(v\cdot(u\cdot v)) \\
&=||u||+||v||+2u\cdot v \\
&=||u||+||v||
\end{align}
$$
Conversely, suppose that $||u+v||^{2}=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}$. Note that 

$$
\begin{align}
||u+v||^{2}&=(u+v)\cdot(u+v) \\
&=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}+2u\cdot v
\end{align}
$$
Thus,
$$
\begin{align}
||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}&=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}+2u\cdot v \\
2u\cdot v&=0 \\
u\cdot v&=0
\end{align}
$$
$\blacksquare$.

### Orthogonal Complements

If a vector $z$ is orthogonal to every vectors in a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, then $z$ is said to be orthogonal to $W$.  The set of all vectors $z$ that are orthogonal to $W$ is called the **orthogonal complement** of $W$ denoted by $W^{\perp}$ .

$$
W^{\perp}=\{ z \in \mathbb{R}^{n}: z\cdot w=0 \text{ for all }w\in W\}
$$

> [!remark] Remark:
> The orthogonal complement of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the set $\{ 0 \}$ because $0\cdot w=0$ for any $w\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ and vice versa.
> 

Example:
Let $W$ be a plane through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, and let $L$ be the line though the origin and perpendicular to $W$.

$z$ is in the line segment $Span(z)=L$ is perpendicular to the line segment from 0 to $w \in W$.

![[Pasted image 20260618224444.png]]

So each vector on $L$ is orthogonal to every vectors $w$ in $W$. In fact, $L$ consists of all vectors that are orthogonal to the $w$ in $W$ and vice versa.

Thus,

$$
L=W^{\perp} \text{ and }W=L^{\perp}
$$
> [!theorem] Theorem
>1) A vector $x \in W^{\perp}$ iff $x$ is orthogonal to every vector in a set that spans $W$.
>2) $W^{\perp}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
>3) If $x$ is in both $W$ and $W^{\perp}$, then $x=0$
>4)  If $V$ is a subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $V\subset W$, then $W^{\perp} \subset V^{\perp}$.
>5) $(W^{\perp})^{\perp}=W$

> Let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix. The orthogonal complement of the row space of $A$ is the null space of $A$, and the orthogonal complement of the column space of $A$ is the null space of $A^{T}$:

### Angles in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ 

^bc84ba

If $u$ and $v$ are non zero vectors in either $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, then there is a relation between their inner product and the angle $\theta$ between the two line segment. 

$$
u\cdot v=||u||||v||\cos \theta
$$

![[Pasted image 20260619151942.png]]

The side of length of this triangle is $||u||$, $||v||$ and $||u-v||$. Thus, by the law of cosines

$$
||u-v||^{2}=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}-2||u||||v||\cos \theta
$$

Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
||u||||v||\cos \theta&= \frac{1}{2}[||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}-||u-v||^{2}] \\
&=\frac{1}{2}[u\cdot u+v\cdot v-(u-v)\cdot(u-v)] \\
&=\frac{1}{2}[u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}+v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}-(u_{1}-v_{1})^{2}-(u_{2}-v_{2})^{2}] \\
&= \frac{1}{2}[u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}+v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}-(u_{1}^{2}-2u_{1}v_{1}+v_{1}^{2})-(u_{2}^{2}-2u_{2}v_{2}+v_{2}^{2})] \\
&= \frac{1}{2}[2u_{1}v_{1}+2u_{2}v_{2}] \\
&=u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2} \\
&=u\cdot v
\end{align}
$$

Notice that

$$
||u-v||^{2}=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}-2u\cdot v
$$

In statistic the value of $\cos \theta$ defined as correlation coefficient.


Can we use this concept and try to look back what is the inner product of 2 different vector $v_{1},v_{2}$ where $v_{1} \in Span\{ v_{2} \}$?

While the algebraic formula ($u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \dots$) tells you _how to calculate_ it, the formula $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\| \cos \theta$ tells you _what it means_.

$$
u\cdot v=||u||\cdot(||v||\cos \theta)
$$

The $||v||\cdot \cos \theta$ is actually the projection length of $v$ onto $u$.

Therefore, the inner product is simply:

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (\text{Length of }\mathbf{u}) \times (\text{Length of }\mathbf{v}\text{'s shadow on }\mathbf{u})$$

This also explain why the length of one vector is defined as

$$
\sqrt{ u\cdot u }
$$
Because $\theta=0$ and $\cos \theta=1$, thus $u\cdot u$ is simply the square of its length.


Besides that

$$\cos \theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|}$$

Thus, 
1) $\cos \theta> 0\implies \theta<90^\circ$
2) $\cos \theta=0\implies \theta=90^\circ$
3) $\cos \theta<0\implies \theta>90^\circ$
