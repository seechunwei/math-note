We usually solve a differential equation by recognizing it as a certain kind of equation (say, separable, linear, exact, and so on)

What if it does not fall into one of the classes we learn before? The substitution procedures that are discussed in this section may be helpful in this situation.

Let say we have a DE $\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y)$, and we let $y=g(x,u)$. So now it become $f(x,g(x,u))$.

It means that now the variable $y$ is being replace by another relation depend on variable $x$ and $u$. (The whole DE only in term of $x$ and $u$)

If $g$ possesses first-partial derivatives, then the Chain Rule

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}
$$

### Why is $u$ a function of $x$?
In the original differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$, $y$ is an unknown function of $x$ (that is, $y = y(x)$—which is why $\frac{dy}{dx}$ exists).

When we introduce a helper variable $u$ related to $x$ and $y$ (such as $y = g(x, u)$ or $u = x + y$), as $x$ varies, $y(x)$ varies, which forces $u$ to vary with $x$ as well.
Therefore, $u$ is regarded as a function of $x$: $u = u(x)$, and has its own derivative $\frac{du}{dx}$.

---

### The Total Rate of Change (Multivariable Chain Rule)

Let $y=g(x,u)$.

From multivariable calculus, the total rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$ is:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}}_{\text{direct rate}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}}_{\text{indirect rate through } u}
$$

#### 1. The "Two Paths" Mental Picture
Because $u$ depends on $x$, when $x$ changes, it affects $y$ along **two simultaneous pathways**:

```text
       Path 1 (Direct)
    x ------------------> y
     \                   /
      \                 /
       -> u ----------->
       Path 2 (Indirect)
```

1. **Path 1 (Direct):** $x$ changes $y$ directly (treating $u$ as constant).
   * The rate of change along this path is the partial derivative:
     $$
     \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}
     $$
2. **Path 2 (Indirect):** $x$ changes $u$, which in turn changes $y$.
   * First, $x$ changes $u$ at the rate $\frac{du}{dx}$.
   * Then, that change in $u$ changes $y$ at the rate $\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}$.
   * Along this path, the rate is:
     $$
     \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}
     $$

Adding both pathways gives the total derivative:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dx}
$$

#### 2. The "Tiny Nudge" Explanation ($\Delta x$)
If you nudge $x$ by a tiny amount $\Delta x$:
1. $x$ changes by $\Delta x$.
2. This forces $u$ to change by a tiny amount $\Delta u \approx \left(\frac{du}{dx}\right)\Delta x$.

By linear approximation, the total change $\Delta y$ is:
$$
\Delta y \approx \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\right)\Delta x + \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\right)\Delta u
$$

Dividing both sides by $\Delta x$:
$$
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \approx \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}
$$

Taking the limit as $\Delta x \to 0$ (since $\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta x} = 1$):
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\frac{du}{dx}
$$

#### 3. Familiar Check: The Product Rule is just this formula!
Suppose $y = x \cdot u$. Here, $g(x, u) = x \cdot u$:
* $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = u$
* $\frac{\partial g}{\partial u} = x$

Plugging into the formula:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = u + x \frac{du}{dx}
$$
This is literally the ordinary **Product Rule**!

### Transforming the Differential Equation

Where does the equation $g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx} = f(x, g(x, u))$ come from?

Notice that we have **two separate facts** that both describe $\frac{dy}{dx}$:

1. **Fact 1 (The original Differential Equation given to us):**
   $$
   \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)
   $$
2. **Fact 2 (From differentiating our substitution $y = g(x, u)$):**
   $$
   \frac{dy}{dx} = g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx}
   $$

#### Setting Them Equal
Because **both** expressions are equal to $\frac{dy}{dx}$, we set them equal to each other:
$$
\underbrace{g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx}}_{\text{Fact 2}} = \underbrace{f(x, y)}_{\text{Fact 1}}
$$

Now, to eliminate the remaining $y$ on the right side, replace $y$ with $g(x, u)$:
$$
g_x(x, u) + g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx} = f(x, g(x, u))
$$


---

### Solving for $\frac{du}{dx}$

Now we isolate $\frac{du}{dx}$ using basic algebra:
$$
g_u(x, u)\frac{du}{dx} = f(x, g(x, u)) - g_x(x, u)
$$
$$
\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{f(x, g(x, u)) - g_x(x, u)}{g_u(x, u)}
$$

Because the entire right-hand side now depends only on $x$ and $u$ (every $y$ has been eliminated), we define it as $F(x, u)$:
$$
\frac{du}{dx} = F(x, u)
$$

If we solve this new DE for $u = \phi(x)$, then the solution to the original equation is:
$$
y = g(x, \phi(x))
$$

---

> [!remark] Daily Practice vs. Theoretical Proof
> * **In Daily Practice (Differentiate with respect to $x$ directly):**  
>   When solving actual problems (such as $u = x + y$, $u = \frac{y}{x}$, or $u = y^{1-n}$), you **do not** need to write out the abstract partial derivatives $g_x$ and $g_u$. Simply differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ directly:
>   $$
>   u = x + y \implies \frac{du}{dx} = 1 + \frac{dy}{dx} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{du}{dx} - 1
>   $$
>   This is 100% mathematically rigorous (justified by the Sum Rule of single-variable calculus) and avoids algebraic mistakes.
> 
> * **Why the Formula in the Proof Looks Complicated:**  
>   A textbook proof must work for **any arbitrary substitution** $y = g(x, u)$ (whether linear, fractional, trigonometric, or nonlinear). Because the author cannot know in advance which function you will pick, they must use the general multivariable chain rule $\frac{dy}{dx} = g_x + g_u \frac{du}{dx}$ to prove that a transformed first-order ODE $\frac{du}{dx} = F(x, u)$ always exists. For any specific problem, that general formula simply reduces to ordinary single-variable differentiation!

### Concrete Example

Consider the differential equation:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = (x + y)^2
$$

1. **Choose substitution:** Let $u = x + y \implies y = u - x$, so $g(x, u) = u - x$.
2. **Differentiate:** 
   $$
   \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{du}{dx} - 1
   $$
3. **Substitute into the DE:**
   $$
   \frac{du}{dx} - 1 = u^2 \implies \frac{du}{dx} = u^2 + 1
   $$
4. **Solve for $u$:** This is separable!
   $$
   \frac{du}{u^2 + 1} = dx \implies \arctan(u) = x + c \implies u = \tan(x + c)
   $$
5. **Back-substitute to find $y$:**
   $$
   y = u - x = \tan(x + c) - x
   $$
