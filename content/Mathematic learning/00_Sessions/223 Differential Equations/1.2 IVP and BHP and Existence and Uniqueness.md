# 1.2 Initial-Value Problem

We are often interested in solving differential equation with a condition imposed on the unknown function $y(x)$ and its derivatives at a number $x_{0}$. 

> [!definition] $nth$-order IVP
> $nth$-order IVP is about solving a differential equation on some interval that containing $x_{0}$ and subject to $n$ side conditions specified at $x_{0}:$
> 
> Solve: $\frac{d^{n}y}{dx^{n}}=f(x,y,y',\dots,y^{(n-1)})$
> Subject to: $y(x_{0})=y_{0},y'(x_{0})=y_{1},\dots,y^{(n-1)}(x_{0})=y_{n-1}$ (which is called **initial conditions (IC**)

This means that we need to first find an $n-$parameter family of solutions of the differential equation and use the initial conditions at $x_{0}$ to determine the $n$ constant in this family. The resulting particular solution is defined on some interval $I$ that contain $x_{0}$.

## Geometric Interpretation:
First order IVP:
Solve: $\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y)$
Subject to: $y(x_{0})=y_{0}$

## First Order IVP

Show that $y=ce^{x}$ is a one-parameter family of solutions of the first order equation $y'=y$.

$y':$
$$
\begin{align}
y'=ce^{x}
\end{align}
$$
Thus, by substitution
$$
y'=y
$$
Hence $y=ce^{x}$ is a one-parameter family of solution for the $DE$ on interval $I=(-\infty,\infty)$.

**a)** Let say we impose an initial condition $y(0)=3$:

By substitution:
$$
\begin{align}
3&=ce^{0} \\
c&=3
\end{align}
$$

Thus, $y=3e^{x}$ is a solution of the IVP
$$
y'=y, y(0)=3
$$
**b)** Let's try impose another initial condition $y(1)=-2$. Then,

By substitution:
$$
\begin{align}
-2&=ce^{1} \\
c&=-2e^{-1}
\end{align}
$$
Thus, $y=-2e^{x-1}$ is a solution for the IVP.

### Interval I of Definition of a Solution

The one-parameter family of solutions of the first-order differential equation $y'+2xy^{2}=0$ is $y= \frac{1}{(x^{2}+c)}$. (Verify!)

If we impose the initial condition $y(0)=-1$, then $c=-1$. Hence $y=\frac{1}{x^{2}-1}$ is the solution of IVP.

Now consider the domain of the function $y= \frac{1}{(x^{2}-1)}$, the domain is $(-\infty,-1)\cup(-1,1)\cup(1,\infty)$. 

Now consider the interval $I$ of the solution for
$$
y'+2xy^{2}=0
$$
By definition, the interval $I$ can be taken to be any interval that $y(x)$ is defined and differentiable. (which is one of the interval: $(-\infty,-1)$, $(-1,1)$ or $(1,\infty)$)

Now consider the interval $I$ of the solution for
$$
y'+2xy^{2}=0, y(0)=-1
$$

Since the interval $I$ must contain $x_{0}=0$, thus the largest interval is $(-1,1)$.

## Existence and Uniqueness

In this chapter we only consider the first-order IVP:

We want to answer 2 question:

1) Existence:
Does the  differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y)$ possess solutions?
Do any of the solution curves pass though the point $(x_{0},y_{0})$?

2) Uniqueness:
When can we be certain that there is precisely one solution curve passing through the point $(x_{0},y_{0})$?

> [!Theorem] Theorem 1.2.1 Existence of a Unique Solution
> Let $R$ be a rectangle in the $xy$-plane defined by $a\leq x\leq b$, $c\leq y\leq d$ that contains the point $(x_{0},y_{0})$ in its interior. If $f(x,y)$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ are continuous on $R$, then there exists some interval $I_{0}:(x_{0}-h,x_{0}+h),h>0$, contained in $[a,b]$ and a unique function $y(x)$, defined on $I_{0}$, that is a solution of the initial-value problem
> 

> [!remark]
> It is also called Picard-Lindelöf Theorem

![[Pasted image 20261011150937.png]]

**Why $\frac{dy}{dx}$ must continuous?**
If it is not continuous ($\to \infty$) , then the function have a vertical tangent (because the slop increase infinity even though $\Delta x\to 0$), thus the it does not have a solution, because the candidate function is not differentiable

**What is $\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}$?** 
It is read as "**partial derivative of $f$ with respect to $y$**". 

When a function depends on more than one variable-such as $f(x,y)$ , we cannot just write $\frac{df}{dy}$ because there are 2 independent variable $x$ and $y$. Instead we use $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$, which means

>Differentiate with respect to $y$, while treating $x$ as if it were a fixed constant number (like 2 or 5)

**Example 1:** $f(x,y)=x^{2}y^{3}$

We treat $x^{2}$ as a constant thus its derivative is 0 , and we differentiate $y^{3}$ which is $\frac{d}{dy}y^{3}=3y^{2}$ . Hence we apply product rule:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=x^{2}(3y^{2})=3x^{2}y^{2}
$$

**Example 2**: $f(x,y)=x^{4}+5y^{2}$
We treat $x^{4}$ as constant, thus its derivative is $0$. Hence
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=10y
$$

### Intuition for Existence and Uniqueness

**1. When $f(x,y)$ is continuous (Existence):**
The slope is continuous at each point $(x,y)$, shown by the arrows. Every solution curve that passes through the point follows the flow of the slope. If it is not continuous (e.g. undefined or infinite slope, arrow disappears), the curve cannot continue smoothly, so a solution does not exist.

**2. When $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ is continuous (Uniqueness):**
If $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ is continuous, curves starting at the same point are locked into the same flow. If it is not continuous (e.g. $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \to \infty$), a second solution can peel off the flow and branch away, so uniqueness fails.
### Example

The differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx}=xy^{1/2}$ possesses at least 2 solutions whose graph pass through $(0,0)$.
Solution:
$$
y= \frac{x^{4}}{16} \text{ and }y=0
$$

![[Pasted image 20261011144722.png]]

Inspection of the functions
$$
f(x,y)=xy^{1/2} \text{ and } \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}= \frac{x}{2y^{1/2}}
$$
shows that they are continuous when $y>0$. Hence by Theorem 1.2.1, we can conclude that through any point $(x_{0},y_{0})$, $y_{0}>0$, there is some interval centered at $x_{0}$ on which the given differential equation has a unique solution. So without solving the ODE, we can know the exist some interval centered at $y=2$, has a unique solution. 

> [!question]
> Does the interval have to be centered at $x_0$? And because the theorem says "there exists some", does that mean we cannot ensure a given interval has a unique solution even though both inspection functions are continuous?
> 
> 1. **Must it be centered at $x_0$?**
>    * The theorem states $I_0 = (x_0 - h, x_0 + h)$ because $x_0$ is an interior point. This ensures you can step both forward ($x > x_0$) and backward ($x < x_0$) from $x_0$ by some distance $h$.
>    * So it doesn't need to be at center it can be $h_{1},h_{2}$
> 
> 2. **Can we ensure a given interval has a unique solution?**
>    * **No.** Theorem 1.2.1 is strictly **local** (it only guarantees *some* small interval $I_0$ around $x_0$). Even if $f$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ are continuous on a region:
>      * The solution curve could wander out of the safe region (e.g. dipping down to touch $y = 0$).
>      * The solution could blow up to $\pm\infty$ in finite time (e.g. $\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2 \implies y = \frac{1}{1-x}$).
> 
> *(Note: There exists a separate, stronger theorem 4.1.1 for linear ODEs in Chapter 4 where the solution is guaranteed on the entire given interval).*

#### Example: Why Theorem 1.2.1 is Local (Finite-Time Blow-Up)

Consider the IVP:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2, \quad y(0) = 1
$$

1. **Check continuity:**
   * $f(x, y) = y^2$ is continuous everywhere on $(-\infty, \infty)$.
   * $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y$ is continuous everywhere on $(-\infty, \infty)$.

   Both functions are continuous on the **entire $xy$-plane**!

2. **Solve the IVP:**
   $$
   \frac{dy}{y^2} = dx \implies -\frac{1}{y} = x + C
   $$
   Using $y(0) = 1$:
   $$
   -\frac{1}{1} = 0 + C \implies C = -1
   $$
   Hence:
   $$
   y(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x}
   $$

3. **Interval of definition:**
   * The solution blows up at $x = 1$ (vertical asymptote).
   * Since $x_0 = 0$, the interval of definition containing $x_0$ is only:
     $$
     I = (-\infty, 1)
     $$

Even though $f$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ are continuous everywhere on $(-\infty, \infty)$, the solution cannot cross $x = 1$. This illustrates why Theorem 1.2.1 can only guarantee **some** interval $I_0 = (-h, h)$ with $h < 1$, and cannot guarantee a given interval like $[-2, 5]$.

### How General Theorem 1.2.1 Connects to the Linear ODE Theorem

^b070e8

[[1.2 IVP and BHP and Existence and Uniqueness#^f7347d]]

What happens if we apply Theorem 1.2.1 specifically to a **first-order linear ODE**?
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = f(x) \iff \frac{dy}{dx} = \underbrace{f(x) - P(x)y}_{F(x,y)}
$$

Let's test the two conditions of Theorem 1.2.1:
1. **$F(x,y) = f(x) - P(x)y$ is continuous** on $I \times (-\infty, \infty)$ whenever $P(x)$ and $f(x)$ are continuous on $I$.
2. **$\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}$ is continuous:**
   $$
   \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big(f(x) - P(x)y\big) = -P(x)
   $$
   Notice that $\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = -P(x)$ **contains no $y$ term**! Because $P(x)$ is continuous on $I$, $\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}$ is automatically continuous everywhere on the vertical strip $I \times (-\infty, \infty)$.

#### The Upgrade: Why Local ($I_0$) Becomes Global ($I$)
* **In general/nonlinear ODEs (e.g. $y' = y^2$):** The derivative $\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = 2y$ grows with $y$. Slopes accelerate rapidly, allowing curves to escape to $\pm\infty$ in finite time (blow up). Hence Theorem 1.2.1 can only guarantee a small local interval $I_0 = (x_0 - h, x_0 + h)$.
* **In linear ODEs:** Because $\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = -P(x)$ is independent of $y$, growth in $y$ is strictly linear. Solutions **cannot blow up in finite time** inside $I$.
* **Conclusion:** The local existence interval $I_0$ expands to cover the **entire open interval $I$** where $P(x)$ and $f(x)$ are continuous!

# Extra

[[MAT223 Tutotial 1#^451ae6]]
That is actually a **very sharp observation**. 

In Boundary-Value Problems (BVPs), the interval matters a lot—much more than in Initial-Value Problems (IVPs). Unlike IVPs, **a BVP can have a unique solution, infinitely many solutions, or no solution at all**, even when the differential equation itself is perfectly well-behaved.

Here is what there is to check regarding the interval:

---

### 1. Are the equation's coefficients continuous on the interval?
For the linear equation:
$$y'' + P(x)y' + Q(x)y = g(x)$$

The coefficients here are:
* $P(x) = 0$
* $Q(x) = 4$
* $g(x) = 0$

All coefficients are constants, so they are continuous everywhere on $(-\infty, \infty)$. There are no singular points (no division by zero) anywhere on $\left[\frac{\pi}{8}, \frac{\pi}{6}\right]$.

---

### 2. Does the interval length cause non-uniqueness or non-existence?
This is the main danger with BVPs.

When you substitute boundary conditions into $y(x) = a \sin(2x) + b \cos(2x)$ at $x_1$ and $x_2$:
$$\begin{pmatrix} \sin(2x_1) & \cos(2x_1) \\ \sin(2x_2) & \cos(2x_2) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y(x_1) \\ y(x_2) \end{pmatrix}$$

By Cramer's Rule, a unique solution for $a$ and $b$ exists **if and only if the determinant is non-zero**:

$$\det = \sin(2x_1)\cos(2x_2) - \cos(2x_1)\sin(2x_2) = \sin\big(2(x_1 - x_2)\big) \neq 0$$

This determinant equals zero when:
$$2(x_2 - x_1) = k\pi \implies x_2 - x_1 = \frac{k\pi}{2} \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z})$$

#### Checking our interval:
$$x_2 - x_1 = \frac{\pi}{6} - \frac{\pi}{8} = \frac{4\pi - 3\pi}{24} = \frac{\pi}{24}$$

Since $\frac{\pi}{24}$ is **not** an integer multiple of $\frac{\pi}{2}$, the determinant is non-zero:
$$\sin\left(2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{24}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{12}\right) \neq 0$$

Therefore, a **unique solution is guaranteed**.

---

### What happens if the interval *is* bad? (An example)
Suppose the boundary points were $x = 0$ and $x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ (interval length $= \frac{\pi}{2}$):
* $y(0) = b = 0 \implies b = 0$
* $y\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = a \sin(\pi) + b \cos(\pi) = -b$

If the problem asked for:
* $y(0) = 0$ and $y\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 1$: **No solution exists** (requires $b=0$ and $b=-1$ simultaneously).
* $y(0) = 0$ and $y\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$: **Infinitely many solutions** ($a$ can be any real number).

---

### Summary
In practice:
* When solving by hand, if your linear system for $a$ and $b$ produces a **valid, non-contradictory answer with no division by zero**, you have already implicitly confirmed that the interval is valid and the solution is unique.
* However, keeping the interval length in mind is essential to anticipate whether a BVP might fail to have a unique solution.

## Why Check $y$ and $y'$ vs. Only Checking the DE?

### 1. Verification vs. Solving a BVP / IVP

| Context                | Verification Problem (e.g. $(y-x)y' = y-x+8$)                                                                                                           | Boundary-Value Problem (e.g. $y'' + 4y = 0$)                                                        |
| :--------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The Task**           | "Given a function $y(x)$, **verify** it is a solution."                                                                                                 | "Given the general solution, **find** the constants $a$ and $b$."                                   |
| **Why check $y, y'$?** | By definition, a solution must be differentiable on an open interval $I$. If you do not determine where $y'$ exists, the domain of validity is missing. | The general solution is already given ($y = a\sin 2x + b\cos 2x$). You do not need to re-verify it. |
| **The Interval**       | **Unknown** — you must find where $y$ and its derivatives are well-defined.                                                                             | **Pre-determined** by the boundary conditions: $x \in [\frac{\pi}{8}, \frac{\pi}{6}]$.              |

### 2. Linear ODE Existence & Uniqueness Theorem (1st, 2nd, and $n$-th Order)

^f7347d

The Linear Existence & Uniqueness Theorem applies across **all orders**:

#### A. First-Order Linear ODE (Section 2.3)
$$
y' + P(x)y = f(x)
$$
> [!Theorem] Theorem (1st-Order Linear IVP)
> If $P(x)$ and $f(x)$ are continuous on an open interval $I$ containing $x_0$, then for any initial condition $y(x_0) = y_0$:
> 1. There exists a **unique solution $y(x)$ defined on the entire interval $I$** (no finite-time blowup inside $I$).
> 2. The solution is **continuously differentiable ($C^1$)** on $I$.

#### B. Second-Order Linear ODE (Section 4.1)
$$
y'' + P(x)y' + Q(x)y = g(x)
$$
> [!Theorem] Theorem (2nd-Order Linear IVP)
> If $P(x)$, $Q(x)$, and $g(x)$ are continuous on an open interval $I$ containing $x_0$, then for any initial conditions $y(x_0) = y_0$ and $y'(x_0) = y_1$:
> 1. There exists a **unique solution $y(x)$ defined on the entire interval $I$**.
> 2. The solution is **twice continuously differentiable ($C^2$)** on $I$.

* **Example:** In $y'' + 4y = 0$, $P(x) = 0$, $Q(x) = 4$, and $g(x) = 0$ are continuous everywhere on $(-\infty, \infty)$. The theorem guarantees solutions like $\sin(2x)$ and $\cos(2x)$ exist, are unique for given initial values, and are automatically $C^2$ everywhere without needing manual inspection of $y, y'$ or unexpected blowups.

#### C. General $n$-th Order Linear ODE
For $y^{(n)} + P_{n-1}(x)y^{(n-1)} + \dots + P_0(x)y = g(x)$, if all coefficient functions are continuous on $I$, a unique solution exists on the entire interval $I$ and is **$n$-times continuously differentiable ($C^n$)** on $I$.

> [!remark]
> it is a specialize version of [[1.2 IVP and BHP and Existence and Uniqueness#^b070e8]]

### 3. Why Non-Linear / Radical Equations Require Checking $y$ and $y'$
Consider:
$$(y - x)y' = y - x + 8, \quad y(x) = x + 4\sqrt{x + 2}$$

1. **Restricted Domain & Singular Derivative:**
   $$y' = 1 + \frac{2}{\sqrt{x + 2}}$$
   The derivative has $\sqrt{x+2}$ in the denominator, which is undefined at $x = -2$. Thus, $y(x)$ is only a valid solution on the open interval $I = (-2, \infty)$.
2. **Equation is Non-Linear:**
   The term $(y - x)y'$ makes this non-linear. The linear theorem does not apply, so you cannot rely on the DE alone—you must inspect the function $y(x)$ and its derivatives directly.

---

### Summary Rule of Thumb
* **Verifying a candidate solution:** Inspect $y$ and its derivatives to state the open interval of validity $I$ (crucial whenever $\sqrt{\cdot}$, $\frac{1}{\cdot}$, or $\ln(\cdot)$ appear).
* **Solving a linear BVP / IVP:** Check whether the DE's coefficients have discontinuities or singularities on the interval. If they are continuous, the solutions are automatically smooth on that interval.
