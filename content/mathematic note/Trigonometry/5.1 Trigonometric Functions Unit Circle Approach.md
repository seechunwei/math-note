
## 5.1 The unit circle

The set of points at a distance 1 from the origin is a circle of radius 1 .
Its equation is
$$
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
$$

$$
\begin{align}
\sqrt{ (y-0)^{2}+(x-0)^{2} }=1
\end{align}
$$

$$
\begin{align}
\sqrt{ (y-y_{1})^{2} +(x-x_{1})^{2}}&=r \\ 
(y-y_{1})^{2}+(x-x_{1})^{2}=r^{2}
\end{align}
$$
$(x_{1},y_{1})$ is the center.

### Terminal Points on the Unit Circle

Suppose $t$ is a real number, let $t$ be the distance along the unit circle.
Terminal point is a point that determine by the real number $t$ starting at $(1,0)$. 

In other word angle in radian start from quadrant one $(1,0)$ in anticlockwise.

### Finding terminal point

Notice that we can find any terminal point given the angle by solving 2 equation which is

$$
\begin{align}
x^{2}+y^{2}&=1 \\
y&=(\tan \theta)x
\end{align}
$$

Why $\tan \theta$? because the gradient of the straight line that pass though the terminal point given $\theta$ is 
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{y-0}{x-0}= \frac{y}{x}
\end{align}
$$
which is the 2 perpendicular side of right angle triangle.

### What if we don't want to use trigonometric function here?

Consider the terminal point $P(x,y)$ determined by $t=\frac{\pi}{4}$, it is a line represent by $y=x$ why? because it is the same distance from $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$. Thus, by substitution,

$$
\begin{align}
x^{2}+x^{2}&=1 \\
2x^{2}&=1 \\
x^{2}&=\frac{1}{2} \\
x&=\pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2 }}
\end{align}
$$
Since $P$ lies in Quadrant 1, $x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2 }}$. Thus,
$P\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2 }}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2 }} \right)=P\left( \frac{\sqrt{ 2 }}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{ 2 }}{2} \right)$

How about $t=\frac{\pi}{6}$?

Imagine the terminal point $P(x,y)$ on the unit circle at an angle of $t = \frac{\pi}{6}$ (which is $30^\circ$).

1. Drop a vertical line from $P$ to the x-axis. This forms a right-angled trian
2. gle with a hypotenuse of $1$ (the radius), a base of $x$, and a height of $y$.
    
2. Now, **reflect this triangle across the x-axis** down into Quadrant 4. This gives us a new point, $P'(x, -y)$.
    

Let’s look at the large combined triangle $\triangle OPP'$:

- The total angle at the origin $O$ is now $\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{3}$ (or $60^\circ$).
    
- The two sides $OP$ and $OP'$ are both radii of the unit circle, so their lengths are both $1$.
    

Since $\triangle OPP'$ is an isosceles triangle with a top angle of $60^\circ$, it forced to be an **equilateral triangle**! This means all three sides must be exactly equal to $1$.

### Doing the Math

Because the triangle is equilateral, the entire vertical side spanning from $P$ to $P'$ must have a length of $1$.

The length of that side is simply the distance from $y$ to $-y$:

$$y - (-y) = 1$$

$$2y = 1$$

$$y = \frac{1}{2}$$

Now that we have the exact value for $y$ purely from geometry, we can substitute it back into the equation of the unit circle to find $x$:

$$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$

$$x^2 + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = 1$$

$$x^2 + \frac{1}{4} = 1$$

$$x^2 = \frac{3}{4}$$

Taking the square root, and noting that $P$ lies in Quadrant 1 (so $x$ must be positive):

$$x = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$

### The Final Coordinates

Thus, the terminal point for $t = \frac{\pi}{6}$ is:

$$P\left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2} \right)$$

$\cos \theta$ is the y-coordinate

In the world of mathematics, the **$30^\circ-60^\circ-90^\circ$ triangle** is considered "special" because the ratios of its sides are always constant and involve clean, algebraic values. For anyone interested in the logic behind trigonometry, this triangle is the "source code" for the unit circle values you see for $\pi/6$ and $\pi/3$.

---

## 1. The Fundamental Ratio

In any $30^\circ-60^\circ-90^\circ$ triangle, the sides always follow a specific proportion. If we let the shortest side (opposite the $30^\circ$ angle) be $x$, the sides are:

- **Short Leg ($30^\circ$):** $x$
    
- **Long Leg ($60^\circ$):** $x\sqrt{3}$
    
- **Hypotenuse ($90^\circ$):** $2x$
    

---

## 2. The Geometric Derivation (The "Why")

The reason these ratios exist isn't arbitrary; it comes from the symmetry of an **equilateral triangle**.

1. **Start with an equilateral triangle** where every side has a length of **2** and every angle is **$60^\circ$**.
    
2. **Drop an altitude** (a straight line) from the top vertex to the base.
    
3. Because an equilateral triangle is perfectly symmetrical, this altitude does two things:
    
    - It bisects the top angle into two **$30^\circ$** angles.
        
    - It bisects the base into two segments of length **1**.
        

Now, look at one of the resulting right triangles. You have a hypotenuse of **2** and a base (short leg) of **1**. To find the altitude (long leg), we use the **Pythagorean Theorem**:

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

$$1^2 + (\text{altitude})^2 = 2^2$$

$$1 + (\text{altitude})^2 = 4$$

$$(\text{altitude})^2 = 3$$

$$\text{altitude} = \sqrt{3}$$

This confirms the ratio: **$1 : \sqrt{3} : 2$**.

---

## 3. Connection to the Unit Circle

This triangle is exactly what you were using to find the coordinates for $\pi/6$.

On a **Unit Circle**, the hypotenuse must be **1** (the radius). To turn our $1 : \sqrt{3} : 2$ triangle into a "Unit" triangle, we simply divide every side by **2**:

- **Hypotenuse:** $2 / 2 = \mathbf{1}$
    
- **Short Leg (opposite $30^\circ$):** $\mathbf{1/2}$ (This is your **y-coordinate** or $\sin(\pi/6)$)
    
- **Long Leg (opposite $60^\circ$):** $\mathbf{\sqrt{3}/2}$ (This is your **x-coordinate** or $\cos(\pi/6)$)

### The Reference Number

find a terminal point in any quadrant we need only know the “corresponding” terminal point in the first quadrant and change the sign. Thus, we use the idea of the reference number to help us find terminal points.

==Definition==

Let $t$ be a real number. The reference number $\tilde{t}$ associated with $t$ is the shortest distance along the unit circle between the terminal point determined by $t$ and the x-axis.


Can we define the reference number $\bar{t}$ to be the nearest number from $y$-axis?
Cannot, because the number $t$ start from $x-$axis.
