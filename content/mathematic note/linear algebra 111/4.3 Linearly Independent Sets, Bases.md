> [!info] The Spanning Set Theorem
Let $S=\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}$ be a set in vector space $V$, and let $H=Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}$.
a) If one of the vectors in $S$ say $v_{k}$ is a linear combination of the remaining vectors in $S$, then the set formed from $S$ by removing $v_{k}$ still spans $H$.
b) If $H \neq \{ 0 \}$, some subset of $S$ is a basis for $H$

Proof:
a)
Suppose $v_{p}$ is a linear combination of the remaining vectors in $S$. We need to show that $Span\{ v_{1},\dots v_{p} \}=Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p-1} \}$.
It is clear that $Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p-1} \}\subseteq Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}$. Thus, we only need to show that $Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}\subseteq Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p-1} \}$. By supposition,

$$
v_{p}=c_{1}v_{1}+\dots c_{p-1}v_{p-1}
$$
for some real scalar $c_{1},\dots,c_{p-1}$. For any $x \in Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}$, we can write

$$
x=a_{1}v_{1}+\dots+a_{p-1}v_{p-1}+a_{p}v_{p}
$$

By substitution

$$
\begin{align}
x&=a_{1}v_{1}+\dots+a_{p-1}v_{p-1}+a_{p}(c_{1}v_{1}+\dots c_{p-1}v_{p-1}) \\
&=(a_{1}+a_{p}c_{1})v_{1}+\dots+(a_{p-1}+a_{p}c_{p-1})v_{p-1}
\end{align}
$$

Thus, $x \in Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p-1} \}$. Thus, $$
Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p} \}\subseteq Span\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{p-1} \}
$$
It follow that $H=Span\{ v_{1},\dots v_{p-1} \}$.


b)
Suppose $S$ is linearly independent, then $S$ is a basis for $H$. Suppose $S$ is linearly dependent, that is there exist one vector in the set is a linear combination of other vector. By part a),  let $S'$ be the set that without that vector, then $S'$ still span $H$ , we can keep on repeating this process until the spanning set is linearly independent, hence it is a basis for $H$.

If the spanning set is reduced to one vector, then that sole vector is nonzero (By our supposition) which implies the set is linearly independent. Hence, the set is a basis for $H$.

> [!cite] Remark
> In higher-level pure mathematics, we actually _do_ say that the basis for the zero subspace $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ is the empty set $\emptyset$, because:
>
>- Vacuously, $\emptyset$ is linearly independent.
  >  
>- By convention, $\text{span}(\emptyset) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

### Row space

> [!info] Theorem
If two matrices $A$ and $B$ are row equivalent, then their row spaces are the same.
>
If $B$ is in echelon form, the nonzero rows of $B$ form a basis for the row space of $A$ as well as for that of $B$.

Proof:
If $B$ is obtained from $A$ by row operations, the rows of $B$ are linear combinations of rows of $A$. Thus it follow that any linear combination of rows of $B$ is also linear combination of rows of $A$. Hence, $Row(B)\subseteq Row(A)$.

Since row operations are reversible, the same argument shows that $Row(A)\subseteq Row(B)$. Therefore, the row spaces are the same.

Proof for second part:
Let $r_{1},\dots r_{k}$ be the nonzero rows of $B$. Consider the equation 

$$
c_{1}r_{1}+\dots+c_{k}r_{k}=0
$$
**Look at the first pivot column:** Let's say row 1 has its pivot (a non-zero number) in column $j_1$. By the definition of echelon form, every single row below row 1 must have a $0$ in column $j_1$

f we look only at the $j_1$-th component of our vector equation, it simplifies beautifully:

$$c_1 \cdot (\text{pivot}) + c_2 \cdot 0 + c_3 \cdot 0 + \dots + c_k \cdot 0 = 0$$

$$c_1 \cdot (\text{pivot}) = 0$$

Since the pivot is non-zero, **$c_1$ must equal $0$**.
By induction, traveling down the staircase column by column, every single coefficient is forced to be zero:

$$c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$$

Because the trivial solution is the _only_ solution, the non-zero rows are strictly linearly independent.

Therefore, the nonzero rows of $B$ form a basis for the row space of $B$, and also for the row space of $A$.

> [!info] Theorem
If $A$ and $B$ are two row equivalent matrices, then the columns of $A$ have exactly the same linear dependence relationships as the columns of $B$.

Proof:
Let $a_{1},\dots ,a_{n}$ be the columns of $A$ and $b_{1},\dots,b_{n}$ be the columns of $B$. The solution of $Ax=0$ 

$$
c_{1}a_{1}+\dots+c_{n}a_{n}=0
$$
is the same as the solution of $Bx=0$ denoted by

$$
c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n}=0
$$

Thus, the columns of $B$ have the same linear dependence relationship as the columns of $A$.

> [!info] Theorem
The pivot columns of a matrix $A$ form a basis for $ColA$.

To prove that the pivot columns of a matrix $A$ form a basis for $\text{Col}(A)$, we must show two things, just like any other basis proof:

1. The pivot columns of $A$ are **linearly independent**.
    
2. The pivot columns of $A$ **span** $\text{Col}(A)$.

Proof:
Let’s let $A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_n \end{bmatrix}$ and let $B = \text{RREF}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & \dots & b_n \end{bmatrix}$.

Let’s look at the pivot columns of the reduced matrix $B$. Because $B$ is in reduced row echelon form, its pivot columns are the standard basis vectors of $\mathbb{R}^m$:

$$e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, e_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \dots$$

These vectors are linearly independent.  Thus the pivot columns of $A$ are linearly independent as well by the earlier theorem above.

Since we know that $Col(A)=Span\{ a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n} \}$
Then by the Spanning Set Theorem, the non pivot columns of $A$ may be discarded from the spanning set of $ColA$. Since these pivot columns are linearly independent, they form basis for $Col(A)$.
