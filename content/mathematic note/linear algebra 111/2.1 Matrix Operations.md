If $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, it is a matrix with  $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Each entry in $A$ is denoted by $a_{ij}$ and it's called the $(i,j)-entry$ of $A$.

The column are denoted by $a_{1},\dots,a_{n}$

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
a_{1} & a_{2} & \dots & a_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

==Diagonal entries==
It is the entry $a_{ij}$ where $i=j$.

==Diagonal matrix==
It is a square matrix whose $a_{ij}=0$ when $i\neq j$. An example is an identity matrix.

### 2. The Algebraic Purpose: Scaling

The most important "job" of a diagonal matrix is to perform **scaling** on the basis vectors.

- If $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, a diagonal matrix $D$ simply stretches or shrinks each axis by a factor $d_i$.
    
- If the matrix were rectangular (e.g., $m \times n$), the transformation would be mapping a vector from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to a different space $\mathbb{R}^m$.
    

A diagonal matrix is meant to represent a transformation where the basis vectors are **eigenvectors**. Since the concept of eigenvalues and eigenvectors ($\mathbf{Ax} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$) is only defined for operators mapping a space back to itself, the matrix must be square.

==Sum and scalar==

Two matrix is equal iff
1) same size
2) each column are equal $\implies$ each entries are equal

The sum $A+B$ is the matrix whose columns are the sum of corresponding columns in $A$ and $B$. Or $(A)_{ij}+(B)_{ij}$ for all $i,j$.

If $r$ is a scalar and $A$ is a matrix, then the scalar multiple $rA$ is the matrix whose columns are r times the corresponding columns in $A$.

==Theorem==
Let $A,B$ and $C$ be matrices of the same size, and let $r$ and $s$ be scalars.
a)$A+B=B+A$
b) $(A+B)+C=A+(B+C)$
c) $A+0=A$
d) $r(A+B)=rA+rB$
e) $(r+s)A=rA+rB$
f) $r(sA)=(rs)A$

==Matrix Multiplication

We can think matrix multiplication as composition of linear transformation.

$A(Bx)=(AB)x$

Notice that the composition of linear transformation is a linear transformation as well.

Given $A$ is $m\times n$ and $B$ is $n\times p$ and $x \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$
$Bx=x_{1}b_{1}+\dots.x_{p}b_{p}$

Thus, by linearity of multiplication of $A$

$$
\begin{align}
A(Bx)&=A(x_{1}b_{1})+\dots+A(x_{p}b_{p}) \\
&=x_{1}(Ab_{1})+\dots x_{p}(Ab_{p}) \\
&=\begin{bmatrix}
Ab_{1} & \dots & Ab_{p}
\end{bmatrix}x
\end{align}
$$

### The transpose of a matrix

$$
(A^{T})_{ij}=(A)_{ji}
$$


==Theorem==
a) $(A^{T})^{T}=A$
b) $(A+B)^{T}=A^{T}+B^{T}$
c) For every scalar $r$, $(rA)^{T}=rA^{T}$
d) $(AB)^{T}=B^{T}A^{T}$

Proof of d):

$$
\begin{align}
((AB)^{T})_{ij}&=(AB)_{ji} \\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n} (A)_{jk}(B)_{ki}
\end{align}
$$

By definition $(B^{T})_{ij}=(B)_{ji}$ and $(A^{T})_{ij}=(A)_{ji}$.

$$
\begin{align}
(B^{T}A^{T})_{ij}&=\sum_{k=1}^{n} (B^{T})_{ik}(A^{T})_{kj} \\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(B)_{ki}(A)_{jk} \\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n} (A)_{jk}(B)_{ki} 
\end{align}
$$
Q.E.D

==Remark==
Notice that the size of $(AB)$ is row of $A$ $\times$ column of $B$. Thus, $(AB)^{T}$ is column of $B$ $\times$ row of $A$. Thus, it is $B^{T}A^{T}$.
