
Vectors space denoted by $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is all the vector with $n$ real component.

Within all vector space:
We can add any two vectors, and we can multiply all vectors by scalars. In other words, we can take linear combinations.

Normally our vectors belong to one of the spaces $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

Example:
1) The infinite dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$, its vector have infinity many components, $x=(1,2,3,\dots)$. But the law $x+y$ and $c x$ stay unchanged.

2) The space of $3\times 2$ matrices. In this case the space is almost the same as $\mathbb{R}^{6}$. It is closed under addition and multiplication.

3) The space of functions $f(x)$.??


But we are also concern about the space that are inside the standard spaces $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

For example a plane that pass though origin in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ is a vector space on its own right. (all linear combination of 2 $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ vectors). It is a subspace of the original space $\mathbb{R}^{3}$

> [!note] Definition
A subspace of a vector space is a non empty subset that satisfy the requirement for a vector space: Linear combinations stay in subspace.

A subspace is a subset that is “closed” under addition and scalar multiplication.

### The Column Space of $A$

The column space and the null space of a matrix A. The column space contains all linear combinations of the columns of A. It is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{m}$.

With m>n we have more equations than unknowns—and usually there will be no solution. The system will be solvable only for a very “thin” subset of all possible b’s.

Why?
It is because the number of row $m$, determine the dimension of vector, thus if we only have 4 vector in $\mathbb{R}^{5}$, the spanning set does not span $\mathbb{R}^{5}$.

> [!info]
> The system $Ax=b$ is solvable if and only if the vector $b$ can be expressed as linear combination of column of $A$. IN other world, $b$ is in column space.

For example,
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
5 & 4 \\
2 & 4
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
u \\
v
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
b_{1} \\
b_{2} \\
b_{3}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

There are 3 equation with 2 variable. It can be expressed as

$$
u\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
5 \\
2
\end{bmatrix}+v\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
4 \\
4
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
b_{1} \\
b_{2} \\
b_{3}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice that the spanning set of these 2 vectors is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ pass though the origin. Thus, $Ax=b$ is only solvable if $b$ lies in the plane.

If $b$ is off the plane, $Ax=b$ has no solution. This plane is called column space of $A$ denoted by $C(A)$.

Why it is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{m}$?
It is because
1) $A(x+x')=b+b'$
2) $A(cx)=c b$

The smallest possible column space is the zero matrix $A=0$. $C(A)$ is somewhere between the zero space and the whole space $\mathbb{R}^{m}$.

### The Nullspace of A

> [!info]
> The nullspace of $A$ is the linear combination of column of $A$ such that $Ax=0$. It is denoted by $N(A)$. It is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

1) $A(x+x')=0$
2) $A(cx)=0$

Notice that it is about homogeneous system. Trivial solution or non-trivial solution(free variable). The solution of $Ax=0$ is always a subspace.

If solution has only trivial solution, it means that the column are linear independent. (This implies no free variable$\implies$ any row of $A$ is not linear combination of another rows $\implies$ have unique solution)

We want to be able, for any system Ax=b, to find C(A) and N(A): all attainable right-hand sides b and all solutions to Ax=0.

The vectors b are in the column space and the vectors x are in the nullspace.
