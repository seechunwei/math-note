We can construct second solution $y_{2}$ of a homogeneous equation provided that we know a nontrivial solution $y_{1}$ of the DE.

The basic idea is that the DE can be reduced to a linear first-order DE by means of a substitution.

Consider the second order DE below:
$$
\begin{align}
a_{2}(x)y''+a_{1}(x)y'+a_{0}(x)y=0 \tag*{(1)}
\end{align}
$$

Suppose that $y_{1}$ denotes a nontrivial solution of (1) on an interval $I$. We want to seek a solution $y_{2}$ where $y_{1},y_{2}$ are linearly independent. 

From [[Chapter 4 Higher-order DE 4.1  Preliminary theory—linear equations#^28fd31]], we know that $y_{2}(x)=\mu(x)y_{1}(x)$. Hence, function $u(x)$ can be found by substituting $y_{2}(x)=u(x)y_{1}(x)$ into DE.

### Example
Given that $y_{1}=e^{x}$ is a solution of $y''-y=0$ on the interval $(-\infty,\infty)$, use reduction of order to find a second solution $y_{2}$.

Let $y=u(x)y_{1}(x)\implies y=ue^{x}$. Hence, 

$$
\begin{align}
y'&=u'(x)y_{1}(x)+u(x)y_{1}'(x) \\
&=u'e^{x}+ue^{x}
\end{align}
$$
and
$$
\begin{align}
y''&= u''e^{x}+u'e^{x}+u'e^{x}+ue^{x} \\
&=u''e^{x}+2u'e^{x}+ue^{x}
\end{align}
$$

Hence substitute in the DE:
$$
\begin{align}
y''-y&=e^{x}(u''+2u')=0 \\
\end{align}
$$

Since $e^{x} \neq 0$, hence $u''+2u'=0$. Let $w=u'$, this linear second-order equation in $u$ becomes $w'+2w=0$. Solve for this we get $w=u'=c_{1}e^{-2x}$. Integrating again we get
$$
u= -\frac{1}{2}c_{1}e^{-2x}+c_{2}
$$
Thus,
$$
y=u(x)e^{x}= -\frac{c_{1}}{2}e^{-x}+c_{2}e^{x}
$$

By choosing $c_{2}=0$ and $c_{1}=-2$ (make it simple since it work for all $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$), we obtain $y_{2}=e^{-x}$. $\blacksquare$

> [!remark]
> **Can we choose $c_{1} = 0$ and $c_{2} = 0$?**
> No, we must choose $c_{1} \neq 0$:
> 1. **Linear independence:** If $c_{1} = 0$ and $c_{2} = 0$, we get $y = 0$. The trivial solution $y = 0$ is always linearly dependent on $y_{1}$ and cannot be a second solution $y_{2}$.
> 2. **Division by $w$:** When separating variables, we divided by $w$, which requires $w \neq 0$ (so $c_{1} \neq 0$). If $c_{1} = 0$, then $w = u' = 0 \implies u = \text{constant}$, which only reproduces a constant multiple of $y_{1}$.
> 
> Setting $c_{2} = 0$ is standard because the term $c_{2}e^{x}$ is already a multiple of $y_{1}$, so it contributes no new independent solution.

## General Case

Suppose we divide by $a_{2}(x)$ to put equation (1) in the standard form
$$
\begin{align}
y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0 \tag*{(3)}
\end{align}
$$
where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are continuous on some interval $I$. Assume $y_{1}(x)$ is one of the solution for (3), if we define $y=u(x)y_{1}(x)$, it follow that

$$
\begin{gathered}
y'=u'y_{1}+uy_{1}' 
\end{gathered}
$$
and
$$
\begin{align}
y''&= u''y_{1}+u'y_{1}'+u'y_{1}'+uy_{1}'' \\
&=u''y_{1}+2u'y_{1}'+uy_{1}''
\end{align}
$$

Substitute into (3),

$$
\begin{align}
y''+Py'+Qy &= u''y_{1}+2u'y_{1}'+uy_{1}''+P(u'y_{1}+uy_{1}' )+Q(uy_{1}) \\
&=u''y_{1}+(2y_{1}'+Py_{1})u'+ (y_{1}''+Py_{1}'+Qy_{1})u \\
\end{align}
$$

Note that $(y''+Py_{1}'+Qy_{1})=0$. Hence,

$$
\begin{align}
u''y_{1}+(2y_{1}'+Py_{1})u'&=0
\end{align}
$$

Let $w=u'$, we obtain
$$
w'y_{1}+(2y_{1}'+Py_{1})w=0
$$
Note that it is both linear and separable. Hence,

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{w} w'&= \frac{-Py_{1}-2y_{1}'}{y_{1}} \\
\ln \lvert w \rvert&= \int -P- \frac{2y_{1}'}{y_{1}} \, dx  \\
&= -\int P  \, dx- 2 \ln \lvert y_{1} \rvert \\
&= -\int P \, dx - \ln \left\lvert  \frac{1}{y_{1}^{2}}  \right\rvert \\
w&= \frac{e^{\int -P(x) \, dx }}{y_{1}^{2}}
\end{align}
$$

> [!remark]
> We integrate with respect to $x$, because $P$ and $y_{1}$ is in term of $x$ and $w=u'(x)$ is in term of $x$. $w'=\frac{dw}{dx}$.

Since $w=u'$
$$
u(x)=\int \frac{e^{-\int P(x) \, dx }}{(y_{1}(x))^{2}} \, dx 
$$

From $y=u(x)y_{1}(x)$, we have

$$
y_{2}=y_{1}(x) \int \frac{e^{-\int P(x) \, dx }}{(y_{1}(x))^{2}} \, dx
$$

> [!remark]
> We omit the $c_{1}$, $c_{2}$ here because eventually we will choose the the constant that make the function look simple
