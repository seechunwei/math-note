
> [!theorem] The Characteristic Equation
> A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ iff $\lambda$ satisfies the characteristic equation
> 
> $$
> \det(A-\lambda I)=0
> $$

> [!question]
> Why?
> Recall that a scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ iff the equation
> 
> $$
> (A-\lambda I)x=0
> $$
> has nontrivial solution. By Invertible Matrix Theorem, the matrix $A-\lambda I$ is not invertible. Thus, it implies that
> 
> $$
> \det(A-\lambda I)=0
> $$

> [!remark]
> It can be shown that $\det(A-\lambda I)$ is in fact a polynomial of degree $n$, written in the form
> 
> $$
> \det(A-\lambda I)=p(\lambda)=(-1)^{n}\lambda^{n}+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\dots+a_{1}\lambda+a_{0}
> $$
> 
> and is called the characteristic polynomial of  $A$.

Example:
Determine the eigenvalue of the matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

Solve the characteristic equation:

$$
\begin{align}
\det(A-\lambda I)&=0 \\
\det \begin{bmatrix}
3-\lambda & -2 \\
1 & -\lambda
\end{bmatrix}&=0 \\
-\lambda(3-\lambda)-(-2)(1)&=0 \\
\lambda^{2}-3\lambda+2&=0 \\
(\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)&=0
\end{align}
$$
Hence, the eigenvalues of $A$ are $2$ and $1$.

Example:
Determine the eigenvalues of the matrix
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & -2 \\
0 & 0 & 0 \\
-2 & 0 & 4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Solving for the characteristic equation:

$$
\begin{align}
\det(A-\lambda I)&=0 \\
\det \begin{bmatrix}
1-\lambda & 0 & -2 \\
0 & -\lambda & 0 \\
-2 & 0 & 4-\lambda
\end{bmatrix}&=0 \\
\end{align}
$$

Cofactor expansion across columns 2

$$
\begin{align}
\det(A-\lambda I)&=(-1)^{2}(-\lambda) \det \begin{bmatrix}
1-\lambda & -2 \\
-2 & 4-\lambda
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=(-\lambda)[(1-\lambda)(4-\lambda)-(-2)(-2)] \\
&=(-\lambda)(\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+4-4) \\
&=(-\lambda)(\lambda^{2}-5\lambda) \\
&=-\lambda^{2}(\lambda-5)
\end{align}
$$

So the eigenvalues of $A$ are $0$ and $5$.

> [!definition]
> Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix and $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. Then the **algebraic multiplicity** of $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial of $A$.
> 

Example:
For matrix earlier
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & -2 \\
0 & 0 & 0 \\
-2 & 0 & 4
\end{bmatrix}
$$
its eigenvalues $\lambda=0$ and $\lambda=5$ respectively have the algebraic multiplicity $2$ and $1$.

# Similar matrices

> [!definition]
> Let $A$ and $B$ be two $n\times n$ matrices. Then $A$ is **similar** to $B$ if there is an invertible matrix $P$ such that $A=PBP^{-1}$. The change from $A$ into $P^{-1}AP$ is called a **similarity transformation**

> [!remark]
> a) If writing $Q=P^{-1}$, then $A=PBP^{-1}$ becomes $QAQ^{-1}=B$, that is, $B$ is similar $A$. Therefore, we always just say $A$ and $B$ are similar.
> 
> b) When $A$ and $B$ satisfy $AP=PB$ for some matrix $P$, this does not imply $A$ and $B$ are similar, unless $P$ is invertible.

> [!theorem]
> If $n\times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$ are similar, then they have the same characteristic polynomial and hence the same eigenvalues (with the same multiplicities).

> [!remark]
> The converse of the theorem is false, 2 $n\times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$ having the same eigenvalues are not necessarily similar.

Proof:
Suppose that $A$ and $B$ are $n\times n$ matrices and they are similar. Then $B=P^{-1}AP$ and

$$
\begin{align}
B-\lambda I&=P^{-1}AP-\lambda P^{-1}P \\
&=P^{-1}AP-P^{-1}(\lambda I)P \\
&=P^{-1}(A-\lambda I)P
\end{align}
$$

so,

$$
\begin{align}
\det(B-\lambda I)&=\det(P^{-1}(A-\lambda I)P) \\
&=\det P^{-1}\cdot \det(A-\lambda I)\cdot \det P \\
&=\det(A-\lambda I) && \blacksquare
\end{align}
$$

> [!question]
> Why showing $\det(B-\lambda I)=\det(A-\lambda I)$ implies that they have same characteristic polynomial and same eigenvalues (with same multiplicity)?
> 
> In this context, $\det(A - \lambda I)$ isn't just a formula that spits out a single number; **it is the literal definition of the characteristic polynomial itself.**
