
> [!definition]
> An eigenvector of an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is a nonzero vector $x$ such that $Ax=\lambda x$ for some scalar $\lambda$. A scalar $\lambda$ is called an eigenvalue of $A$ if there is a nontrivial solution $x$ of $Ax=\lambda x$: such an $x$ is called eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda$.

Think of $A$ as a linear transformation $x\mapsto Ax$, some of the vector map by $A$ to its multiple. It stretch or dilates the vector in same direction.

Why eigenvector is a nontrivial solution $x$ of $Ax=\lambda x$ because $x=0$ is the trivial solution for $Ax=\lambda x$, because $0=c 0$ for any real scalar $c$.

How to determine if a given vector is an eigenvector of a matrix?

Example:
Let
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 6 \\
5 & 2
\end{bmatrix} ,u=\begin{bmatrix}
6 \\
-5
\end{bmatrix}, v=\begin{bmatrix}
3 \\
-2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
Au=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 6 \\
5 & 2
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
6 \\
-5
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
-24 \\
20
\end{bmatrix}=-4u
$$
$$
Av=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 6 \\
5 & 2
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
3 \\
-2
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
-9 \\
11
\end{bmatrix}\neq\lambda v
$$

Thus, $u$ is an eigen vector corresponding to an eigenvalue $(-4)$, but $v$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$.

How to determine if a given value is an eigenvalue of a matrix?

Show that $7$ is an eigenvalue of matrix $A$ in example above:

The scalar $7$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ iff there exist nontrivial solution for 

$$
\begin{align}
Ax=7x&&(1)
\end{align}
$$

We need to solve for 

$$
\begin{align}
(A-7I)x=0 &&(2)
\end{align}
$$

> [!question]
> Why it is $7I$? because then only we can perform the subtraction of matrix , we cannot solve for $A-7$. In particularly,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align}
> Ax&=7x \\
> Ax&=7Ix \\
> Ax-7Ix&=0 \\
> (A-7I)x&=0
> \end{align}
> $$
> 


Thus, 

$$
A-7I= \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 6 \\
6 & 2
\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}
7 & 0 \\
0 & 7
\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}
-6 & 6 \\
5 & -5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The columns of $A-7I$ are obviously linearly dependent, so it has nontrivial solutions.  Thus, 7 is an eigenvalue of $A$. To find the corresponding eigenvectors, use row operations:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
-6 & 6 & 0 \\
5 & -5 & 0
\end{bmatrix}\sim \begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
The general solution set is $x=x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$. Each vector in this form is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda=7$.

> [!remark]
> Notice that (1) and (2) are equivalent to the statement below
> 
> $$
> (A-\lambda I)x=0
> $$
> It is obvious that the solution set is the null space of $A-\lambda I$. So this set is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and it is called eigenspace of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$.



> [!theorem] Theorem 1
> The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are the entries on its main diagonal

> [!theorem] Theorem 2
> If $v_{1},\dots,v_{r}$ are eigenvectors that correspond to distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}$ of an $n\times n$ matrix $A$, then the set $\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{r} \}$ is linearly independent.

Proof:
## Method 1: Induction
Prove
$$c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_r v_r = 0$$
forces all the scalars to be zero: $c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_r = 0$.

$$
\begin{align}
A(c_{1}v_{1})+\dots+A(c_{k+1}v_{k+1})&=0 \\
\end{align}
$$
Why we multiply $A$? because we want to use the property of eigenvector. (The eigenvector is always with respect to a matrix)
1) Haven't use the property of eigenvector and eigenvalue
2) Didn't understand that $Av_{i}=\lambda_{i} v_{i}$

And since each $v_i$ is an eigenvector, we can substitute $A v_i = \lambda_i v_i$. This transforms our equation into:

$$c_1 \lambda_1 v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 v_2 + \dots + c_{k+1} \lambda_{k+1} v_{k+1} = 0 \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Now we have two equations:

- **Equation 1:** $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_{k+1} v_{k+1} = 0$
    
- **Equation 2:** $c_1 \lambda_1 v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 v_2 + \dots + c_{k+1} \lambda_{k+1} v_{k+1} = 0$
What we can do to eliminate $v_{k+1}$ so that we can use our inductive hypothesis?

By multiplying Equation 1 by $\lambda_{k+1}$ and subtracting it from Equation 2, the $v_{k+1}$ terms cancel out completely.

Here is what we get when we group the remaining terms:

$$c_1(\lambda_1 - \lambda_{k+1})v_1 + c_2(\lambda_2 - \lambda_{k+1})v_2 + \dots + c_k(\lambda_k - \lambda_{k+1})v_k = 0$$

By inductive hypothesis, $c_{i}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k+1})=0$ . By zero product property, one of the factor must equal to 0, since $\lambda_i$ and $\lambda_{k+1}$ are distinct eigenvalues, it follow that $\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k+1}\neq 0$. Thus, $c_{i}=0$

$$c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$$

Let's look back at our original linear combination equation (**Equation 1**):

$$c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_k v_k + c_{k+1} v_{k+1} = 0$$

This implies that
$$
c_{k+1}v_{k+1}=0
$$
Since $v_{k+1}\neq 0$, it follow that $c_{k+1}=0$ $\blacksquare$

## Method 2: Contradiction

So, we assume that the set of eigenvectors $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_r\}$ is actually **linearly dependent**

If a set of vectors is linearly dependent, it means at least one vector in the set can be written as a linear combination of the vectors that come before it. Let's pick the first vector where this happens, and call it $v_k$ (where $k \le r$).

This means we can write:

$$v_k = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_{k-1} v_{k-1} \quad \text{--- (Equation A)}$$

Multiply the equation with matrix $A$

$$
Av_{k}=Ac_{1}v_{1}+\dots+Ac_{k-1}v_{k-1}
$$

$$\lambda_k v_k = c_1 \lambda_1 v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 v_2 + \dots + c_{k-1} \lambda_{k-1} v_{k-1} \quad \text{--- (Equation B)}$$

Multiply equation $A$ with $\lambda_{k}$ and subtract form equation $B$:

$$
0=c_{1}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k})v_{1}+\dots+c_{k-1}(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k})v_{k-1}
$$

Now, look at what this equation is saying. It is a linear combination of the vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}\}$ that equals the zero vector.

But remember how we set up this proof: we chose $v_k$ to be the _first_ vector that depended on the previous ones. That means the set of vectors before it, **$\{v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}\}$, must be linearly independent.**

Because $\{v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}\}$ is a linearly independent set, the only way their linear combination can equal zero is if every single coefficient is exactly zero:

$$c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_k) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, k-1$$

Since all the eigenvalues are distinct, we know that $(\lambda_i - \lambda_k) \neq 0$. Therefore, it must be true that:

$$c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_{k-1} = 0$$

If all these coefficients $c_i$ are zero, let's plug them back into our very first assumption (**Equation A**):

$$v_k = 0v_1 + 0v_2 + \dots + 0v_{k-1} \implies v_k = 0$$

This means $v_k$ must be the zero vector. But $v_k$ is an **eigenvector**, and by definition, eigenvectors can never be the zero vector!

This is our contradiction. Our original assumption—that the set of eigenvectors is linearly dependent—must be false. Therefore, the set $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ is linearly independent. $\blacksquare$

## Why we need to study eigenvectors ?

Consider the difference equation

$$
\begin{align}
x_{k+1}=Ax_{k} &&(k=0,1,2,\dots)
\end{align}
$$

What it means: It is a sequence of vector $\{ x_{k} \}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ where you have a starting vector $x_{0}$, to find $x_{1}$ we multiply $Ax_{0}=x_{1}$. To find $x_{2}$ we multiply $A$ again $A^{2}x_{0}=x_{2}$.

The Problem: What if i want to know when $k=100$ ? It is impossible to calculate $A^{100}x_{0}$.

The Goal: Find an explicit formula for $x_{k}$ that only depends on the step $k$ and the initial state $x_{0}$.

The text states that the simplest way to build a solution is to choose an initial vector $x_0$ that happens to be an **eigenvector** of $A$ with a corresponding eigenvalue $\lambda$.

Then $Ax_{0}=\lambda x_{0}$.
- $x_{1}=Ax_{0}=\lambda x_{0}$
- $x_{2}=A^{2}x_{0}=\lambda(Ax_{0})=\lambda^{2}x_{0}$
- $x_3 = A x_2 = A(\lambda^2 x_0) = \lambda^2 (A x_0) = \lambda^2 (\lambda x_0) = \lambda^3 x_0$
By induction, for any step $k$, we get equation (9):

$$x_k = \lambda^k x_0$$

**The Big Realization:** Matrix multiplication $A^k x_0$ (which is hard) has been completely replaced by standard scalar exponentiation $\lambda^k x_0$ (which is incredibly easy

The math line at the bottom proves why this explicit formula works by plugging $x_k = \lambda^k x_0$ back into the original difference equation $A x_k = x_{k+1}$:

$$A x_k = A(\lambda^k x_0) = \lambda^k (A x_0) = \lambda^k (\lambda x_0) = \lambda^{k+1} x_0 = x_{k+1}$$

Because the left side ($A x_k$) matches the right side ($x_{k+1}$), the formula is verified as a valid solution.

What if the $x_{0}$ is not a eigenvector of $A$? Since the eigenvectors of an $n\times n$ matrix often form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, you can write any random $x_{0}$ as a linear combination of eigenvectors:

$$
x_{0}=c_{1}v_{1}+\dots+c_{n}v_{n}
$$
Because the system is linear, you can apply the shortcut to each eigenvector component individually. The general, complete explicit solution for any state $k$ becomes:

$$x_k = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k v_2 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n^k v_n$$

## How to find eigenvalue of a general matrix $A$?
because theorem 1 only can applied to triangular matrix.

Example;
Given
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
3 & -2 \\
1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Find the eigenvalue of $A$. By definition,  $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ iff

$$
(A-\lambda)x=0
$$
have non trivial solution. We row reduce the augmented matrix below:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A-\lambda & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\begin{bmatrix}
3-\lambda & -2 & 0 \\
1 & -\lambda & 0
\end{bmatrix}&\xrightarrow[]{R_{1}^{2}} \begin{bmatrix}
1 & -\lambda  & 0\\
3-\lambda & -2 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \\
&\xrightarrow[]{R_{2}^{1}(\lambda-3)} \begin{bmatrix}
1 & -\lambda  & 0\\
0 & -\lambda^{2}+3\lambda-2 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

$(A-\lambda)x=0$ has non-trivial solution iff $-\lambda^{2}+3\lambda-2=0$. Since $-\lambda^{2}+3\lambda-2=(-\lambda+1)(\lambda-2)$ , hence we have $\lambda=1$ or $\lambda=2$.

For $\lambda=1$,
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -\lambda  & 0\\
0 & -\lambda^{2}+3\lambda-2 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1  & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Thus the solution set is

$$
x=\text{Span}\{ \begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix} \}
$$
For $\lambda=2$ we can get $x=\text{Span}\left\{  \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}  \right\}$
These are the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda$.

> [!definition]
> Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix and $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $A$. Then, the solutions set of
> $$
> (A-\lambda I)x=0
> $$
> which is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, is called the **eigenspace** of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$, and is denoted $V_{\lambda}$.
> 
> The dimension of the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda$ is called the **geometric** **multiplicity** of $\lambda$.

Thus, from example earlier dim$V_{1}=1$ and dim$V_{2}=1$.

# Properties of Eigenvalues

> [!theorem]
> The number $0$ is an eigenvalue of a square matrix $A$ iff  $A$ is singular (that is, $A$ is not invertible)


^0ae03d

Proof:
Suppose that $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. Then $Ax=0x=0$ has nontrivial solution $x$. Thus,$A$ is singular. Conversely, suppose $A$ is singular then $Ax=0=0x$ have nontrivial solution, by definition $0$ is the eigenvalue of $A$. $\blacksquare$

> This theorem can be added to Invertible Matrix Theorem
