
The change of coordinates matrix from $B$ to $C$

Given two bases $B=\{ b_{1},b_{2} \}$ and $C=c_{1},c_{2}$ for a vector space $V$ such that

$$
b_{1}=4c_{1}+c_{2} \text{ and }b_{2}=-6c_{1}+c_{2}
$$

Thus,

$$
[b_{1}]_{C}=\begin{bmatrix}
4 \\
1
\end{bmatrix} \text{ and }[b_{2}]_{C}=\begin{bmatrix}
-6 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$
by definition.

Suppose $[x]_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$. Find $[x]_{C}$.

$$
\begin{align}
x&=3b_{1}+b_{2} \\
[x]_{C}&=3[b_{1}]_{C}+[b_{2}]_{C}
\end{align}
$$

Thus, it is equal to the matrix equation

$$
[x]_{C}=\begin{bmatrix}
[b_{1}]_{C} & [b_{2}]_{C}
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
3 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Thus,

$$
[x]_{C}=\begin{bmatrix}
4 & -6 \\
1 & 1
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
3 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
6 \\
4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

> [!info] Theorem 15
Let $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$  and $C=\{ c_{1},\dots,c_{n} \}$ be bases of a vector space $V$. Then there is a unique $n\times n$ matrix $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ such that
>
>$$
[x]_{C}=P_{C\leftarrow B}[x]_{B}$$
The columns of $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ are the $C-$coordinate vectors of the vectors in the basis $B$. That is,
>
>$$
P_{C \leftarrow B}= \begin{bmatrix}
[b_{1}]_{C} & [b_{2}]_{C} & \dots & [b_{n}]_{C}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Proof:
For any vector $x$ in $V$, since $B$ is a basis for $V$, there exist unique scalars $x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}$ such that

$$
x=x_{1}b_{1}+x_{2}b_{2}+\dots+x_{n}b_{n}
$$
Then, since $[]_{C}$ is a linear transformation, we get 

$$
\begin{align}
[x]_{C}&=x_{1}[b_{1}]_{C}+\dots+x_{n}[b_{n}]_{C} \\
&=\begin{bmatrix}
[b_{1}]_{C} & \dots & [b_{n}]_{C} 
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
x_{n}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=P_{C\leftarrow B}[x]_{B}
\end{align}
$$
where $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ is an $n\times n$ matrix.


To show that the matrix $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ is unique, suppose that $Q$ is any matrix such that for any nonzero $x$ in $V$,
$$
[x]_{C}=Q[x]_{B}
$$
In particular, let $x=b_{1}$. Then, we have 
$$
[b_{1}]_{C}=Q[b_{1}]_{B}
$$

Notice that 
$$
b_{1}=1b_{1}+0b_{2}+\dots+0b_{n}
$$
Thus, $[b_{1}]_{B}=e_{1}$.

Thus,
$$
\begin{align}
[b_{1}]_{C}&=Qe_{1} \\
Q_{1}&=[b_{1}]_{C}
\end{align}
$$
where $Q_{1}$ is the first column of $Q$.

Similarly, for each $k=2,3\dots,n$. Let $x=b_{k}$. Then, 
$$
[b_{k}]_{C}=Q_{k}
$$
Therefore,

$$
Q=\begin{bmatrix}
[b_{1}]_{C} & \dots & [b_{n}]_{C}
\end{bmatrix}=P_{C\leftarrow B}
$$
Q.E.D.


---


The matrix is called the change of coordinate matrix from $B$ to $C$. Multiplication by $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ convert $B-$coordinate into $C-$coordinate.

The columns of $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ are linearly independent because they are the coordinate vector of the linearly independent set $B$. (Exercise 29 4.4)

[[4.4 Coordinate System#^22e697]]

Since $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ is square. it must be invertible by Invertible Matrix Theorem.

Thus,

$$
(P_{C\leftarrow B})^{-1}[x]_{C}=[x]_{B}
$$
It convert $C-$coordinates into $B-$coordinate.

### Change of Basis in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$

If $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$ and $S=\{ e_{1},\dots,e_{n} \}$. Since 
$b_{1}$ written as linear combination of $\{ e_{1},\dots,e_{n} \}$ and the coordinate vector form by weight is still $b_{1}$. Thus, $[b_{1}]_{S}=b_{1}$. Thus,

$$
P_{S\leftarrow B}=P_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}
b_{1} & b_{2} & \dots & b_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$


Example
Let $b_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}-9 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$, $b_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$, $c_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$, $c_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -5\end{bmatrix}$ and consider the bases for $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ given by $B=\{ b_{1},b_{2} \}$, and $C=\{ c_{1},c_{2} \}$. Find the change-of-coordinates matrix from $B$ to $C$.

We know that from theorem 15

$$
P_{C\leftarrow B}= \begin{bmatrix}
[b_{1}]_{C} & [b_{2}]_{C}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$[b_{1}]_{C}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1} \\ b_{1}\end{bmatrix}$ such that $b_{1}=a_{1}c_{1}+b_{1}c_{2}$
$[b_{2}]_{C}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{2} \\ b_{2} \end{bmatrix}$ such that $b_{2}=a_{2}c_{1}+b_{2}c_{2}$


Thus, it is equal to the solution set of

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} & c_{2} 
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
a_{1} \\
b_{1}
\end{bmatrix}= b_{1}
$$
and 
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} & c_{2}
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
a_{2} \\
b_{2}
\end{bmatrix}=b_{2}
$$

We can solve the system of equation simultaneously.

We row reduce

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} & c_{2} & b_{1} & b_{2}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 3 & -9 & -5 \\
-4 & -5 & 1 & -1
\end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 6 & 4 \\
0 & 1 & -5 & -3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Thus,

$$
[b_{1}]_{C}= \begin{bmatrix}
6 \\
-5
\end{bmatrix} \text{ and }[b_{2}]_{C}=\begin{bmatrix}
4 \\
-3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore,

$$
P_{C\leftarrow B}=\begin{bmatrix}
[b_{1}]_{C} & [b_{2}]_{C}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
6 & 4 \\
-5 & -3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Remark

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} & c_{2} & b_{1} & b_{2}
\end{bmatrix}\sim \begin{bmatrix}
I & P_{C\leftarrow B}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Another description of the change-of-coordinates matrix $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ uses the concept of composition of coordinate mapping.

We know that $P_{B}[x]_{B}=x$ and $[x]_{C}=P^{-1}_{C}x$
. Thus,

$$
[x]_{C}=P^{-1}_{C}x=P^{-1}_{C}P_{B}[x]_{B}
$$
