
> [!definition] Definition Linear equation
> A first-order differential equation of the form 
> $$
> a_{1}(x) \frac{dy}{dx}+a_{0}(x)y=g(x)
> $$
> is said to be a linear equation in the variable $y$

> [!remark]
> When $a_{1},a_{0},g$ are constant, the DE is autonomous

**Standard form**
By dividing both side by $a_{1}(x)$ the coeffficient of $\frac{dy}{dx}$, we obtain the standard form
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dy}{dx}+P(x)y=f(x) &&(1)
\end{align}
$$
where $P(x) = \frac{a_0(x)}{a_1(x)}$ and $f(x) = \frac{g(x)}{a_1(x)}$.

> [!Theorem] Theorem: Existence & Uniqueness of 1st-Order Linear ODEs
> Let $P(x)$ and $f(x)$ be continuous on a common open interval $I$ containing $x_0$. Then for any initial condition $y(x_0) = y_0$, there exists a **unique solution** $y(x)$ to the IVP:
> $$
> \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = f(x), \quad y(x_0) = y_0
> $$
> defined on the **entire interval $I$**, and $y(x)$ is continuously differentiable ($C^1$) on $I$.

> [!NOTE] Connection to Chapter 1 (Theorem 1.2.1)
> * **Singular Points:** Any point where $a_1(x) = 0$ is a singular point (division by zero). The interval $I$ must be chosen to exclude all zeros of $a_1(x)$.
> * **No Finite-Time Blowup:** Unlike general ODEs (like $y' = y^2$ which blows up to $\infty$ in finite time), a linear ODE's solution is guaranteed to exist smoothly across the **entire interval $I$** without unexpected blowups. (See detailed connection in [[Mathematic learning/00_Sessions/223 Differential Equations/1.2 IVP and BHP and Existence and Uniqueness.md#How General Theorem 1.2.1 Connects to the Linear ODE Theorem|1.2 IVP and BHP and Existence and Uniqueness]]).
We seek a solution of (2) on an interval I for which both coefficient functions P and f are continuous.

# Method of solution

Notice that the LHS of standard form (1), look like a derivative of a product (product rule). Thus, we try find a function $\mu(x)$, so that whenever we multiply the whole equation by $\mu(x)$ (the last part in the image) it can be convert to some derivative of product

![[Screenshot 2026-10-14 162250.png]]

Thus, 
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d\mu}{dx}&=\mu P \\
\frac{1}{\mu} \frac{du}{dx}&=P \\
\int \frac{1}{\mu} \frac{d\mu}{dx} \, dx&=\int P \, dx \\
\int \frac{1}{\mu} \, du&= \int P \, dx \\
\ln \lvert \mu \rvert&=\int P \, dx +c_{1}\\
\mu(x)&=c_{2}e^{\int P(x) \, dx}&&c_{2}=e^{c_{1}}
\end{align}
$$

Since any constant will do, we just choose $c_{2}=1$, thus the function
$$
\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x) \, dx}
$$
is called an integrating factor.

Thus, we multiply $(1)$ with $\mu(x)$ on both side, it become
$$
\frac{d}{dx}[\mu(x)y]=\mu(x)f(x)
$$
And we integrate for the last equation and solve for $y$.

$$
\begin{align}
y=e^{-\int P(x) \, dx} \int e^{\int P(x) \, dx } \,f(x) dx+ce^{-\int P(x) \, dx} &&(2)
\end{align}
$$

> [!remark]
> Notice that we showed that if (1) have a solution in $I$, it must in the form of  (2), notice that singular point is excluded in this interval (prevent division by zero), thus (2) is a one-parameter  family of solution called general solutions

this is a one-parameter

## Example
Solve $\frac{dy}{dx}-3y=6$

Notice that this is a linear equation with $P(x)=-3$ and $g(x)=6$. Thus, we find $\mu(x):$
$$
\begin{align}
\mu(x)&=e^{\int P(x) \, dx} \\
&=e^{\int -3 \, dx} \\
&=e^{-3x}
\end{align}
$$
Thus, multiply with both side yield

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dx}(e^{-3x}y)&=6e^{-3x} \\
e^{-3x}y&=6\int e^{-3x}  \, dx \\
&=-6\left( \frac{e^{-3x}}{3} \right)+c \\
y&= -2+ ce^{3x}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that $P(x)$, $g(x)$, and $y(x)$ are continuous on $(-\infty,\infty)$.

> [!remark]
> No need to check if $y'(x)$ is continuous or exist or not because it is guaranteed by the linear ODE theorem. And it is unique


# Singular Points and Intervals of Definition

### Example: Choosing the Interval of Definition and Dealing with $|x^2 - 9|$

Solve:
$$
(x^2 - 9)\frac{dy}{dx} + xy = 0
$$

#### 1. Put into Standard Form
Divide both sides by the lead coefficient $(x^2 - 9)$:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{x}{x^2 - 9}y = 0
$$
Here, $P(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 - 9}$ and $f(x) = 0$.

#### 2. Identify Singular Points and Candidate Intervals
* The lead coefficient vanishes when $x^2 - 9 = 0 \implies x = \pm 3$.
* These singular points divide the real line into **three open intervals of continuity**:
  1. **First interval:** $(-\infty, -3)$
  2. **Second interval (middle):** $(-3, 3)$
  3. **Third interval:** $(3, \infty)$

#### 3. Compute the Integrating Factor
$$
\int P(x)\,dx = \int \frac{x}{x^2 - 9}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\ln|x^2 - 9|
$$
Thus:
$$
\mu(x) = e^{\frac{1}{2}\ln|x^2 - 9|} = \sqrt{|x^2 - 9|}
$$

> [!QUESTION] Why does the textbook solve on the 1st and 3rd intervals $(-\infty, -3)$ and $(3, \infty)$?
> 
> It comes down to the sign of $(x^2 - 9)$ inside the absolute value:
> 
> * **On the 1st and 3rd intervals $(-\infty, -3)$ and $(3, \infty)$:**  
>   Here, $|x| > 3 \implies x^2 > 9 \implies \mathbf{x^2 - 9 > 0}$ (always positive!).  
>   Therefore, the absolute value can be dropped cleanly without a sign change:
>   $$
>   |x^2 - 9| = x^2 - 9 \implies \mu(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 9}
>   $$
>   **A single calculation covers BOTH intervals $(-\infty, -3)$ and $(3, \infty)$ at the same time!**
> 
> * **On the 2nd (middle) interval $(-3, 3)$:**  
>   Here, $|x| < 3 \implies x^2 < 9 \implies \mathbf{x^2 - 9 < 0}$ (negative!).  
>   To drop the absolute value, the sign must flip:
>   $$
>   |x^2 - 9| = -(x^2 - 9) = 9 - x^2 \implies \mu(x) = \sqrt{9 - x^2}
>   $$
>   This requires a separate calculation with a different integrating factor ($\sqrt{9 - x^2}$).
> 
> Solutions **do** exist on the middle interval $(-3, 3)$ as well, but solving on $(-\infty, -3)$ and $(3, \infty)$ allows a unified formula without splitting into cases.

#### 4. Solving on $(-\infty, -3)$ and $(3, \infty)$
Multiplying the standard form by $\mu(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 9}$:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt{x^2 - 9}\,y\right] = 0
$$
Integrating both sides:
$$
\sqrt{x^2 - 9}\,y = c \implies y = \frac{c}{\sqrt{x^2 - 9}}
$$
This solution is valid on either $(-\infty, -3)$ or $(3, \infty)$.
