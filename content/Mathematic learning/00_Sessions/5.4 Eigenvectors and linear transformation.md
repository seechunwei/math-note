Question 3: **The Existence Crisis**
Is it always possible to find a basis $B$ such that $[T]_{B}$ is diagonal?

To answer this, when it will become diagonal? Notice that $T(x)=P_{S\leftarrow B}[T]_{B}P_{B\leftarrow S}[x]_{S}$, $[T]_{B}$ is diagonal iff $A$ is diagonalizable, and the columns of eigenvector form $P$ (the set of eigenvector is a basis, thus it is invertible). Thus, $[T]_{B}$ is a diagonal matrix iff the standard matrix $A=PDP^{-1}$ for some diagonal matrix $D$. (This happen when $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvector)

When does $A$ is not diagonalizable or what kind of transformation is not diagonalizable? 

Consider shear transformation
$T(x,y)=(x+y,y)$ which represented by the $\begin{bmatrix}1 & k \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

To understand what this matrix does, let's track where it sends a generic point $\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$:

$$T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x + ky \\ y \end{bmatrix}$$

Any point that on the $x$-axis or in the form of $(k,0)$ will remain unchanged, any other point will get skewed 
For example $(x,1)$ move sideway by $k$ and $(x,,2)$ will move sideway by 2$k$.


Consider the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
Why the matrix/linear transformation is not diagonalizable?

Because it only have one eigenvalue which is $\lambda=1$ with 2 algebraic multiplicity.

$$
A-1I=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Thus, the eigenspace is

$$
x\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
which only have 1 dimension implies that geometric multiplicity is 1. (Not diagonalizable)

The eigenvector is a no-turn direction. When you apply linear transformation, it never change its direction.

To diagonalize a 2D matrix, you need to find **two separate, independent directional axes** that do not rotate during the transformation

How about $\begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 7 & -2\end{bmatrix}$?
Since the matrix is triangular matrix, it follow that $\lambda=5$ and $\lambda=2$. 

Since it has 2 distinct eigenvalue, it follow that the matrix is diagonalizable. 
(Question why it is diagonalizable since it is shear transformation?)

Let’s look at the mapping of a generic vector under this matrix:

$$T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 7 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5x \\ 7x - 2y \end{bmatrix}$$

At first glance, that $7x$ in the bottom row looks like a vertical shear component, similar to the horizontal shear we looked at before. But there is a massive difference here: **the scaling factors on the diagonal are fighting against the skewing.**

There is one $(x,y)$ that can make the effect of $5x$ and $7x$ balance the effect of $-2y$. For example $(1,1)$.

Why for a linear transformation to be diagonalizable, it need $n$ linearly independent eigenvector?

Notice that from result earlier we know that a linear transformation $T$ is diagonalizable iff the standard matrix $A$ can be written as $A=PDP^{-1}$ for some diagonal matrix $D$. What property that a standard matrix must process that guarantee it is diagonalizable? 

It is actually The Diagonalization Theorem. [[5.3 Diagonalization#^e56d7b]]. If we multiply both sides on the right by $P$, we get an equivalent statement

$$
AP=PD
$$
(We can notice this equation from earlier working example). Let $P=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1} & v_{2} & \dots & v_{n}\end{bmatrix}$. Then

$$
AP=\begin{bmatrix}
Av_{1} & Av_{2} & \dots & Av_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Let the diagonal entry of $D$ be $\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n}$. Notice that since $D$ is a diagonal matrix, it follow that 

$$
PD=\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_{1}v_{1} & \lambda_{2}v_{2} & \dots & \lambda_{n}v_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The equation $AP=PD$ implies that 

$$
Av_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}
$$
for $1\leq i\leq n$. Notice that $\lambda_{i}$ is a scaling factor for each $v_{i}$. Thus, it follow that if there exist such $n$ such eigenvalue and eigenvector, then $A$ is diagonalizable.

Why $\{ v_{1},\dots,v_{n} \}$ must be linearly independent? It is because notice that $P$ is a invertible matrix, thus its columns bust be a basis for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ which implies that $A$ must have $n$ linearly independent eigenvector.

What is the consequences if a matrix is diagonalizable? Think about power of matrix $A^{1000}$, if the matrix is diagonalizable, then there exist a diagonal matrix $D$ such as $A=PDP^{-1}$. Notice that calculating $A^{1000}$ become very simple as we just need to calculate power of the diagonal matrix. For example:

$$
\begin{align}
A^{2}&=PDP^{-1}PDP^{-1} \\
&=PD^{2}P^{-1}
\end{align}
$$
We can propose a statement:

> [!theorem]
> If $A$ is diagonalizable, then $A^{n}=PD^{n}P^{-1}$ for some diagonal matrix $D$.

We prove by mathematical induction. Let $P(n)$ be the statement above. For basis step ,we already $P(2)$ is true.

For inductive step, suppose that $n$ is an arbitrary integer and suppose $P(n)$ is true. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
A^{n+1}&=A(A^{n}) \\
&=A(PD^{n}P^{-1}) &&\text{(By inductive hypothesis)} \\
&=(PDP^{-1})(PD^{n}P^{-1}) \\
&=PD(P^{-1}P)D^{n}P^{-1} \\
&=PD^{n+1}P^{-1}
\end{align}
$$

By by mathematical induction, $P(n)$ is true for all $n\in \mathbb{Z}$ where $n\geq 2$.$\blacksquare$

It is actually true for $n\geq 0$.

### What if the linear transformation map a vector in a vector space to a vector in different vector space?

For example $\mathbb{R}^{3}\to \mathbb{R}^{4}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{3}\to P_{2}$? 

Consider a linear transformation $T:V\to W$. Let $B$ and $C$ be the bases for $V$ and $W$, respectively. Then, is there exist a matrix of $T$ relative to the bases $B$ and $C$ denoted by $\begin{bmatrix}T\end{bmatrix}_{C,B}$ that map $\begin{bmatrix}x\end{bmatrix}_{B}$ to $\begin{bmatrix}T(x)\end{bmatrix}_{C}$?

Let $B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}  \}$ and $C=\{ c_{1},\dots,c_{m} \}$. Since $B$ is a basis it follow that there exist unique $c_{1},\dots,c_{n}$ such that

$$
\begin{align} 
x&= c_{1}b_{1}+\dots c_{n}b_{n}
\end{align}
$$
Apply linear transformation $T$ for both side

$$
\begin{align}
T(x)&=c_{1}T(b_{1})+\dots+c_{n}T(b_{n})
\end{align}
$$
Apply the coordinate mapping to $V$ with respect to basis $C$.

$$
\begin{align}
[T(x)]_{C}&=c_{1}[T(b_{1})]_{C}+\dots+c_{n}[T(b_{n})]_{C} \\
&=M \begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
c_{n}
\end{bmatrix} && M=\begin{bmatrix}
[T(b_{1})]_{C} & \dots & [T(b_{n})]_{C}
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

where $M$ is a $m\times n$ matrix. Notice that the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}c_{1} \\ \vdots \\ c_{n}\end{bmatrix}=[x]_{B}$ is the coordinate matrix of $x$ with respect to basis $B$. Thus, the answer is yes.

> [!theorem]
> Let $V$ and $W$ be two vector spaces and $T:V\to W$ be a linear transformation. Let $B=\{ b_{1},\dots ,b_{n} \}$ and $C=\{ c_{1},\dots,c_{m} \}$. be bases for $V$ and $W$ respectively. Then, there is an $m\times n$ matrix $[T]_{C,B}$ called the matrix for $T$ relative to the bases $B$ and $C$ such that 
> 
> $$
> [T]_{C,B}=\begin{bmatrix}
> [T(b_{1})]_{C} & \dots & [T(b_{n})]_{C}
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 

> [!remark]
> 
> If $W=V$ and $C=B$, then $M=[T]_{B,B}=[T]_{B}$ is the $B-$matrix of $T$.

What if the linear transformation is a identity transformation?

> [!theorem] Corollary
> Let $I:V\to V$ be the identity transformation on a vector space $V$. Let $B$ and $C$ be two bases for $V$. Then $[I]_{C,B}=P_{C\leftarrow B}$

Proof:
Suppose $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$. Then, 

$$
\begin{align}
[I]_{C,B}&= \begin{bmatrix}
[I(b_{1})]_{C} & \dots & [I(b_{n})]_{C}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=\begin{bmatrix}
[b_{1}]_{C} & \dots & [b_{n}]_{C}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= P_{C\leftarrow B}
\end{align}
$$
It is the change of coordinate matrix from basis $B$ to basis $C$. $\blacksquare$

> [!theorem]
> Let $T:V\to V$ be a linear transformation on vector space $V$, and let $B$ and $C$ be two bases for $V$. Then, 
> 
> $$
> [T]_{C,C}=[I]_{C,B}[T]_{B,B},[I]_{B,C}
> $$

Imagine i need to work under $C-$coordinate $([x]_{C})$, but i need to apply linear transformation $[T]_{B.B}$ . First we need to map $[x]_{C}$ to $[x]_{B}$

Formally, let $V$ be a vector space, and let $T:V\to V$ be a linear transformation. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
[x]_{B}&=P_{B\leftarrow C}[x]_{C} \\
&=[I]_{B,C}[x]_{C}
\end{align}
$$


By definition of matrix representation:

1. $T_{C,C} [x]_C = [T(x)]_C$
2. $T_{B,B} [x]_B = [T(x)]_B$


Then. we apply linear transformation $[T]_{B,B}$ to both side

$$
\begin{align}
[T(x)]_{B}=[T]_{B,B}[x]_{B}&=[T]_{B,B}[I]_{B,C}[x]_{C} 
\end{align}
$$
Then we map it back to $C-$coordinate

$$
\begin{align}
[I]_{C,B}[T]_{B,B}[x]_{B}=[I]_{C,B}[T]_{B,B}[I]_{B,C}[x]_{C}
\end{align}
$$

Thus, $[T]_{C,C}=[I]_{C,B}[T(x)]_{B}=[I]_{C,B}[T]_{B,B},[I]_{B,C}$
$\blacksquare$

### Another approach to prove:

Given a matrix of composition of 2 linear transformation relative to 2 bases, we can always split them into 2 linear transformation by adding a basis between them.

> [!theorem] Lemma
> Let $V,V'$ and $W$ be vector spaces and $T:V\to V'$ and $S:V'\to W$ be two linear transformations. Suppose that $B,B'$ and $C$ are bases for $V,V'$ and $W$ respectively. Then the matrix of $S\circ T$ relative to $B$ and $C$ is given by
> 
> $$
> [S\circ T]_{C,B}=[S]_{C,B'}[T]_{B',B}
> $$

(Notice that the codomain of $T$ is the domain of $S$)

Proof:
From definition, we know that
$$
[T(x)]_{B'}=[T]_{B',B}[x]_{B}
$$

Since $[T(x)]_{B'}$ is in $V'$, thus we apply $[S]_{C,B'}$ 

$$
[S(T(x))]_{C}=[S]_{C,B'}[T(x)]_{B'}
$$
By substitution
$$[(S \circ T)(x)]_{C} = [S(T(x))]_{C} = [S]_{C,B'} \left( [T]_{B',B} [x]_B \right)$$

By definition, the matrix representation of $S \circ T: V \to W$ relative to bases $B$ and $C$
$$[(S \circ T)(v)]_{C} = [S \circ T]_{C,B} [v]_B$$
Comparing the two expressions, we have:

$$[S \circ T]_{C,B} [v]_B = \left( [S]_{C,B'} [T]_{B',B} \right) [v]_B \quad \text{for all } v \in V$$

By [[1.9 The Matrix of a Linear Transformation#^f90e92]] (Uniqueness of matrix representation)

$$[S \circ T]_{C,B} = [S]_{C,B'} [T]_{B',B} \quad \blacksquare$$

#### Proof for the theorem using this lemma:

We can write $T=I\circ T\circ I$. Then by the lemma below,

$$
\begin{align}
[T]_{C}=[T]_{C,C}&=[I\circ T\circ I]_{C,C} \\
&=[I\circ T]_{C,B}[I]_{B,C} \\
&=[I]_{C,B}[T]_{B,B}[I]_{B,C} &&\blacksquare
\end{align}
$$

### How to determine if a linear transformation is identity transformation?

A linear transformation $T$ is identity transformation iff $T(x)=x$ for all $x$ in domain. It implies that the standard matrix of $T$ is an identity matrix. Why?

Let
$$
\begin{align}
x&= \begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
x_{n}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= x_{1} \begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
\vdots  \\
0
\end{bmatrix}+\dots+x_{n}\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
\vdots  \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

and $A$ be the standard matrix of $T$. We know that

$$
\begin{align}
T(x)&=x_{1}T(e_{1})+\dots+x_{n}T(e_{n}) \\ 
&= \begin{bmatrix}
T(e_{1}) & \dots & T(e_{n})
\end{bmatrix}x
\end{align}
$$

Thus, 
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
T(e_{1}) & \dots & T(e_{n})
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Since $T$ is a identity transformation, it follow that

$$
T(e_{i})=e_{i}
$$
for $1\leq i\leq n$. Thus, $A$ is an identity matrix.

## Diagonalizable Linear Transformation

> [!definition]
> Let $T:V\to V$ be a linear transformation on vector space $V$. Then $T$ is said to be diagonalizable if there exist an (ordered) basis $B$ of $V$ such that $[T]_{B}$ is a diagonal matrix.

And $[T]_{B}=[a_{ij}]$ is a diagonal matrix such that the diagonal entry $a_{ii}$ is the eigenvalue corresponding to $v_{i}$, for each $i=1,2,\dots,n$.
