
An important reason for specifying a basis $B$ for a vector space $V$ is to impose a “coordinate system” on $V$.

If $B$ contains $n$ vectors, then the coordinate system will make $V$ act like $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ (From dimension theory we know that the number of vector of basis determine the dimension of a vector space) .  If $V$ is already $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ itself, then $B$ will determine a coordinate system that gives a new "view" of $V$.

> [!info] Theorem
The Unique Representation Theorem
Let $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$ be a basis for a vector space $V$. Then, for each $x \in V$, there exists a unique set of scalars $c_{1},\dots,c_{n}$ such that
>$$
x=c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n}
$$

Proof:
Since $B$ span $V$, it follow that every $x \in V$ implies that $x \in Span~B$. Thus, the existence of such scalars holds. Suppose $x$ has another representation
$$
x=d_{1}b_{1}+\dots+d_{n}b_{n}
$$

for scalars $d_{1},\dots,d_{n}$. Then, subtracting we have
$$
0=x-x=(c_{1}-d_{1})b_{1}+\dots+(c_{n}-d_{n})b_{n}
$$

Since $B$ is linearly independent. it follow that 

$$
c_{1}-d_{1}=\dots=c_{n}-d_{n}=0
$$
Thus, $c_{j}=d_{j}$ for $1\leq j\leq n$. Q.E.D

> [!info] Definition
Suppose $B=\{ b_{1},\dots b_{n} \}$ is a basis for a vector space $V$ and $x$ is in $V$. The coordinates of $x$ relative to the basis $B$ (or the $B-$coordinates of $x$) are the weights $c_{1},\dots,c_{n}$ such that
>$$
x=c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n}
$$


If $c_{1},\dots,c_{n}$ are the coordinates of $x$, then vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
$$
[x]_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
c_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
is the **coordinate vector of $x$ (relative to $B$)**. The mapping $x\mapsto[x]_{B}$ is the coordinate mapping (determined by $B$).

(The concept of coordinate mapping assumes that the basis $B$ is an indexed set whose vectors are listed in some fixed preassigned order)

Example 
Let $b_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ and $b_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$. Suppose an $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ has the coordinate vector $[x]_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$.

$$
-2\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
0
\end{bmatrix}+3\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
2
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
6
\end{bmatrix}
$$
It is same as 

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 2
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
-2 \\
3
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
6
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Thus the matrix form by $\begin{bmatrix}b_{1} & b_{2} \end{bmatrix}$ is the matrix that map $[x]_{B}$ to $x$.

### Coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
When a basis $B$ for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is fixed, the $B$-coordinate vector of a specified $x$ is easily found.

Example
Let $b_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$, $b_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$, $x=\begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$ and $B=\{ b_{1},b_{2} \}$. Find the coordinate vector $[x]_{B}$ of $x$ relative to $B$.

By definition $[x]_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1} \\ c_{2}\end{bmatrix}$ where

$$
c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}+c_{2} \begin{bmatrix}
-1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
4 \\
5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

It has the same solution as 

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
b_{1} & b_{2} & x
\end{bmatrix}
$$

We row reduce the augmented matrix and we will get 

$$
[x]_{B}= \begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
c_{2}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
3 \\
2
\end{bmatrix}
$$


The matrix $\begin{bmatrix}b_{1} & b_{2}\end{bmatrix}$ changes the $B$-coordinates of a vector $x$ into the **standard coordinates** for $x$. 

Thus, let $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$. Let
$$
P_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}
b_{1} & b_{2} & \dots & b_{n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Then the vector equation

$$
x=c_{1}b_{1}+c_{2}b_{2}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n}
$$

is equivalent to

$$
P_{B}[x]_{B}=x
$$
We call $P_{B}$ the **change of coordinates matrix** from $B$ to the standard basis in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

Since the columns of $P_{B}$ form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $P_{B}$ is Invertible (by the Invertible Matrix Theorem). Thus,

$$
P^{-1}_{B}x=[x]_{B}
$$

The correspondence $x\mapsto[x]_{B}$ produced here by $P^{-1}_{B}$ is the coordinate mapping mentioned earlier.

Since $P^{-1}_{B}$ is an invertible matrix, the coordinate mapping is one-to-one linear transformation from $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ onto $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ by the Invertible Matrix Theorem.

> [!info] Definition
Let $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$ be a basis for a vector space $V$. Then the mapping $[]_{B}$ defined by
>$$
[]_{B}:V\to \mathbb{R}^{n}$$
>$$
x\mapsto[x]_{B}$$
is a coordinate mapping that maps a vector in $V$ into a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. (it is like isomorphism it map any vector in a vector space to a $n\times 1$ vector )


> [!info] Theorem 9
Let $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$ be a basis for a vector space $V$. Then the coordinate mapping $x\mapsto[x]_{B}$ is a one-to-one linear transformation from $V$ onto $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

Proof:
Suppose 2 arbitrary vectors in $V$, say
$$
\begin{align}
u&=c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n} \\
w&=d_{1}b_{1}+\dots+d_{n}b_{n}
\end{align}
$$
Then, 
$$
u+w=(c_{1}+d_{1})b_{1}+\dots+(c_{n}+d_{n})b_{n}
$$
it follow that

$$
[u+w]_{B}= \begin{bmatrix}
c_{1}+d_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
c_{n}+d_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
c_{n}
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}
d_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
d_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=[u]_{B}+[w]_{B}
$$

So the coordinate mapping preserves addition. If $r$ is any scalar, then

$$
\begin{align}
ru&=r(c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n}) \\
&=rc_{1}b_{1}+\dots +rc_{n}b_{n}
\end{align}
$$
Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
[ru]_{B}= \begin{bmatrix}
rc_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
rc_{n}
\end{bmatrix}= r \begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
c_{n}
\end{bmatrix}= r[u]_{B}
\end{align}
$$
Thus, the coordinate mapping also preserves scalar multiplication and hence is a linear transformation.

Thus the coordinate mapping also preserve scalar multiplication and hence is a linear transformation.

==Exercise 27==, show that the coordinate mapping is one-to-one.

Suppose $u,w \in V$ such that $[u]_{B}=[w]_{B}$. We need to show that $u=w$.

Suppose

$$
[u]_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
\vdots  \\
c_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=[w]_{B}
$$
(We suppose $V$ has $n$ dimension)
Thus, let $B=\{ b_{1},\dots,b_{n} \}$

$$
\begin{align}
u&=c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n} \\
w&=c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{n}b_{n}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that

$$
u-w=(c_{1}-c_{1})b_{1}+\dots+(c_{n}-c_{n})b_{n}=0
$$

Thus, it follow that $u=w$. Thus, the coordinate mapping is one-to-one.

> [!cite] Remark
The linearity of the coordinate mapping extends to linear combination. The $B$-coordinate vector of a linear combination of $u_{1},\dots,u_{p}$ is the same linear combination of their coordinate vectors.

In general a one-to-one linear transformation from a vector space $V$ onto a vector space $W$ is called an isomorphism from $V$ onto $W$.


The notation for $V$ and $W$ may be differ, but the 2 space are indistinguishable as a vector spaces. Every vector space calculation in $V$ is accurately reproduced in $W$, and vice versa.

### Example of Isomorphism

Let $B$ be the standard basis of the space $P_{3}$ of polynomials; that is, let $B=\{ 1,t,t^{2},t^{3} \}$. A typical element $p$ of $P_{3}$ has the form

$$
p(t)=a_{0}+a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}+a_{3}t^{3}
$$

Since $p$ is already displayed as a linear combination of the standard basis vectors, we conclude that

$$
[p]_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}
a_{0} \\
a_{1} \\
a_{2} \\
a_{3}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Thus, the coordinate mapping $p\mapsto[p]_{B}$ is an isomorphism from $P_{3}$ onto $\mathbb{R}^{4}$. All vector space operation in $P_{3}$ correspond to operations in $\mathbb{R}^{4}$.

### Example 2
Let $B=\{ e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4} \}$ be the standard basis for the vector space $M_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ of all $2\times 2$ matrices over $\mathbb{R}$, that is

$$
e_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix},e_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix},e_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 \\
1 & 0
\end{bmatrix},e_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0  \\
0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

A vector $v \in M_{2\times 2}$ is a linear combination of $B$

$$
\begin{align}
v&=\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}=ae_{1}+be_{2}+ce_{3}+de_{4}
\end{align}
$$

Thus, the coordinate vector of $v$ relative to $B$ is

$$
[v]_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}
a \\
b \\
c \\
d
\end{bmatrix}
$$
The coordinate mapping $v\mapsto[v]_{B}$ is an isomorphism from $M_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ onto $\mathbb{R}^{4}$.
