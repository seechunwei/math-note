The orthogonal projection of a point in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ onto a line through the origin has an important analogue in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

The 2 important properties
Given a vector $y$ and a subspace $W$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, there is a vector $\hat{y} \in W$ such that 

1) $\hat{y}$ is the unique vector in $W$ for which $y-\hat{y}$ is orthogonal to $W$.
2) $\hat{y}$ is the unique vector in $W$ closest to $y$

For first one
Observe that whenever $y$ is written as a linear combination of vectors $u_{1},\dots,u_{n}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, the terms in the sum can be grouped into 2 part

$$
y=z_{1}+z_{2}
$$
where $z_{1}$ is a linear combination of some $u_{i}$ and $z_{2}$ is a linear combination of the rest of the $u_{i}$. 

> [!question] 
> What if $\{ u_{1},\dots,u_{n} \}$ is an orthogonal basis? Show that $y$ can be written as $z_{1}+z_{2}$ where $z_{1}\in W$ and $z_{2} \in W^{\perp}$.
> Then,
> 
> $$
> y=c_{1}u_{1}+\dots+c_{n}u_{n}
> $$
> We let $W=Span\{ u_{1},u_{2} \}$ , then we can write
> 
> $$
> y=z_{1}+z_{2}
> $$
> where $z_{1}=c_{1}u_{1}+c_{2}u_{2}$ is in $W$
> and $z_{2}=c_{3}u_{3}+\dots+c_{n}u_{n}$ is in $Span\{ c_{3},\dots,c_{n} \}$.
> 
> To show $z_{2}$ is in $W^{\perp}$, we just need to show that $z_{2}$ is orthogonal to the basis $\{ u_{1},u_{2} \}$ for $W$. Thus,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align}
> z_{2}\cdot u_{1}&=(c_{3}u_{3}+\dots+c_{n}u_{n})\cdot u_{1} \\
> &=c_{3}(u_{3}\cdot u_{1})+\dots+c_{n}(u_{n}\cdot u_{1}) \\
> &=0
> \end{align}
> $$
> because $u_{1}$ is orthogonal to $u_{i}$ for $i \ne 1$. A similar calculation shows that $z_{2}\cdot u_{2}=0$. Thus $z_{2} \in W^{\perp}$. $\blacksquare$.

The next theorem shows that the decomposition $y=z_{1}+z_{2}$ can be computed without having an orthogonal basis for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. It is enough to have an orthogonal basis only for $W$. 

> [!info] Theorem 8 The Orthogonal Decomposition Theorem
> Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. Then each $y\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ can be written uniquely in the form
> 
> $$
> y=\hat{y}+z~~(1)
> $$
> where $\hat{y}$ is in $W$ and $z$ is in $W^{\perp}$. 
> The supposition: If $\{ u_{1},\dots,u_{p} \}$ is any orthogonal basis of $W$, then (the hint)
> 
> $$
> \hat{y}= \frac{y\cdot u_{1}}{u_{1}\cdot u_{1}}u_{1}+\dots+ \frac{y\cdot u_{p}}{u_{p}\cdot u_{p}}u_{p}~~(2)
> $$
> and $z=y-\hat{y}$.
> 

We need to prove that for any $y \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ there exist a unique vector $z$ in (1) $W^{\perp}$ and (2) a unique vector $\hat{y}\in W$ such that $y=\hat{y}+z$ .

To prove this we need to express $z$ and $\hat{y}$ in term of $y$. (Why? because we prove for any arbitrary $y$) 

We need to prove for 2 things
1) Existence of $\hat{y}$ and $z$ such that $\hat{y}\in W$ and $z\in W^{\perp}$
2) The uniqueness

Proof: Let $\{ u_{1},\dots,u_{p} \}$ be any orthogonal basis for $W$ and define $\hat{y}$ as (2). Then $\hat{y}\in W$ because it is a linear combination of $\{ u_{1},\dots,u_{p} \}$. (This prove the existence of $\hat{y} \in W$)

Let $z=y-\hat{y}$. (Why? because to show that $y=\hat{y}+z$). Consider

$$
\begin{align}
z\cdot u_{1}&=(y-\hat{y})\cdot u_{1}=y\cdot u_{1}-\left( \frac{y\cdot u_{1}}{u_{1}\cdot u_{1}}u_{1} \right)\cdot u_{1}-0-\dots-0 \\
&=y\cdot u_{1}-y\cdot u_{1} \\
&=0
\end{align}
$$

(Notice that $y$ is an element in vector space, thus there exist $-y$ such that $y+(-y)=0$.)

Similarly $z$ is orthogonal to each $u_{j}$ in the basis for $W$. Hence $z \in W^{\perp}$. (The existence of $z\in W^{\perp}$)

To show that the decomposition is (1) unique. Suppose $y$ can also be written as $y=\hat{y}_{1}+z_{1}$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\hat{y}+z&=\hat{y}_{1}+z_{1} \\
\hat{y}-\hat{y}_{1}&=z-z_{1}
\end{align}
$$
To show $\hat{y}=\hat{y}_{1}$, we need to show that $\hat{y}-\hat{y}_{1}=0$. Notice that $\hat{y}-\hat{y}_{1}\in W$ and $z-z_{1}\in W^{\perp}$ (because of axiom 1). And let $v=\hat{y}-\hat{y}_{1}$, the only vector that in $W\cap W^{\perp}$ is 0. (because $v\cdot v=0\implies v=0$). Thus, it follow that $\hat{y}=\hat{y}_{1}$ and $z=z_{1}$. $\blacksquare$

> [!cite] Remark
> The $\hat{y}$ is actually the $proj_{W}y$ projection of $y$ onto $W$. The vector $z$ is called the component of $y$ orthogonal to $W$.

## Properties of Orthogonal Projections

If $\{ u_{1},\dots,u_{p} \}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$ and if $y$ is in $W$, then the formula for $proj_{w}y$ is exactly the same as the Theorem 5 in 6.2. In this case, $proj_{w}y=y$


If $y$ is already in $W$, the two concepts are completely identical.

- **Theorem 5** says: "Since $y \in W$, you can break it down into the components along the orthogonal basis vectors $u_1, \dots, u_p$ using the weights $c_j = \frac{y \cdot u_j}{u_j \cdot u_j}$."
    
- **The Projection Formula** blindly calculates $\text{proj}_W y = \left(\frac{y \cdot u_1}{u_1 \cdot u_1}\right)u_1 + \dots + \left(\frac{y \cdot u_p}{u_p \cdot u_p}\right)u_p$.
    

Because the formulas are structurally identical, when $y \in W$, calculating the orthogonal projection of $y$ is literally just using Theorem 5 to find $y$'s coordinate weights relative to the basis of $W$.

Think of the orthogonal projection $\text{proj}_W y$ as a flashlight shining from directly above the subspace $W$ (imagine $W$ is a flat 2D tabletop), casting a perpendicular shadow of the vector $y$ onto that tabletop. But what if $y$ is already lying completely flat on the tabletop ($y \in W$)?

This result follow from next theorem.

> [!info] Theorem 9 The Best Approximation Theorem
> Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, let $y$ be any vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, and let $\hat{y}$ be the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto $W$. Then, $\hat{y}$ is the closest point in $W$ to $y$, in the sense that
> 
> $$
> ||y-\hat{y}||<||y-v||
> $$
> for all $v\in W$ distinct from $\hat{y}$.
>

The vector $\hat{y}$ in Theorem 9 is called the **best approximation to $y$ by elements of $W$** 

The distance from $y$ to $v$, given by $||y-v||$, can be regarded as the “error” of using $v$ in place of $y$. Theorem 9 says that this error is minimized when $v=\hat{y}$.

### Orthogonal matrix

Suppose $U$ is an $n\times p$ matrix with orthonormal columns, and let $W$ be the column space of $U$. Then,

Theorem 6:
$$
U^{T}Ux=I_{p}x=x~~ \text{ for all x in }\mathbb{R}^{p}
$$
Theorem 10:
$$
UU^{T}y=proj_{W}y
~~ \text{ for all y in }\mathbb{R}^{n}
$$

If $U$ is an $n\times n$ matrix with orthonormal columns, then $U$ is an orthogonal matrix, the columns space $W$ is all of $\mathbb{R}^{n},$ thus $y \in Col(U)=W$, thus $proj_{W}y=y$ .  Hence, $UU^{T}y=Iy=y$ for all $y$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

(Notice that theorem 6 does not require $U$ to be a square matrix but Theorem 10 need it to satisfy $UU^{T}y=y$)

At first we define orthogonal matrix as:
> [!NOTE] Definition
> Orthogonal matrix is a square matrix whose columns from an orthonormal set. 
> 
> In other words, all orthogonal matrices have orthogonal columns, but not all matrices with orthonormal columns are orthogonal matrices.

After we combine Theorem 6 and Theorem 10, we define

> [!NOTE] Definition
> An $n\times n$ matrix is said to be orthogonal if its transpose is the same as its inverse, that is, if
> $$
> A^{-1}=A^{T}
> $$
> or, equivalently, if
>
>$$
AA^{T}=A^{T}A=I_{n}
$$



> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $A$ be an orthogonal matrix.  Then
> a) its transpose $A^{T}$ and inverse $A^{-1}$ are orthogonal
> b) the determinant of $A$ is either 1 or -1;
> c) $AB$ is orthogonal, where $B$ is another orthogonal matrix.
> 

Proof a):
Suppose $A$ is an orthogonal matrix. Then

$$
\begin{align}
A^{-1}&=A^{T} \\
(A^{-1})^{T}&=(A^{T})^{T} \\
\implies(A^{T})^{-1}=(A^{T})^{T}~(1) &\text{ and }\implies(A^{-1})^{T}=A~(2)
\end{align}
$$

Thus, $(1)$ show that $A^{T}$ is also orthogonal while  $(2)$ show that $A^{-1}$ is also orthogonal. $\blacksquare$

Proof b):
Suppose $A$ is an orthogonal matrix. Then

$$
\begin{align}
\det (A^{-1})&=\det A^{T} \\
\frac{1}{\det A}&=\det A \\
(\det A)^{2}&=1 \\
\det A&=\pm 1
\end{align}
$$
$\blacksquare$
This proof require 2 properties
1) $\det(A^{-1})= \frac{1}{\det A}$
2) $\det(A^{T})=\det A$

Proof c):
Suppose $A$ and $B$ are orthogonal matrix. Then,

$$
\begin{align}
(AB)^{T}&=B^{T}A^{T} \\
&=B^{-1}A^{-1} \\
&=(AB)^{-1}
\end{align}
$$
$\blacksquare$
