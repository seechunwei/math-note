

> [!info] Definition
The null space of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$, written as $Nul(A)$ is the set of all solutions of the homogeneous equation $Ax=0$. In set notation,
>
>$$Nul (A)=\{ x:x \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \text{ and }Ax=0 \}$$
>
From linear transformation's view, null space is known as kernel, it is the set of all vector in a vector space that map to zero vector in $\mathbb{R}^{m}$.

To test if $u \in Nul(A)$, we just need to see if $Au=0$.

> [!cite] Remark
We need to state null space for a specify matrix, because it is not independent.
>
>It is either $Nul(A)=\{ 0 \}$ or $Nul(A)$ have infinitely many solution. It is always a linear span of a vector set


> [!important] Property
The two important property in definition of Nullspace of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$
>1) All solution set of $Ax=0$
>2) It is a vector space, specifically a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$

The second property lead to the next theorem

> [!info] Theorem 2
The null space of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. Equivalently, the set of all solution to a system $Ax=0$ of where $A\in M_{m\times n}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.


Proof:

$$
Nul(A)=\{ x:x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and }Ax=0 \}
$$

Certainly $Nul(A)$ is a subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ because $A$ has $n$ columns.

We need to show that $Nul(A)$ satisfy 3 properties of a subspace. It is clear that $0 \in Nul(A)$ because

$$
A(0)=0
$$

Thus, $Nul(A)$ is a non empty set.

Next suppose any vector $u,v \in Nul(A)$ , we have

$$
A(u)=0 \text{ and } A(v)=0  
$$
Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
A(u+v)=A(u)+A(v)=0+0=0
\end{align}
$$

Thus,
$u+v \in Nul(A)$. 

Next, for any vector $u \in Nul(A)$ and any real scalar $c$, we have

$$
A(cu)=cA(u)=c(0)=0
$$

Thus, $cu \in Nul(A)$. Thus, $Nul(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

> [!cite] Remark
Notice that only the solution set of homogeneous  equation will always form a subspace, because the zero vector is not a solution of a nonhomogeneous system.

### An explicit description of Nul A

We say that $Nul(A)$ is defined implicitly, because it is defined by a condition must be checked. To find the explicit description of $Nul(A)$, wee need to solve the equation $Ax=0$, and express the solution set as a linear span of a vector set in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$(To show that it is a subspace).

> [!cite] Remark
> 1) The spanning set of $Nul(A)$ are linearly independent 
> 2) The number of vectors in the spanning set for $Nul(A)$ equals the number of free variables in $Ax=0$.

### The column space of a matrix

> [!info] Definition
> The column space of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$, written as $Col(A)$ is the set of all linear combinations of the columns of $A$. If $A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1} & \dots & a_{n}\end{bmatrix}$, then
> $$
Col(A)=Span\{ a_{1},\dots,a_{n} \}
$$

It is denoted as

$$
Col(A)=\{ b:b=Ax \text{ for some }x \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \}
$$

Notice that the column of $A \in M_{m\times n}$ have $m$ entries, thus column space of $A$ is in $\mathbb{R}^{m}$.

> [!info] Theorem 3
> The column space of $m\times n$ matrix $A$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{m}$

From the view of linear transformation, $Col(A)$ can be written as $Ax$ show that $Col(A)$ is the range of the linear transformation $x\mapsto Ax$.

> [!important] Existence

### Linear transformations

The function $T:\mathbb{R}^{4}\to M_{2\times2}(\mathbb{R})$ defined by

$$
T(x)=T(\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} \\
x_{2} \\
x_{3} \\
x_{4}
\end{bmatrix})=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} & x_{2} \\
x_{3} & x_{4}
\end{bmatrix}=A
$$
is a linear transformation.

For any $x \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$ and $y \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$, we have

$$
\begin{align}
T(x+y)&=T(\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1}+y_{1} \\
x_{2}+y_{2} \\
x_{3}+y_{3} \\
x_{4}+y_{4}
\end{bmatrix}) \\
&=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1}+y_{1} & x_{2}+y_{2} \\
x_{3}+y_{3} & x_{4}+y_{4}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} & x_{2} \\
x_{3} & x_{4}
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}
y_{1} & y_{2}  \\
y_{3} & y_{4}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=T(x)+T(y)
\end{align}
$$

For any $x \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$ and any real scalar $c$, we get

$$
\begin{align}
T(cx)&=\begin{bmatrix}
cx_{1} & cx_{2} \\
cx_{3} & cx_{4}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= c \begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} & x_{2} \\
x_{3} & x_{4}
\end{bmatrix} \\
&=cT(x)
\end{align}
$$

Thus, $T$ is a linear transformation.


> [!info] Theorem
Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$). A transformation $T:V\to W$ is linear iff
>$$
T(cu+dv)=cT(u)+dT(v)
$$

> [!info] Theorem
Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$) and suppose that $T:V\to W$ is a linear transformation. Then,
a) $T(0)=0$;
b) $T(u-v)=T(u)-T(v)$ for all $u$ and $v$ in $V$
c) $T(-v)=-T(v)$ for all $v$ in $V$
^123

==Proof a)==
Preliminary step: If $T(0)=0$, then $T(0)+T(0)=0$.

Proof:
For the zero vector in $V$, we know that $0+0=0$. So,

$$
T(0)=T(0+0)=T(0)+T(0)
$$

For the zero vector in $W$, we know that $T(0)+0=T(0)$ and this $0$ is unique in $W$. Thus, $T(0)=0$.

==Proof b)==
Preliminary step:  $T(u-v)=T(u+(-1)v)=T(u)+T(-1v)=T(u)-T(v)$
Proof:

 For any $v \in V$, it follow that $-v=(-1v)$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
T(u-v)&=T(u+(-v)) \\
&=T(u)+T(-1v) \\
&=T(u)+(-1)T(v) \\
&=T(u)-T(v)
\end{align}
$$

==Proof c) ==
For any $v \in V$ , there exist an unique $-v$ such that $v+(-v)=0$, and $-v=(-1)v$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
T(-v)&=T(-1(v)) \\
&=(-1)T(v)
\end{align}
$$
Since $(-1)T(v) \in V$, it follow that 
$$
(-1)T(v)=-T(v)
$$
Thus,

$$T(-v) = -T(v) \quad \blacksquare$$

Another approach
Let $v$ be any vector in $V$. By the definition of additive inverses in $V$:

$$v + (-v) = 0_V$$

Applying the linear transformation $T$ to both sides:

$$T(v + (-v)) = T(0_V)$$

Since $T$ is linear, we apply the **additivity property** to the left side:

$$T(v) + T(-v) = T(0_V)$$

From our previous proof, we already know that $T(0_V) = 0_W$. Substituting this in gives:

$$T(v) + T(-v) = 0_W$$

This equation tells us that when the vector $T(-v)$ is added to $T(v)$, the result is the zero vector of $W$. By the uniqueness of the additive inverse in any vector space, $T(-v)$ _must_ be the negative of $T(v)$:

$$T(-v) = -T(v) \quad \blacksquare$$

### Kernel and Range of Linear Transformation

Subspaces of vector spaces other than $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ are often described in terms of a linear transformation instead of a matrix.

> [!info] Definition
A linear transformation $T$ from a vector space $V$ into a vector space $W$ is a rule that assigns to each vector $x$ in $V$ a unique vector $T(x)$ in $W$, such tha
>1) $T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v)$ for all $u,v \in V$
>2) $T(cu)=cT(u)$ for all $u\in V$ and all scalars $c$.

In section 1.8, we didn't specify the domain and codomain is a vector space.

The kernel of such $T$ is the set of all $u \in V$ such that $T(u)=0$ (The zero vector in $W$).

$$
Ker(T)=\{ u \in V:T(u)=0 \}
$$



The range of $T$ is the set of all $w\in W$ such that $w=T(u)$ for some $u \in V$.

$$
\mathrm{Im}(T)=\{ T(u)\in W: \text{ for some }u \in V \}
$$
