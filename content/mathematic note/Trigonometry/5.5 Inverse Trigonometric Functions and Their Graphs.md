### Composing Trigonometry functions and their inverse

Suppose we have $\cos(\tan ^{-1}x)$ , how to write it as an algebraic expression in $x$?

Let $u=\tan ^{-1}x$
we need to write $\cos$ in term of tangent

$$
\begin{align}
\sin ^{2}x&=1-\cos ^{2}x \\
\end{align}
$$

$$
\begin{align}
\tan ^{2}x&=\sec ^{2}x-1 \\
\sec x&= \pm \sqrt{ \tan ^{2}x+1 }
\end{align}
$$
This is the "pure math" part. The range of $\tan^{-1}(x)$ is $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$. In these two quadrants (the right half of the unit circle), **cosine is always positive**. Therefore, we can safely ignore the negative sign and use:
$$\cos(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2(u)}}$$

By substitution:

$$
\cos(\tan ^{-1}x)= \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 1+x^{2} }}
$$

### The inverse secant, cosecant and cotangent

#### Inverse of secant
$y=\sec x$, $0\leq x< \frac{\pi}{2}$ . $\pi\leq x< \frac{3\pi}{2}$. (Notice that $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ are asymptotes)

Thus, $x$ is in first quadrant and third quadrant. In these two quadrant $\tan x\geq 0$ ? Why we choose this 2 quadrant?

![[Pasted image 20260605225355.png]]


Thus, the range of $\sec ^{-1}x$ is $\left[ 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\cup\left[ \pi, \frac{3\pi}{2} \right)$ and when $x\geq 1$, $\sec ^{-1}x \in\left[ 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right)$ 
when $x\leq -1$, $\sec ^{-1}x \in\left[ \pi, \frac{3\pi}{2} \right)$.


Consider $\frac{d}{dx}(\sec ^{-1}x)= \frac{1}{x\sqrt{ x^{2}-1 }}$.

Let $y=\sec ^{-1}x$ , Thus, $\sec y=x$

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dx}\sec y&=\frac{d}{dx} x\\

\end{align}
$$

$\frac{d}{dx}\sec y$ =$\sec y\tan y\cdot \frac{dy}{dx}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}=1$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\sec y\tan y\cdot \frac{dy}{dx}&=1 \\
\frac{dy}{dx}&= \frac{1}{\sec y\tan y}
\end{align}
$$

Since $y=\sec ^{-1}x$ , it follow that

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{x\tan y}
$$

For $y$ in first quadrant and third quadrant. Thus,

$$
\tan y=\pm \sqrt{ \sec ^{2}y-1 }=\pm \sqrt{ x^{2}-1 }
$$

- **Quadrant I:** $\tan y$ is **positive** ($\geq 0$).
    
- **In Quadrant III:** $\tan y$ is **also positive** ($\geq 0$).
Thus,


$$\frac{d}{dx}(\sec^{-1} x) = \frac{1}{x\sqrt{x^2-1}}$$

If we define $y$ to be in first quadrant and second quadrant

Then for positive input $x\geq 1\to$ Quadrant 1 (From the graph). Then, The denominator $x \tan y$ is (positive) $\times$ (positive) = **positive**.

For negative inputs $x\leq -1\to$ Quadrant II.  
The denominator $x \tan y$ becomes (negative) $\times$ (negative) = **positive**.

### The Unified Formula for Q1 & Q2

Because the denominator ends up being forced positive in both scenarios under the Quadrant 1/2 convention, mathematicians have to introduce an **absolute value sign** to make a single formula work for all inputs:

$$\frac{d}{dx}(\sec^{-1} x) = \frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}}$$

#### Inverse of cosecant

We restrict the domain of $\csc x$ to be to be $0<x\leq \frac{\pi}{2}$ or $\pi<x\leq \frac{3\pi}{2}$. (Notice that $0$ and $\pi$ are the asymptotes) 

![[Pasted image 20260605230519.png]]

Why $\csc x$ is defined at first and third quadrant? (It is same as $\sec$, it is for the convenient of derivative of $\csc ^{-1}x$ )

Consider:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\csc ^{-1}x
$$

Let $y=\csc ^{-1}x$, thus $\csc y=x$. It follow that

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dx}\csc y&=\frac{d}{dx}x
\end{align}
$$
Since
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\csc y=-\csc x\cot x
$$
and
$$
\frac{d}{dx}=1
$$
Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
-\csc y\cot y\cdot \frac{dy}{dx}&=1 \\
\frac{dy}{dx}&= \frac{1}{-\csc y\cot y} \\
&=\frac{1}{-x\cot y}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that 
$$
\cot y=\pm\sqrt{\csc ^{2}y-1  }
$$

Since $y$ is in Quadrant I and III. It follow that $\cot y\geq 0$. Thus, 

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dy}{dx}&= \frac{1}{-x\sqrt{ x^{2}-1 }} \\
&=\frac{-1}{x\sqrt{ x^{2}-1 }}
\end{align}
$$

#### The inverse of cotangent 

We restrict the domain of $y=\cot x,0<x<\pi$. (This restriction is nothing to do with the derivative)

![[Pasted image 20260605232049.png]]

Consider:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\cot ^{-1} x
$$

Let $y=\cot ^{-1} x$,  thus $\cot y=x$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dx}\cot y&=\frac{d}{dx}x
\end{align}
$$
Since,
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\cot y= -\csc ^{2}x
$$

It follow that,

$$
\begin{align}
-\csc ^{2}y\cdot \frac{dy}{dx}&=1 \\
\frac{dy}{dx}&= \frac{1}{-\csc ^{2}y}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that $\csc ^{2}y=\cot ^{2}y+1$ . Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dy}{dx}&= \frac{1}{-\cot^{2}y-1} \\
&=\frac{1}{-x^{2}-1} \\
&=-\frac{1}{x^{2}+1}
\end{align}
$$
