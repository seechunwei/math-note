
Now we consider a special case of first order linear DE with constant coefficients.
$$
\begin{align}
ay'+by=0 \tag*{(1)}
\end{align}
$$
where $a \neq 0$ and $b$ are constants. It is linear and separable but now we consider the alternative method that only use algebra.

Note that (1) deduce that $y'=ky$ for some constant $k$. Hence, the trivial solution for 1 is $y=e^{mx}$ for some value $m$. Hence, substitute back inside the DE yields

$$
\begin{align}
ame^{mx}+be^{mx}=0\implies e^{mx}(am+b=0)
\end{align}
$$

Since $e^{mx} \neq 0$ for all $x$, hence $am+b=0$. Therefore, $m$ is the root of the polynomial equation $am+b=0$, and for this particular $m$, $y=e^{mx}$ is the solution for (1).


## Auxiliary Equation

For second order linear DE with constant coefficient
$$
\begin{align}
ay''+by'+cy=0 \tag*{(3)}
\end{align}
$$

it is easy to show that $y=e^{mx}$ is the solution for (3) for any value $m$ that satisfy the quadratic equation
$$
am^{2}+bm+c=0
$$
and it is called auxiliary equation for (3). By quadratic formula, it follow that 

$$
m= \frac{-b\pm \sqrt{ b^{2}-4ac }}{2a}
$$
There exists 3 cases according to the discriminant $b^{2}-4ac$:
- $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ are real and distinct $(b^{2}-4ac>0)$
- $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ are real and equal $(b^{2}-4ac=0)$
- $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ are conjugate complex numbers $(b^{2}-4ac<0)$

### Case 1: Distinct Real Roots
Hence we have 2 solution $y_{1}=e^{m_{1}x}$ and $y_{2}=e^{m_{2}x}$, we wee that these functions are linearly independent on $(-\infty,\infty)$ and hence form a fundamental set. It follow that the general solution of (2) on this interval is
$$
y=c_{1}e^{m_{1}x}+c_{2}e^{m_{2}x}
$$

### Case 2: Repeated Real Roots

When $b^{2}-4ac=0$, we obtain a single repeated real root $m_{1}= -\frac{b}{2a}$, yielding one solution:
$$y_{1}=e^{m_{1}x}=e^{-\frac{b}{2a}x}$$

To find the second linearly independent solution $y_{2}$, we put equation (3) into standard form by dividing by $a$:
$$y''+\frac{b}{a}y'+\frac{c}{a}y=0 \implies P(x)=\frac{b}{a}$$

Applying the reduction of order formula:
$$y_{2}=y_{1}(x)\int \frac{e^{-\int P(x)\,dx}}{(y_{1}(x))^{2}} \, dx$$

Here:
- $e^{-\int P(x)\,dx} = e^{-\int \frac{b}{a}\,dx} = e^{-\frac{b}{a}x}$
- $(y_{1}(x))^{2} = \left(e^{-\frac{b}{2a}x}\right)^{2} = e^{-\frac{b}{a}x}$

Substituting these into the formula:
$$
\begin{align}
y_{2} &= e^{m_{1}x}\int \frac{e^{-\frac{b}{a}x}}{e^{-\frac{b}{a}x}} \, dx \\
&= e^{m_{1}x}\int 1 \, dx \\
&= xe^{m_{1}x}
\end{align}
$$

The functions $y_{1}=e^{m_{1}x}$ and $y_{2}=xe^{m_{1}x}$ form a fundamental set of solutions. Hence, the general solution on $(-\infty,\infty)$ is:
$$y=c_{1}e^{m_{1}x}+c_{2}xe^{m_{1}x}$$
