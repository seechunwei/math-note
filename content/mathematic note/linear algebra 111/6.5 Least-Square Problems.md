> [!theorem]  Theorem 15
> Given an $m\times n$ matrix $A$ with linearly independent columns, let $A=QR$ be a $QR$ factorization of $A$ as in Theorem 12. Then, for each $b\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$, the equation $Ax=b$ has a unique least-square solution, given by
> 
> $$
> \hat{x}=R^{-1}Q^{T}b
> $$

Proof:
Let $\hat{x}=R^{-1}Q^{T}b$. Then

$$
A\hat{x}=QR(R^{-1}Q^{T}b)=Q(R R^{-1})Q^{T}b=Q Q^{T}b
$$

By theorem 12, the columns of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis for $\text{Col}A$. Hence, by Theorem 10, $Q Q^{T}b$ is the orthogonal projection $\hat{b}$ of $b$ onto $\text{Col}A$. Then, by Theorem 9, $\hat{x}$ is a least square solution of $Ax=b$. The uniqueness of $\hat{x}$ follows from Theorem 14. $\blacksquare$

Another approach to prove:
Suppose an arbitrary least-square solution $\hat{x}$ for $Ax=b$. Thus, by Theorem 13, 

$$
A^{T}A\hat{x}=A^{T}b
$$
By theorem 12, $A=QR$ for where the columns of $Q$ is orthonormal and $R$ is a upper triangular invertible matrix with positive diagonal. Thus, by substitution

$$
\begin{align}
(QR)^{T}(QR)\hat{x}&=(QR)^{T}b \\
(R^{T}Q^{T})(QR)\hat{x}&= (R^{T}Q^{T})b \\ 
R^{T}(Q^{T}Q)R\hat{x}&=R^{T}Q^{T}b
\end{align}
$$

By theorem 10, $Q^{T}Q=I$, thus

$$
\begin{align}
R^{T}IR\hat{x}&=R^{T}Q^{T}b \\
R^{T}R\hat{x}&=R^{T}Q^{T}b
\end{align}
$$

Since $R$ is invertible , it follow that $R^{T}$ is invertible.[[1.6 Inverse and Transposes#^0e8787]]  Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
(R^{T})^{-1}R^{T}R\hat{x}&=(R^{T})^{-1}R^{T}Q^{T}b \\
R\hat{x}&=Q^{T}b
\end{align}
$$

Since $R$ is invertible, we multiply both sides from the left by $R^{-1}$:

$$\hat{x} = R^{-1}Q^Tb$$

The uniqueness of $\hat{x}$ follow by Theorem 15. $\blacksquare$

> [!remark] Numerical Notes 
> Since $R$ in Theorem 15 is upper triangular, $\hat{x}$ should be calculated as the exact solution of the equation
> 
> $$

> $$
> It is much faster to solve by back-substitution.

# Projection Matrices

Is there a matrix that project $b$ to $\hat{b}$?

[[linear algebra 111/Exercise 6.5#^caf71f]]

> [!definition]
> The matrix
> 
> $$
> P=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}
> $$
> is a projection matrix of $b$ onto the $\text{Col}A$. Note that
> 
> $$
> Pb=A\hat{x}=\hat{b}=\text{proj}_{ColA}b
> $$


> [!theorem]
> Let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix with linearly independent columns. Then the $m\times m$ matrix
> $$
> P=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}
> $$
> has the following properties:
> 
> a) $A=PA$
> b) Col $P=\text{Col}A$ 
> c) $b-Pb$ is orthogonal to $\text{Col}P$ for all $b\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$
> d) The "best solution" to $Ax=b$ is the unique solution to $Ax=Pb$
> e) $P$ is symmetric $(P^{T}=P)$ and idempotent $(P^{2}=P)$.

Proof a):
Since $P=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}$, we have

$$
\begin{align}
PA&=(A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T})A \\
&= A((A^{T}A)^{-1}(A^{T}A)) \\
&=AI_{n} \\
&=A
\end{align}
$$

Proof b):
Since $Pb=A((A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}b)$ is of the form $Ax$, it follows that $\text{Col}P\subset\text{Col}A$.

> [!question]
> How to understand this intuitively?
> $\text{Col}P$ is the range of $P\hat{b}=b$. Since it map every vector into the column space of $A$, it follow that $\text{Col}P\subset\text{Col}A$.

On the other hand, since $A=PA$, it follow that

$$
Ax=(PA)x=P(Ax)
$$
That is, $Ax$ is of the form $Pb$. So, $\text{Col}A\subset\text{Col}P$. $\blacksquare$

> [!question]
> How to understand this intuitively?
> Any vector in $\text{ColA}$ is in the form of $Pb$.

Proof c):
Let $W=\text{Col}P$, $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{m}$. By Orthogonal Decomposition Theorem, any $b\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ can be written uniquely in the form:

$$
b=\hat{b}+z
$$
where $\hat{b}=\text{proj}_{W}b$ and $z=b-\hat{b}$.

Since $Pb$ is the orthogonal projection of $b$ onto $W$. It follow that $\hat{b}=Pb$. Thus,

$$z = b - Pb$$
which is in $W^{\perp}$. Thus $z$ is orthogonal to the subspace $\text{Col}P$ .

Proof d):
Since $Pb$ is the orthogonal projection of $b$ onto $\text{Col}A$ , it follow that by Best Approximation Theorem the best solution for $Ax=b$ is a solution of $Ax=Pb$. Since the columns of $A$ is linearly independent, it follow that the solution is unique.

Proof e)
Since $P=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}$,

$$
\begin{align}
P^{T}&=(A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T})^{T} \\
&=(A^{T})^{T}[(A^{T}A)^T]^{-1}A^{T}\\
&=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T} \\
&=P
\end{align}
$$
So $P$ is symmetric.

$$
\begin{align}
P^{2}&=(A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T})(A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}) \\
&=(A)[(A^{T}A)^{-1}(A^{T}A)](A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T} \\
&=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T} \\
&=P
\end{align}
$$
So, $P$ is idempotent.

> [!remark]
> Part $(e)$ implies that $P$ is an orthogonal projection matrix.
> 

> [!theorem]
> Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ and let $\{ w_{1},\dots,w_{p} \}$ be an orthogonal basis for $W$. Then for any $b\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$, the projection matrix $P$ onto $W$ is 
> 
> $$
> P= \frac{w_{1}w_{1}^{T}}{w_{1}\cdot w_{1}}+ \frac{w_{2} w_{2}^{T}}{w_{2}\cdot w_{2}}+\dots+ \frac{w_{p}w_{p}^{T}}{w_{p}\cdot w_{p}}
> $$
> 

Why $b\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ ? Because it is range of a linear transformation represented by $m\times n$ matrix.

Proof :
For any vector $b\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$, let $\hat{b}$ be the orthogonal projection of $b$ onto $W$. Thus,

$$\hat{b} = \left(\frac{b \cdot w_1}{w_1 \cdot w_1}\right)w_1 + \left(\frac{b \cdot w_2}{w_2 \cdot w_2}\right)w_2 + \dots + \left(\frac{b \cdot w_p}{w_p \cdot w_p}\right)w_p$$
where $\{ w_{1},\dots,w_{p} \}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$.

Notice that $b\cdot w_{i}=w_{i}\cdot b=w_{i}^{T}b$. Thus,

$$
\hat{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{p} \left( \frac{w^{T}_{i}b}{w_{i}\cdot w_{i}} \right)w_{i}
$$

Since $cw_{i}=w_{i}c$, it follow that 

$$
\hat{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{p} w_{i}\left( \frac{w^{T}_{i}b}{w_{i}\cdot w_{i}} \right)=\sum_{i=1}^{p} \left( \frac{w_{i}w^{T}_{i}b}{w_{i}\cdot w_{i}} \right)
$$

By matrix associativity, we can group the outer product $w_i w_i^T$ together:

$$\left(\frac{b \cdot w_i}{w_i \cdot w_i}\right)w_i = \left(\frac{w_i w_i^T}{w_i \cdot w_i}\right)b$$
Now, let's substitute this rewritten form for every single term in our projection formula:

$$\hat{b} = \left(\frac{w_1 w_1^T}{w_1 \cdot w_1}\right)b + \left(\frac{w_2 w_2^T}{w_2 \cdot w_2}\right)b + \dots + \left(\frac{w_p w_p^T}{w_p \cdot w_p}\right)b$$

Because matrix multiplication distributes over addition, we can factor out the vector $b$ on the right-hand side:

$$\hat{b} = \left( \frac{w_1 w_1^T}{w_1 \cdot w_1} + \frac{w_2 w_2^T}{w_2 \cdot w_2} + \dots + \frac{w_p w_p^T}{w_p \cdot w_p} \right) b$$

Thus, 

$$P = \frac{w_1 w_1^T}{w_1 \cdot w_1} + \frac{w_2 w_2^T}{w_2 \cdot w_2} + \dots + \frac{w_p w_p^T}{w_p \cdot w_p}$$

where $\hat{b}=Pb$. $\blacksquare$

> [!definition]
> An $n\times n$ matrix $P$ is said to be idempotent if $P^{2}=P$. An idempotent matrix $P$ is called a projection matrix if $P$ maps vectors onto a specific subspace.
> 
> Further, a real projection matrix $P$ is called an orthogonal projection matrix if $P$ is symmetric, that is, $P^{T}=P$.
> 

### Layer 1: Idempotent Matrix ($P^2 = P$) — The "No Change After the First Time" Rule

The word **idempotent** comes from Latin (_idem_ meaning "same," and _potis_ meaning "power"). In algebra, an operation is idempotent if applying it multiple times gives you the exact same result as applying it once.

- **The Matrix Rule:** If you multiply a vector $x$ by the matrix $P$ once, you get an output: $Px$.
    
- If you apply the matrix a second time to that output, you get $P(Px) = P^2x$.
    
- The definition states $P^2 = P$, which means:
    
    $$P(Px) = Px$$
**How to visualize this:** Imagine a machine that flattens a lump of dough into a pancake. If you put the dough through the machine once, it becomes a pancake. If you put that pancake through the machine a second time, nothing changes—it remains the exact same pancake.

### Layer 2: Projection Matrix — The "Target Subspace" Concept

The text says an idempotent matrix is called a **projection matrix** if it "maps vectors onto a specific subspace."

Connecting this back to Layer 1:

- The first time you apply $P$ to a vector $x$, it travels through space and lands directly on the target floor ($Px$).
    
- Once the vector is _already sitting on the floor_, applying $P$ again does absolutely nothing to it because it has already reached the target territory. This is exactly why a projection matrix **must** be idempotent ($P^2x = Px$).

### Layer 3: Orthogonal Projection Matrix — The "Straight Down Drop" ($P^T = P$)

This is where the geometry gets refined. A matrix can project a vector onto a plane at a weird, skewed angle (called an _oblique projection_). However, in linear algebra, we almost always prefer dropping vectors at a perfect **$90^\circ$ angle**. This straight-down drop is called an **orthogonal projection**.
