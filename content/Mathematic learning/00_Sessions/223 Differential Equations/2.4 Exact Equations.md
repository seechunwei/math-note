
Consider the simple first-order differential equation
$$
ydx+xdy=0
$$
is separable and linear, but we can try solving in another method. Notice that the LHS is the differential of the function $f(x,y)=xy$., that is
$$
d(xy)=ydx+xdy
$$

We will examine First order DE in the form of
$$
M(x,y)dx+N(x,y)dy=0
$$
to construct a function $f(x,y)$ by partial integration.

## Prerequisite (Multivariable calculus) 
Don't worry at all! You do **not** need a whole multivariable calculus course to understand this intuitively. 

Here is the simple, real-world way to picture it:

---

### Step 1: The Mountain Analogy (What is $f(x, y)$?)

Imagine you are hiking on a mountain. 
* $x$ is your position **East / West**.
* $y$ is your position **North / South**.
* $z = f(x, y)$ is your **elevation (altitude)** above sea level.

```
       North (y)
          ^
          |      [Mountain Surface z = f(x,y)]
          |
----------+----------> East (x)
         /
        v
```

Now, what are those strange symbols $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$?

* **$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$** is simply: *"If you walk purely **East**, what is the steepness (slope) under your feet?"*
* **$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$** is simply: *"If you walk purely **North**, what is the steepness (slope) under your feet?"*

---

### Step 2: What is the Differential $dz$?

Now suppose you take a step that goes **a little bit East ($dx$)** and **a little bit North ($dy$)**.

How much did your altitude change ($dz$)?
$$
dz = \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) dx}_{\text{Altitude gained going East}} + \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) dy}_{\text{Altitude gained going North}}
$$
It's that intuitive! Total height change = (East slope $\times$ East step) + (North slope $\times$ North step).

---

### Step 3: Now... Why does it say "CONTINUOUS"?

Think about what a real mountain looks like versus a broken fantasy landscape:

#### 1. If slopes are "Continuous" (A Smooth Mountain):
* As you walk around, the steepness changes **gradually and smoothly**.
* If walking East is flat, and walking North is flat, then walking **North-East is guaranteed to be flat too**.
* You can lay a flat piece of cardboard on the ground, and it will rest nicely on the surface (this is called a *tangent plane*).
* The formula $dz = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy$ works perfectly in every direction.

#### 2. If slopes are "NOT Continuous" (A Torn / Cracked Mountain):
* Imagine a bizarre landscape where if you look strictly East, it's flat. If you look strictly North, it's flat.
* But if you try to step **diagonally North-East, the ground has a sudden, vertical tear or crack that drops 100 feet down!**
* If the slopes are not continuous, you cannot predict what happens when you move in both $x$ and $y$ at the same time. The formula completely fails.

> **In plain words:**  
> Saying *"the first partial derivatives are continuous"* is just the mathematician's strict way of saying:  
> **"The mountain is smooth with no cracks, sharp tears, or sudden drop-offs."**


## Differential of a function of 2 variable

If $z=f(x,y)$ is a function of 2 variable with continuous first partial derivatives in a region $R$ of the xy-plane, then the **differential** is defined to be

$$
dz= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx+ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy
$$

When $f(x,y)=c$ for some constant $c$, then $dz=0$. Thus,
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx+ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy=0
$$

In other word, we can always generate a first-order differential equation given a one-parameter family of solution $f(x,y)=c$.

> [!definition]
> A differential expression $M(x,y)dx + N(x,y)dy$ is an **exact differential** in a region $R$ of the $xy$-plane if it corresponds to the differential of some function $f(x,y)$ defined in $R$:
> $$
> df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy = M(x,y)dx + N(x,y)dy
> $$
> A first-order differential equation of the form
> $$
> M(x,y)dx + N(x,y)dy = 0
> $$
> is said to be an **exact equation** if the expression on the left-hand side is an exact differential.

For example, $x^2 y^3 dx + x^3 y^2 dy = 0$ is an exact equation because:
$$
d\left(\frac{1}{3}x^3 y^3\right) = x^2 y^3 dx + x^3 y^2 dy
$$
How do we know whether an equation is exact without guessing $f(x, y)$ in advance?

---

## Theorem 2.4.1 Criterion for an Exact Differential

> [!Theorem] Theorem 2.4.1 (Exactness Test)
> [^1]Let $M(x, y)$ and $N(x, y)$ be continuous and have continuous first partial derivatives in a rectangular region $R$ defined by $a < x < b$, $c < y < d$. Then a necessary and sufficient condition that $M(x, y)dx + N(x, y)dy$ be an exact differential is:
> $$
> \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}
> $$

---

### Part 1: Why Does the Cross-Derivative Test Work? (Clairaut's Theorem)

If $M\,dx + N\,dy$ is an exact differential of some function $f(x, y)$, then by definition:
$$
M(x, y) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \quad \text{and} \quad N(x, y) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
$$

Now, take the cross-derivatives:
* Differentiate $M$ with respect to the **other** variable, $y$:
  $$
  \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}
  $$
* Differentiate $N$ with respect to the **other** variable, $x$:
  $$
  \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}
  $$

#### Why Can We Swap the Order of Differentiation? (Clairaut's Theorem)
For any smooth function with continuous partial derivatives, **the order of partial differentiation does not matter**:
$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}
$$

**1. Quick algebraic example with $H(x, y) = x^3 y^2$:**
* Route A ($x$ then $y$): $\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = 3x^2 y^2 \implies \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(3x^2 y^2) = \mathbf{6x^2 y}$
* Route B ($y$ then $x$): $\frac{\partial H}{\partial y} = 2x^3 y \implies \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2x^3 y) = \mathbf{6x^2 y}$
* Both routes give $6x^2 y$ because exponent multiplication is commutative ($3 \times 2 = 2 \times 3$).

**2. Walking on a square intuition:**
Walking 1 step East then 1 step North lands on the **exact same spot** as walking 1 step North then 1 step East:
```
       (x, y + dy) ---------> (x + dx, y + dy) [Same Final Spot!]
            ^                         ^
            |                         |
          North                     North
            |                         |
          (x, y)   ---------> (x + dx, y)
                     East
```
Therefore, on any smooth mountain, the slopes across the square must match:
$$
\mathbf{\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}}
$$

---

## Method of Solution: Partial Integration

Think of solving an exact equation as satisfying **two clues**:
* **Clue 1:** $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = M(x, y)$
* **Clue 2:** $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = N(x, y)$

### The 3-Step Procedure:

* **Step 1: Partial integrate with respect to $x$**
  $$f(x, y) = \int M(x, y)\,dx + g(y)$$
  *(Creates the unknown function $g(y)$)*

$$\Downarrow$$

* **Step 2: Differentiate with respect to $y$ & match Clue 2 ($N$)**
  $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[\int M(x, y)\,dx\right] + g'(y) = N(x, y)$$
  $$\implies g'(y) = N(x, y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[\int M(x, y)\,dx\right]$$
  $$\Downarrow$$

* **Step 3: Integrate with respect to $y$**
  $$g(y) = \int g'(y)\,dy$$
  *(Yields the final implicit solution $f(x, y) = c$)*

#### Why $+ g(y)$ instead of $+ C$ in Step 1?
In ordinary 1D calculus, the constant of integration is a fixed number $C$.  
In partial integration with respect to $x$, we treated $y$ as a constant. Any expression containing **only $y$** (like $y^3, \sin y, e^y$) has a derivative of zero with respect to $x$:
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[g(y)] = 0
$$
Therefore, the "constant" of integration is an unknown **function of $y$**, denoted **$g(y)$**.

#### Why differentiate with respect to $y$ in Step 2?
We used Clue 1 ($M$) in Step 1. To use **Clue 2** ($N$), we must find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ from our candidate formula and set it equal to $N(x, y)$. This isolates $g'(y)$.

#### Why integrate with respect to $y$ in Step 3?
$g'(y)$ represents $\frac{dg}{dy}$. To undo this derivative and recover $g(y)$, we must integrate with respect to $y$:
$$
g(y) = \int g'(y)\,dy
$$

---

## Why Does the Method Always Work? (Proof of Sufficiency)

> [!QUESTION] The Big Concern
> What if $g'(y) = N(x, y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int M(x, y)\,dx$ has leftover $x$'s in it?  
> Since $g(y)$ must depend **only on $y$**, any surviving $x$ would break the integration and ruin the method!

### The Proof: Showing All $x$'s Vanish
To prove that an expression has **no $x$ in it**, we take its derivative with respect to $x$. If the derivative is $0$, it is completely independent of $x$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[ N(x, y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \int M(x, y)\,dx \right] = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \int M(x, y)\,dx\right]
$$

Now **swap the order of derivatives** using Clairaut's Theorem:
$$
= \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[ \mathbf{\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \int M(x, y)\,dx} \right]
$$

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int M\,dx = M(x, y)$:
$$
= \mathbf{\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}}
$$

**The Punchline:**  
Because the equation is exact, $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$, which means:
$$
\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 0!
$$
The derivative with respect to $x$ is **identically zero**.  
**Every single $x$ is 100% mathematically guaranteed to cancel out**, leaving a clean function of $y$ alone!

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Solving an Exact Equation
Solve:
$$
\underbrace{x^2 y^3}_{M}\,dx + \underbrace{x^3 y^2}_{N}\,dy = 0
$$

**Step 1: Test for Exactness**
* $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 y^3) = 3x^2 y^2$
* $\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^3 y^2) = 3x^2 y^2$
* $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} \implies$ **Exact!**

**Step 2: Partial Integrate $M$ with respect to $x$**
$$
f(x, y) = \int x^2 y^3\,dx = y^3 \int x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{3}x^3 y^3 + g(y)
$$

**Step 3: Differentiate with respect to $y$ and set equal to $N$**
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{1}{3}x^3 y^3 + g(y)\right) = x^3 y^2 + g'(y)
$$
Set equal to $N(x, y) = x^3 y^2$:
$$
x^3 y^2 + g'(y) = x^3 y^2 \implies g'(y) = 0
$$

**Step 4: Integrate $g'(y)$ with respect to $y$**
$$
g(y) = \int 0\,dy = C_1
$$

**Final Implicit Solution:**
$$
\frac{1}{3}x^3 y^3 = c
$$

---

### Example 2: What Happens When an Equation is NOT Exact?
Consider:
$$
\underbrace{y}_{M}\,dx + \underbrace{x^2}_{N}\,dy = 0
$$
1. **Check exactness:**  
   $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 1$, but $\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = 2x$.  
   Since $1 \neq 2x$, the equation is **not exact**.
2. **Try the method anyway:**  
   $$f(x, y) = \int y\,dx = xy + g(y)$$
3. **Differentiate with respect to $y$:**  
   $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x + g'(y) = N = x^2 \implies g'(y) = x^2 - x$$
4. **Catastrophe!** $g'(y)$ contains $x$. Since $g$ must be a function of $y$ only, we cannot find $g(y)$! The method breaks down exactly as predicted.

[^1]: This sentence is the condition for the $M(x,y)$ and $N(x,y)$ to be exact differential, $M$ and $N$ must be continuous for them to be a differential $(dz)$ and they must have continuous first partial derivative so we can check $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}= \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$

## Integrating Factors

We multiply an integrating factor $\mu(x,y)$ to make it a exact equation.
$$
\begin{align}
\mu(x,y)M(x,y)dx+\mu(x,y)N(x,y)dy&=0 \\

\end{align}
$$

Thus by definition of Exact Equation, it follow that 
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\mu M \text{ and } \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=\mu N
$$

By Criterion of an Exact Equation, it follow that

$$
(\mu M)_{y}=(\mu N)_{x}
$$
where the subscript is the denote the partial derivatives. By  the Product rule, it follow that

$$
\begin{align}
\mu_{y}M+uM_{y}&=\mu_{x}N+\mu N_{x} \\
\mu_{y}M-\mu_{x}N&= (N_{x}-M_{y})\mu
\end{align}
$$
Finding $\mu(x,y)$ is not in the scope of this course (solving PDE). Thus, we assume $\mu$ is depend solely of one variable $x$ (or $y$). Thus, $\mu_{y}=0$ and $\mu_{x}= \frac{d\mu}{dx}$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d\mu}{dx}= \frac{M_{y}-N_{x}}{N}\mu
\end{align}
$$

Since LHS is depend on variable $x$, then RHS must also the same. Thus, let $P(x)= \frac{M_{y}-N_{x}}{N}\mu$.

Now it is separable function. Thus, it follow that 

$$
\mu(x)=e^{\int \frac{M_{y}-N_{x}}{N} \, dx}
$$

If it is depend on variable $y$. Then 

$$
\mu(x)=e^{\int \frac{N_{x}-M_{y}}{M} \, dy}
$$

## Example

Consider the non linear first-order differential equation
$$
\begin{align}
(xy) dx+(2x^{2}+3y^{2}-20)dy&=0 \\
\end{align}
$$

Check if it is exact equation:
Let $M(x,y)=xy$ and $N(x,y)=2x^{2}+3y^{2}-20$. Thus,

$$
\begin{align}
M_{y}=\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}= x
\end{align}
$$
and
$$
N_{x}= \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}= 4x
$$

Notice that $M_{y}\neq N_{x}$. Thus, it is not exact equation. Thus, we need to find a integrating factor $\mu$, but we don't know if it depend solely on $x$ or $y$.
Lets try assume it is depend on $x$:

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{M_{y}-N_{x}}{N}= \frac{x-4x}{2x^{2}+3y^{2}-20}= -\frac{3x}{2x^{2}+3y^{2}-20}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that it is depend on $x$ and $y$. 

Lets try assume it is depend on $y$:

$$
\frac{N_{x}-M_{y}}{M}= \frac{4x-x}{xy}= \frac{3}{y}
$$

It work. So the integrating factor will be

$$
\mu(y)=e^{\int 3/y \, dy}=e^{3\ln |y|+c}=c_{1}|y|^{3}=y^{3}
$$

> [!remark]
> Why? because if $y^{3}$ work, then $-y^{3}$ also work. Any constant multiple $cu$ is still an integrating factor. Thus, we choose $\mu(y)=y^{3}$

After multiplying DE by $\mu(y)=y^{3}$, the resulting equation is

$$
xy^{4}dx+(2x^{2}y^{3}+3y^{5}-20y^{3})dy=0
$$
Now $M_{1}(x,y)=xy^{4}$ and $N_{1}(x,y)=2x^{2}y^{3}+3y^{5}-20y^{3}$. Thus, now it is a exact equation and by definition, there exist a function $f(x,y)$ defined in $R$ such that

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=xy^{4} \text{ and } \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=2x^{2}y^{3}+3y^{5}-20y^{3}
\end{align}
$$

Now, 

$$
\begin{align}
f(x,y)&= \int xy^{4} \, dx+g(y)&&(1) \\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}&= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[ \frac{x^{2}y^{4}}{2}\right]+g'(y) \\
g'(y)&= 2x^{2}y^{3}+3y^{5}-20y^{3}- 2x^{2}y^{3}&&(2) \\
&=3y^{5}-20y^{3} \\
g(y)&=\int 3y^{5}-20y^{3} \, dy \\
&=\frac{y^{6}}{2}-5y^{4}+C_{1}
\end{align}
$$

(2) $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=N(x,y)=2x^{2}+3y^{2}-20$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[ \frac{x^{2}y^{4}}{2} \right]=2x^{2}y^{3}$


Thus, by substituting $g(y)$ into $f(x,y)$ (1), we get
$$
f(x,y)= \frac{x^{2}y^{4}}{2}+ \frac{y^{6}}{2}-5y^{4}+C_{1}
$$

Set $f(x,y)=c$. Thus,

$$
\frac{x^{2}y^{4}}{2}+ \frac{y^{6}}{2}-5y^{4}=c
$$
where $c=-C_{1}$ is the solution for the $DE$.
