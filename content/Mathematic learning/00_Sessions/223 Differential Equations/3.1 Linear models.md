# Growth and Decay

Recall that for exponential function, its derivative is proportional to its function value. And a lot of natural phenomenon can be model using the differential equation below:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dx}{dt}=kx, x(t_{0})=x_{0} \qquad \tag*{(1)}
\end{align}
$$
where $k$ is a constant of proportionality.

For example, growth and decay. The rate of growth of certain populations (bacteria, small animals) over short periods of time proportional to the population present at time $t$. 

We can use the solution of (1) to predict the population in the future- that is, at times $t>t_{0}$. The constant $k$ can be determined from the solution of the IVP.

Example 1 Bacterial Growth

A culture initially has $P_0$ bacteria. At $t = 1 \operatorname{h}$, the population is $\frac{3}{2}P_0$. If the rate of growth is proportional to the number of bacteria $P(t)$ present at time t, determine the time necessary for the number of bacteria to triple.

Solving $\frac{dP}{dt} = kP$ gives the general solution:
$$
P(t) = c e^{kt}
$$

### Two Unknowns to Determine
Notice that this solution contains **two unknown constants**:
1. $c$ (constant of integration)
2. $k$ (proportionality constant)

To determine both, we need **two conditions**:

1. **Use initial condition ($t = 0$):** $P(0) = P_0$
   $$
   P(0) = c e^0 = c \implies c = P_0
   $$
   Now the equation is:
   $$
   P(t) = P_0 e^{kt}
   $$

2. **Use second condition ($t = 1$):** $P(1) = \frac{3}{2}P_0$
   $$
   \frac{3}{2}P_0 = P_0 e^{k(1)} \implies \frac{3}{2} = e^k \implies k = \ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) \approx 0.4055
   $$

> **Why substitute $t = 0$ first?**  
> Setting $c = P_0$ first allows $P_0$ to cancel out on both sides when you plug in $t = 1$, isolating $k$ directly.

## Determine the time
To find the time at which the number of bacteria has tripled, we solved $3P_{0}=P_{0}e^{\ln\left( \frac{3}{2} \right)t}$:

$$
\begin{align}
3&=e^{\ln\left( \frac{3}{2} \right)t} \\
\ln(3)&=\ln\left( \frac{3}{2} \right)t \\
t& \approx 2.71 \operatorname{h}
\end{align}
$$


> [!remark]
> The actual number $P_{0}$ of bacteria present at time $t=0$ played no part in determining the time required for the number in the culture to triple. In other word it is an autonomous DE
> 
> If $k>0$, then it is a growth constant, if $k<0$, it is a decay constant (Refer to the graph of $e^{k}$), when $k>0$, $e^{k}>0$ and vice versa

# Half life

In physics the half-life is a measure of the stability of a radioactive substance. The half-life is simply the time it takes for one-half of the atoms in an initial amount $A_{0}$ to disintegrate, or transmute, into the atoms of another element.

### Example 2
A breeder reactor converts relatively stable uranium-238 into the isotope plutonium-239. After 15 years it is determined that 0.043% of the initial amount $A _ { 0 }$ of plutonium has disintegrated. Find the half-life of this isotope if the rate of disintegration is proportional to the amount remaining.

Let $A(t)$ denote the amount of plutonium remaining at time $t$. As in example 1 the solution of the IVP
$$
\frac{dA}{dt}=kt.A(0)=A_{0}
$$

is $A(t)=A_{0}e^{kt}$. From the question we know that $0.043$% of the atom of $A_{0}$ has disintegrated implies that $99.957$% remains.

Thus, $A(15)=0.99957A_{0}$. Thus, by substitution

$$
\begin{align}
A(15)&= A_{0}e^{15k} \\
0.99957 A_{0}&=A_{0}e^{15k} \\
0.99957&=e^{15k} \\
15k&=\ln(0.99957) \\
k&=-0.00002867
\end{align}
$$

Hence, $A(t)=A_{0}e^{-0.00002867t}$. Solve for half-life:

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{2}A_{0}&=A_{0}e^{-0.00002867t} \\
\frac{1}{2}&=e^{-0.00002867t} \\
\ln\left( \frac{1}{2} \right) &= -0.00002867t \\
t&= 24.18 \operatorname{yr}
\end{align}
$$

### Example 3

### 1. The Physical Mechanism (Why it Works)

1. **In the Atmosphere:**  
   Cosmic radiation strikes Nitrogen-14 in the upper atmosphere, continuously creating the radioactive isotope **Carbon-14 ($\text{}^{14}\text{C}$)**. This mixes with ordinary, stable **Carbon-12 ($\text{}^{12}\text{C}$)**. The ratio $\text{}^{14}\text{C} / \text{}^{12}\text{C}$ in the air is constant.

2. **While an Organism is Alive:**  
   Plants absorb carbon via photosynthesis; animals eat plants or other animals. Because of this continuous exchange, **the ratio of $\text{}^{14}\text{C}$ in living tissue exactly equals the atmospheric ratio**.

3. **At the Moment of Death ($t = 0$):**  
   Intake stops completely (no breathing, eating, or photosynthesis).  
   * The stable $\text{}^{12}\text{C}$ stays forever.
   * The trapped $\text{}^{14}\text{C}$ continuously decays radioactively back into Nitrogen.

By measuring the remaining fraction of $\text{}^{14}\text{C}$ compared to the standard atmospheric ratio, we can calculate how much time $t$ has elapsed since death.

---

### 2. The Mathematical Model (First-Order ODE)

Radioactive decay is modeled by the first-order differential equation:
$$\frac{dA}{dt} = kA, \quad A(0) = A_0$$

Where:
* $A(t)$ is the amount of $\text{}^{14}\text{C}$ remaining at time $t$.
* $A_0$ is the initial amount present at death ($t = 0$).
* $k < 0$ is the decay constant.

Solving this separable/linear equation yields:
$$A(t) = A_0 e^{kt}$$

---

### 3. Finding the Decay Constant $k$ via Half-Life

The textbook uses the standard **Cambridge half-life of $\text{}^{14}\text{C}$**:
$$\text{Half-life } T_{1/2} \approx 5730 \text{ years}$$

By definition of half-life, at $t = 5730$, exactly half the substance remains:
$$A(5730) = \frac{1}{2}A_0$$

Substitute this into $A(t) = A_0 e^{kt}$:
$$\frac{1}{2}A_0 = A_0 e^{5730k}$$

Notice $A_0$ cancels out:
$$\frac{1}{2} = e^{5730k} \implies 5730k = \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\ln 2$$
$$k = -\frac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx -0.00012097 \text{ yr}^{-1}$$

So the decay formula for any Carbon-14 sample is:
$$A(t) = A_0 e^{-0.00012097 t}$$

---

### 4. Textbook Example 3: Age of a Fossil

> **Problem:** A fossilized bone is found to contain **$0.1\%$** of its original amount of $\text{}^{14}\text{C}$. Determine the age of the fossil.

1. **Set up the condition:**
   $0.1\% = 0.001$, so $A(t) = 0.001 A_0$.

2. **Equate and solve for $t$:**
   $$0.001 A_0 = A_0 e^{-0.00012097 t}$$
   $$0.001 = e^{-0.00012097 t}$$

3. **Take the natural logarithm of both sides:**
   $$\ln(0.001) = -0.00012097 t$$
   $$-\ln(1000) = -0.00012097 t$$
   $$t = \frac{\ln(1000)}{0.00012097} \approx \frac{6.907755}{0.00012097} \approx \mathbf{57{,}100 \text{ years}}$$

---

### 5. Key Limitations Noted in the Text

* **Limit of $\approx 60{,}000$ years:** After $\approx 10$ half-lives ($10 \times 5730 \approx 57{,}300$ years), less than $0.1\%$ of the $\text{}^{14}\text{C}$ remains. At that point, the radioactivity is so faint that it becomes difficult to distinguish from normal background radiation.
* **Organic Materials Only:** Carbon dating applies only to materials that were once part of the carbon cycle (wood, bones, papyrus, linen). For rocks and geological formations, geologists use other radiometric isotopes with billions of years of half-life (such as Uranium-238, half-life $\approx 4.47$ billion years).

# Newton's Law of Cooling/Warming

We know that the rate of change of temperature is proportional to the difference of temperature of the object and surrounding denoted by the linear first-order differential equation:

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=k(T-T_{m})
$$
where $k$ is a constant and $T(t)$ is the temperature of the object while $T_{m}$ is the ambient temperature.

### Example 4 Cooling of a Cake
When a cake is removed from an oven, its temperature is measured at $3 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { ~ F ~ }$ Three minutes later its temperature is $2 0 0 ^ { \circ }$ F. 

We make the identification $T_{m}=70$, we need to solve

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=k(T-70), T(0)=300
$$

Notice that it is both separable and linear, thus
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{T-70}\frac{dT}{dt}&=k\\
\ln \lvert T-70 \rvert&=kt+c \\
T-70&=c_{1}e^{kt}  \\
T&=70+c_{1}e^{kt}
\end{align}
$$

Substitute the initial condition to solve for $c_{1}$:

$$
\begin{align}
300&= 70+c_{1}e^{0} \\
230&=c_{1}
\end{align}
$$

Hence, the solution for the DE is $T(t)=70+230e^{kt}$. 

Solve for $k$:
From problem we know that $T(3)=200$, thus,

$$
\begin{align}
200&= 70+230e^{3k} \\
130&=230e^{3k} \\
e^{3k}&= \frac{13}{23} \\
k&= \frac{\ln\left( \frac{12}{23} \right)}{3} \\
&= -0.19018
\end{align}
$$

Thus, $T(t)=70+230e^{-0.19018t}$ is the solution for the IVP.

> [!remark]
> The function is easy to understand, $70$ here is the temperature of surrounding and it is the bottom boundary. It make sense because the temperature of the object will only approach to room temperature and never exceed. In this case the function is decreasing because $k<0$

# Mixtures

Imagine there is a tank with solution that contain salt and water like brine. We will put a amount of solution at constant rate for example $\frac{3gal}{min}$ into the tank, and at the same time the solution will come out from the tank at constant rate for example $\frac{3gal}{min}$. So what is the concentration in the tank when after a period of time?

When we talk about solution, we must think about concentration?

So to know the rate of change of concentration, we should know the input rate $R_{in}$ and output rate $R_{out}$ because

$$
\frac{dA}{dt}=R_{in}-R_{out}
$$

We get this DE by assuming the rate $A'(t)$ at which the amount of salt in the mixing tank change was a net rate. (One in one out).

### The Core Formula (Dimensional Analysis)
To calculate the rate of salt entering or leaving, we multiply concentration by fluid flow rate:
$$
\text{Rate of salt } \left( \frac{\text{lb}}{\text{min}} \right) = \text{Concentration } \left( \frac{\text{lb}}{\text{gal}} \right) \times \text{Flow rate } \left( \frac{\text{gal}}{\text{min}} \right)
$$
(It is like chain rule)

- **Input rate $R_{in}$:**
  $$
  R_{in} = c_{in} \cdot r_{in}
  $$
- **Output rate $R_{out}$:**
  Assuming the tank is **well-stirred** (uniform concentration throughout):
  $$
  c_{out}(t) = \frac{\text{current amount of salt in tank}}{\text{current volume of liquid in tank}} = \frac{A(t)}{V(t)}
  $$
  $$
  R_{out} = \left( \frac{A(t)}{V(t)} \right) \cdot r_{out}
  $$

---

### Example 5 Mixture of Two Salt Solutions (Constant Volume)

A large tank holds $300\text{ gal}$ of brine containing $50\text{ lb}$ of salt initially ($A(0) = 50$).
- **Inflow:** brine enters at $r_{in} = 3\text{ gal/min}$ with concentration $c_{in} = 2\text{ lb/gal}$.
- **Outflow:** well-stirred solution is pumped out at $r_{out} = 3\text{ gal/min}$.

Question: How much salt is in the tank after a long time?

#### Step 1: Volume of liquid
Since $r_{in} = r_{out} = 3\text{ gal/min}$, volume is constant:
$$
V(t) = 300\text{ gal}
$$

#### Step 2: Rates of salt
$$
\begin{align}
R_{in} &= (2\text{ lb/gal}) \times (3\text{ gal/min}) = 6\text{ lb/min} \\
R_{out} &= \left( \frac{A(t)}{300}\text{ lb/gal} \right) \times (3\text{ gal/min}) = \frac{A(t)}{100}\text{ lb/min}
\end{align}
$$

#### Step 3: Set up and solve the IVP
$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = 6 - \frac{A}{100}, \quad A(0) = 50
$$

Put in standard linear form:
$$
\frac{dA}{dt} + \frac{1}{100}A = 6
$$

The integrating factor is $e^{\int \frac{1}{100}dt} = e^{t/100}$:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}\left[ e^{t/100}A \right] &= 6e^{t/100} \\
e^{t/100}A &= 600e^{t/100} + c \\
A(t) &= 600 + ce^{-t/100}
\end{align}
$$

Apply initial condition $A(0) = 50$:
$$
50 = 600 + c \implies c = -550
$$

Thus, the amount of salt at time $t$ is:
$$
A(t) = 600 - 550e^{-t/100}
$$

> [!remark]
> **Long-term behavior ($t \to \infty$):**  
> As $t \to \infty$, $e^{-t/100} \to 0$, so $A(t) \to 600\text{ lb}$.  
> This matches our physical intuition: after a long time, the entire tank ($300\text{ gal}$) reaches the incoming concentration ($2\text{ lb/gal}$):
> $$
> 300\text{ gal} \times 2\text{ lb/gal} = 600\text{ lb}
> $$

### Example 6 Variable Volume ($r_{in} \neq r_{out}$)

What if the well-stirred solution is pumped out at a slower rate, say $r_{out} = 2\text{ gal/min}$?

#### Step 1: Volume changes with time
Liquid accumulates at the rate:
$$
r_{in} - r_{out} = 3 - 2 = 1\text{ gal/min}
$$
After $t$ minutes, the volume in the tank is:
$$
V(t) = 300 + (1)t = 300 + t\text{ gal}
$$

#### Step 2: Output rate of salt
$$
R_{out} = \left( \frac{A(t)}{300 + t} \right) \cdot 2 = \frac{2A(t)}{300 + t}\text{ lb/min}
$$

#### Step 3: Set up and solve the IVP
$$
\frac{dA}{dt} + \frac{2}{300 + t}A = 6, \quad A(0) = 50
$$

The integrating factor is:
$$
e^{\int \frac{2}{300+t}dt} = e^{2\ln(300+t)} = (300 + t)^2
$$

Multiply by $(300 + t)^2$:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}\left[ (300 + t)^2 A \right] &= 6(300 + t)^2 \\
(300 + t)^2 A &= 2(300 + t)^3 + c \\
A(t) &= 2(300 + t) + \frac{c}{(300 + t)^2}
\end{align}
$$

Apply $A(0) = 50$:
$$
50 = 2(300) + \frac{c}{300^2} \implies 50 = 600 + \frac{c}{90{,}000} \implies c = -4.95 \times 10^7
$$

Thus:
$$
A(t) = 600 + 2t - \frac{4.95 \times 10^7}{(300 + t)^2}
$$

> [!remark]
> Because fluid accumulates and salt is constantly pumped in ($6\text{ lb/min}$) while only a fraction leaves, $A(t) \to \infty$ as $t \to \infty$ (until the tank eventually overflows).
