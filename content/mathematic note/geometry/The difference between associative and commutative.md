
We learn about associative and commutative in real number. For example,
$$
5+3=3+5
$$
and 
$$
(1+2)+3=1+(2+3)
$$

We can see that the thing that we are deal with is the object (real number). The thing that swap order and group together is the object with the operation.

Let see a bijective function with composition as the binary operation. 

$$
(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ (g \circ h)
$$

Notice we are performing sequence of action to a single object and no matter how we group the operation , the result is the same. (the order doesn't change it need to go for $h\to g\to f$)

But the order is important (commutative not applicable)

If you are just doing a sequence of actions to a single object, it will ALWAYS be associative.

### The Rule Breaker: "Combining" Actions

The associative property completely falls apart when the action is a **Binary Operation**. This means the action requires taking _two separate things_ and mashing them together to create _one brand-new thing_.

When you are combining things, the parentheses dictate **who gets mashed together first**. If you change who gets mashed together first, you change the ingredients for the final step, which changes the final result.

Imagine a martial arts tournament with three fighters: an **Expert**, an **Average Fighter**, and a **Beginner**. The "action" is: **They fight, and the better fighter absorbs the loser and moves on.** (田忌赛马)
